# On Contemplation, Book Two

*De Contemplatione Secundum Sanctorum Patrum atque Doctorum Præcipuorum Sententias*

*On Contemplation according to the Testimonies of the Holy Fathers and Chief Doctors*

## Proemium

*But we all, beholding the glory of the Lord with open face, are transformed into the same image from glory to glory, as by the Spirit of the Lord.* — 2 Corinthians 3:18

The holy Apostle speaks these words in the person of contemplatives and of the perfect, setting forth in few words the progress and perfection of wayfarers in the present life. For the perfection of the created mind consists in this: that it be made like to the uncreated Mind, and this likeness is wrought by contemplation, as the Apostle teaches.

He says "with open face," that is, with the interior face of the soul — the intellect divinely illumined, freed, and instructed, and liberated from the veil of the Mosaic law and the obscurities of prophetic visions and figures. Now, however, we behold "as through a glass in a dark manner" (1 Cor. 13:12). "We are transformed into the same image," that is, we are advanced into a certain gracious, supernatural, and deiform likeness to the divine majesty and the Son of God, since, as Boethius testifies, knowledge is effected by the assimilation of the knower to the known. Hence through beatific knowledge we shall be most perfectly likened to God, as the beloved disciple declares: "We shall be like to him, because we shall see him as he is" (1 John 3:2). This transformation is a saving change by which we pass from our own proper mode of subsistence toward that form which belongs to the divine likeness. And this transformation is wrought "as by the Spirit of the Lord," that is, by the power and grace of the Holy Spirit aiding our weakness (cf. Rom. 8:26). For contemplation, as has often been said, requires a singular grace.

Since in the preceding part of this work I have written what the Lord deigned to grant concerning contemplation and its kinds and practice, it is now my purpose in this second part of the edition to set forth the testimonies of the holy Fathers and chief doctors regarding contemplation, insofar as he shall grant without whom we are not sufficient to think anything wholesome of ourselves (cf. 2 Cor. 3:5).

## Article I. On the Species of Contemplation according to the Divine Dionysius

That a worthy beginning be made, we must first speak of contemplation according to the most divine of theologians and prince of contemplatives, the sacred and most ardent lover of the Divinity, Dionysius.

In the fourth chapter of *On the Divine Names*, Dionysius sets forth three species of contemplation, which he names after kinds of local motion, affirming that they belong differently to angels than to souls still on pilgrimage.

### The Circular Motion

The first kind of contemplative operation he calls the circular motion — taking "motion" in a broad sense for the perfective operation or act of a perfect being, as the Philosopher says in the third book *On the Soul* that understanding and sensing are certain motions. This circular motion, or contemplation proceeding after the manner of circular motion, Dionysius says befits the angels insofar as, extending themselves upward, they are united to the illuminations of the highest Beauty and first Good. These illuminations are said to be without beginning and without end, insofar as in a circle no beginning or end can be found — not that these illuminations never began, since the angels themselves have had a beginning of their being, but because the angels uniformly and incessantly behold and contemplate the fountain and source of all beauty and goodness, the living God.

After this manner of the circular motion, which is around the same immovable centre, so in this intellective operation of the angels, knowledge flows from God into them, terminates immediately in him, and revolves around him alone. By this illumination they immediately return to God, the source and chief of their illumination.

Some have held that this befits only the first hierarchy, which alone is immediately illumined by God. But this is not firmly established. For all and each of the orders are immediately illumined and taught by God, not only regarding the knowledge in which essential beatitude consists, but also regarding the knowledge of certain divine effects; for they know many things in the Word.

Dionysius affirms generally that angels are moved circularly around God, saying: "The divine minds are said to be moved circularly, being united by non-beginning and non-ending illuminations of the Good and the Beautiful."

### The Straight Motion

The straight motion, or intellective operation likened to straight motion, belongs to angels insofar as the higher angels extend themselves to the providence of the lower, and the lowest angels, passing through all things in right order, tend toward those subject to them — or, as Dionysius says: "They are moved in a straight line whenever they come forth in the providence of those subject to them, ordering all things rightly." As Thomas affirms in the *Secunda Secundae* (q. 180, a. 6), this straight motion in the angels is not taken as proceeding discursively from one thing to another in their knowing — for they know by simple intuition — but only according to the order of their providence, insofar as the higher illumines, teaches, and directs the lower. This operation is likened to straight motion because, just as straight motion passes from one thing to another, so in this action the angels do not return from the same to the same but proceed from themselves toward their subjects.

### The Oblique Motion

The oblique motion, or intellective action similar to oblique movement, befits the angels insofar as, even while they provide for lower things in their contemplation of God, they are reflected back to God (the beginning and end of all good and beauty) — for, as the Abbot of Vercelli explains, they remain without departure from themselves, uniformly and unfailingly praising within themselves the Good and the Beautiful, the cause of their unchangeableness. Dionysius says: "They are moved obliquely, because even while providing for lesser beings, they remain irreversibly around the immutable and causal Good." This operation is called oblique motion because, just as oblique motion is composed of circular and straight, so this angelic action partakes of something of the uniformity of the first (insofar as it is around God) and something of the procession to others of the second.

### The Three Motions in the Rational Soul

Dionysius next applies these three motions to the rational soul. The soul is moved circularly when it withdraws from exterior things and gathers itself inward, when it unifies its powers and collects them from their dispersion, when it raises itself by desire, and so fixes itself on God in a simple, unified, and quiet attention. This is the highest operation of the rational soul, which, insofar as it withdraws from the senses and imagination and ascends purely to God, is likened to the circular motion of the angels.

The soul is moved in a straight line, according to Dionysius, when it goes forth to exterior things and does not reflect back into itself — for that would be to move circularly — but from diverse and divided divine symbols is reduced and led up to simple, divine, and unified contemplations.

The soul is moved obliquely when it is not led to divine illuminations by a purely intellectual, simple, and serene extension, but after human fashion, by a rational, discursive, and symbolic investigation of creatures, is guided to the contemplation of the first and superessential Truth, Goodness, and Beauty. For this is what the Theologian says: "The soul is moved obliquely when it is illumined by divine sciences that are proper to itself, not intellectually and purely, but rationally and investigatively." Dionysius here takes intellect and reason strictly and properly, as distinguished from each other.

## Article II. On Contemplation according to Origen

Origen, that most learned of the ancients who devoted himself to sacred letters, distinguishes five degrees of contemplation by which the soul ascends to God.

The first degree is the affective loosening or detachment by which the soul, drawing itself back from its attachments and entanglements, is made free for the consideration of higher things. Without this liberation from disordered affections, no authentic ascent to God can begin.

The second degree is the continuous conjunction of the intellect to elevating objects — that is, the mind's persistent and assiduous attachment to things that lift it upward, meditating on the truths of faith and the works of God, so that the soul grows accustomed to dwelling in a higher atmosphere.

The third degree is the proportionate commensuration of intellect and affection, by which the knowing and the loving powers of the soul are brought into due proportion with each other and with the divine object contemplated. When intellect and will are rightly ordered and balanced in their elevation toward God, the soul is disposed for a more intimate reception of divine light.

The fourth degree is the conjunction to that which exceeds the ordinary measure of graces and virtues, reaching even to the order of the celestial spirits — when the soul, elevated beyond its natural mode, enters into a participation of the angelic life in its contemplation of God.

The fifth and highest degree is the uniform fixation of the recollected spirit in the highest Good, by which the soul is no longer scattered or variable but cleaves to God in a stable, quiet, and luminous repose. In this fixation the soul is gathered into unity within itself and within God, and all dispersal ceases.

## Article III. On Contemplation according to Augustine

Augustine, the most illustrious Doctor of the Church, contributes to the understanding of contemplation from several angles, of which the most notable are his distinction of three kinds of knowledge and his account of three kinds of vision.

### The Triple Knowledge

Regarding knowledge, Augustine distinguishes matutinal, meridional, and vespertinal knowledge. Matutinal knowledge is the knowledge of things as they exist in the Word of God — that is, the knowledge of creatures as they are in their eternal exemplars and ideas in the divine intellect, a knowledge clearer and more perfect than any other creaturely knowledge. Meridional knowledge is the beatific vision of God himself, face to face, which is the knowledge of noon when the light is highest and no shadow remains — this is the knowledge of the blessed in the homeland. Vespertinal knowledge is the knowledge of things as they exist in their own proper nature, which is a dimmer and lower form of knowing, since creatures known in themselves are known in an inferior mode compared with their eternal exemplars.

### Superior and Inferior Reason

Augustine also distinguishes in the rational soul the superior reason, which is directed to eternal things and to the contemplation of divine truth, and the inferior reason, which is turned toward temporal and changeable things, ordering them in the service of the higher life. Contemplation properly pertains to the superior reason in its elevation toward God.

### The Triple Vision

Augustine further distinguishes three kinds of vision: corporeal vision, by which we see with the bodily eyes things that are present to the senses; imaginary or imaginative vision, by which we see images and likenesses of things even when those things are absent from the bodily senses; and intellectual vision, by which the mind sees truth itself and the intelligible realities that have no corporeal form and can receive no image. The highest contemplation belongs to intellectual vision, and in its summit it reaches toward the vision of God himself, insofar as this is granted to a soul in the present state.

## Article IV. On Contemplation according to Bernard

Bernard of Clairvaux, that most ardent doctor and devout lover of God, has written abundantly on contemplation in his sermons, letters, and treatises, from which we draw what is most pertinent.

### The Four Species of Contemplation

Bernard assigns four species of contemplation: admiration of God's majesty (*admiratio majestatis*), contemplation of his judgments (*intuitio judiciorum*), consideration of his benefits (*consideratio beneficiorum*), and inspection of his promises (*inspectio promissorum*). Each of these is a distinct mode of the soul's elevated gaze upon the divine reality: the first corresponds to the second species treated in Book One, concerning the incomprehensible divine majesty; the second to the mysterious and inscrutable ways of divine providence; the third to the benefits of the Incarnation and Passion and the grace poured into souls; and the fourth to the glory promised to the blessed.

### The Ladder of the Cloister

Bernard sets forth the famous *scala claustralium* — the ladder of those in the cloister — by which the soul ascends to God: *lectio* (reading), *meditatio* (meditation), *oratio* (prayer), and *contemplatio* (contemplation). By diligent reading the mind receives the material of divine truth; by meditation it chews and digests what has been received; by prayer it lifts the purified and enkindled heart to God; and by contemplation it is raised above itself into the light of the divine presence. This ladder is not always ascended step by step in visible succession, for the grace of contemplation is freely given; but it describes the ordinary preparation and proximate disposition of the contemplative soul.

### Seven Requirements

For fruitful contemplation Bernard requires seven things: humility, without which no spiritual building stands; compassion for one's neighbor, which dilates the heart; affliction willingly borne, which purifies the soul from self-love; compunction, which turns the heart from sin toward God; earnest prayer, which unites the soul to God; leisure of mind from external occupation; and a certain interior simplicity and freedom from turbulent imaginings.

### The Garden of Contemplation

Contemplation requires a fitting interior garden in the soul — the garden of the enclosed Spouse — and this garden is tended by the practice of virtue, watered by grace, and sheltered from the distractions of the world. In this garden the Spouse makes himself present in visits of illumination and consolation that exceed all human preparation or merit.

### The Epistle to the Brothers of Mont-Dieu

In the letter to the Brothers of Mont-Dieu, Bernard treats at length the progress of the soul toward God in the contemplative life. Among his teachings there: the soul has a certain natural likeness to God that none loses; there is a closer likeness by which the soul strives to imitate the Creator's greatness of virtue and steadfastness; and beyond this is a likeness so properly the soul's own that it is called not likeness but unity of spirit (*unitas spiritus*), when a person becomes one with God — not only by identity of will, but by a certain unity of virtue beyond all willing otherwise. This unity of spirit is called such not only because the Holy Spirit effects it, or because he joins the human spirit to himself, but because this unity itself is the Holy Spirit, who is the Love, the Unity, the Sweetness, the Kiss, and the Embrace of the Father and the Son.

In this unity the blessed conscience finds itself somehow in the middle of the embrace and kiss of Father and Son, and in an ineffable and inconceivable manner is made the *homo Dei*, not God, but what God is by nature the person becomes by grace. The Holy Spirit is the all-powerful artificer who creates in the human being a good will toward God, who makes God propitious toward the human being, who forms affection, gives virtue, prospers action, orders all things mightily and disposes all things sweetly (cf. Wisd. 8:1), and who himself teaches how to find, to hold, and to enjoy.

### The Withdrawal and Return of the Spouse

Bernard also teaches that even from the perfect the heavenly Spouse long desired and sought, withdraws quickly and eludes — withdrawing from the sublime vision, from contemplation's sweetness, from sensible union. This is not to be grieved over inordinately: he withdraws for their protection, lest they be lifted up by the greatness of consolation, lest constant presence lead them to despise companions, and lest they attribute continuous visitation to nature rather than grace. For common proverb says: *Nimia familiaritas parit contemptum* — too great familiarity breeds contempt. He withdraws, therefore, lest the too-frequent be despised; that being absent he may be more desired; that being desired he may be more ardently sought; that long sought he may at last be found more gratefully. He permits us to taste a little how sweet he is, and before we fully experience this, he withdraws himself, and so, as it were spreading his wings over us (cf. Deut. 32:11), he provokes us to fly — as if saying: *You have tasted a little how gracious and sweet I am; if you wish to be fully satisfied with this sweetness, hasten after me in the fragrance of my ointments, lifting your hearts upward where I am at the right hand of God the Father. There you will see me, not through a glass in a dark manner, but face to face; and your heart shall fully rejoice, and no one shall take your joy from you* (cf. John 16:22; 1 Cor. 13:12; Col. 3:1).

### On the Inscrutable Judgments of God

Since the second species of contemplation, following Bernard, concerns the judgments of God, something may be said of these most wonderful and inscrutable judgments. Augustine, in the twentieth book of *The City of God*, chapter two, treats this beautifully: we learn to bear with equanimity the evils that good people suffer, and not to prize the goods that evil people attain; and thus even in those matters where divine justice does not appear, divine teaching is salutary. For we do not know by what judgment of God this good person is poor and that wicked one rich; why the one who ought to be tormented for his dissolute life rejoices, while the one whose laudable life should earn gladness is saddened; why the innocent is crushed in judgment and the wicked justified. All these things, says Augustine, are inscrutable now; but when the day of the final judgment comes, not only what is then judged, but all that has been judged from the beginning and that remains to be judged until that day, shall appear most just. Then also it will be made manifest by what just judgment of God it is that so many — nearly all — of the just judgments of God are now hidden from the senses and minds of mortals; though the faith of the devout is not ignorant that what is hidden is just. — So the most illumined Augustine.

## Article V. On Contemplation according to Hugo of Saint Victor

Hugo of Saint Victor, whom Richard affirms to have been the foremost theologian of his time, speaking of the kinds of spiritual speculation in his commentary on Ecclesiastes, distinguishes three interior visions or considerations of the soul: *cogitatio* (thinking), *meditatio* (meditation), and *contemplatio* (contemplation).

Thinking (*cogitatio*) is when the mind is transiently touched by the notion of things — when a thing is suddenly presented to the soul by its image, entering either through the sense or revived by memory. It is the improvidential regard of the mind, prone to wandering.

Meditation (*meditatio*) is the studious intention of the mind, diligently persisting in the investigation of something; or thus: meditation is the provident gaze of the soul, vehemently occupied in the inquiry after truth. Bernard takes it similarly, saying: meditation is the studious action of the mind, investigating the knowledge of hidden truth under the guidance of one's own reason. Thomas, in the same vein, shows that meditation terminates where contemplation begins.

Contemplation (*contemplatio*) is that liveliness of understanding which, holding all things in plain view, comprehends them by manifest vision. Thus in a sense what meditation seeks, contemplation possesses.

Hugo's disciple Richard of Saint Victor conforms himself to these words: *cogitatio* is the heedless regard of the mind, prone to wandering; *meditatio* is the provident gaze of the soul, vehemently occupied in the pursuit of truth; and that faculty of the soul which inquires into the truth of judgment is called *ratio* (reason), while insofar as it senses the truth of judgment, it receives the name *intellectus* (intellect).

### The Five Digressions of Thought

There is also attributed to Aristotle a small work whose title is *On the Five Digressions of Thought*, of which this is the entire text: The five digressions of thought are: the first is in sense, the second in imagination, the third in reason, the fourth in intellect, the fifth in intelligence. Through sense it is in these things and according to these things; through imagination it is not in these things but according to these things; through reason it is not in these things nor according to these things, but in those things and concerning those things which are according to these things; through intellect it is not in those things, but concerning those things; through intelligence it is not in those things nor concerning those things.

The commentator on this text — who was beyond doubt a Christian — explains: the third digression of thought, existing in reason, "is not in sensibles," as sense is, "nor according to these things," as imagination is, "but in those things and concerning those things which are according to these things" — that is, in the intrinsic and substantial forms of sensible things, which are discerned by the light of the interior eye. Thus reason transcends the limits of sense and imagination, yet knows what it sees by reference to the concretion of the sensible things in which those forms exist.

The fourth digression is in intellect, whose proper work is to abstract from all subsisting things the forms sought by reason, and to behold by the purer ray of the mind what each thing is by its property of acting or existing, or from what genus it comes, or what its condition is. For this reason the fourth digression is "concerning" those things — not with the propriety of concretion, but by abstraction.

Finally, the fifth digression is in *intelligentia* (intelligence), which is "not in those things nor concerning those things" — but concerning God, one and triune, concerning that being which surpasses all things by incomparable excellence, which shares neither genus nor species with created things. Whoever undertakes the journey of consideration toward the contemplation of God must, leaving behind all secondary causes, rely on faith rather than arguments; since the Triune God is above the investigation of nature, inaccessible to sense, not to be formed by imagination, not penetrable by reason, nor comprehensible by intellect. For what is above all things is without form, is simple, is unique, is impregnable, is immense and eternal and enclosed by no limit — before which not only weakness but even silence will the word profess; for whatever a multitude of words may designate, it is the plurality of our profession, praise, and consideration alone, and not of the signified — which is God, the supremely simple — designated under diverse names by the human mind that cannot comprehend him in the purity of his simplicity. For when Trinity or Creator or *Nous* is named, a signification of unity underlies the multiple profession.

If indeed it was Aristotle who composed the text cited above, he must be thought to have intended by the fifth digression — in intelligence — the contemplation not of the blessed Trinity, but of the divine essence. But the author of the commentary, from whom these things are briefly collected, was without doubt a Christian; and he says at the end of his commentary: the more the soul is upheld by the faith of immutable truth, the more ardently and alertly is it kindled in the contemplation of the supercelestial and inscrutable Cause of all things.

## Article VI. On Contemplation and the Ascent of the Soul through the Degrees of Intelligence

Denis here draws together the foregoing distinctions to show how the soul's contemplation rises through successive degrees from the lowest to the highest. The soul that proceeds by sense knows God only indirectly through the report of the external world. The soul that rises to imagination grasps figures and symbols, which serve the higher way. The soul that ascends to reason contemplates the intrinsic natures of things and the principles of divine truth accessible to discursive thought. The soul that rises further to intellect (*intellectus*) grasps intelligible forms abstracted from matter and apprehends by a certain clear, non-discursive light, as in the highest reaches of philosophical and theological science. And the soul that attains to intelligence (*intelligentia*) — the apex of the soul — transcends all creaturely modes and all compositeness, and in this simplest and most exalted act is in contact with the divine simplicity itself.

The movement through these degrees is not primarily an achievement of human industry but a gift of grace: for as each step upward exceeds the proportion of unaided nature, the soul depends more and more upon the light that God himself pours in. At the summit of intelligence the soul is, in a certain manner, beyond itself — neither in sensible things, nor in their images, nor in their rational natures, nor even in abstractions, but in a simple, luminous stillness before the face of the incomprehensible God. This is the threshold of mystical theology, where affirmation fails and silence is the fittest praise.

## Article VII. On Contemplation according to the Holy Doctor [Thomas Aquinas]

Denis writes that he had not intended to form here a special article on contemplation according to Blessed Thomas — the distinguished philosopher and profound and excellent theologian — since what Thomas treats in the *Secunda Secundae* on contemplation has already for the most part been cited and recalled in Book One. Nevertheless, for completeness he records the structure of Thomas's treatment.

Thomas first inquires whether life is suitably and sufficiently divided into the contemplative and the active, a question already treated in Book One, article eleven.

He then raises eight questions concerning the contemplative life. The first is whether the contemplative life pertains only to the intellect, or also consists in affection — treated in article seven of Book One. The second is whether the moral virtues pertain to the contemplative life — resolved in article twenty of Book One. The third is whether the contemplative life consists in one act alone — discussed in article twelve of Book One. The fourth is whether the contemplation of any truth whatsoever belongs to the contemplative life — answered in article three of Book One. The fifth is whether the contemplative life in the present state can be elevated to the vision of God in his essence — not yet treated in Book One, but to be addressed by God's help later. The sixth concerns the motions of contemplation according to Dionysius — recalled in article one of Book Two above. The seventh concerns the delight of contemplation — discussed in article thirteen of Book One. The eighth concerns the duration of the contemplative life — resolved in article fifteen of Book One, and to be treated more fully later.

Thomas then raises four questions comparing the active and contemplative lives. The first is which is the more excellent — addressed in article fourteen of Book One. The second is which is the more meritorious — also treated there. The third is whether the contemplative life is impeded by the active — addressed elsewhere in Book One. The fourth is which ought to precede the other — sufficiently discussed in article twenty of Book One.

Finally, what Thomas says concerning rapture or ecstasy as it concerns contemplation will find its place below, God granting.

## Article VIII. On Contemplation and its Species according to the Devout Doctor [Bonaventure]

Among the most elevated contemplatives of divine things and the most experienced tasters of divine sweetness, the lord Bonaventure — the sweetest and most devout doctor — holds no small place, and seems to have written of contemplation more abundantly than most. From his overflowing works only a very few things are here selected, since his writings are, as is fitting, everywhere circulated and can easily be obtained.

### The Seven Grades from the Itinerarium

In the book called *The Journey of the Mind to God* (*Itinerarius mentis in Deum*), Bonaventure assigns grades or species of contemplation by which the soul ascends, as by successive steps, to the speculation of the eternal Divinity and the most blessed Trinity. The first grade is the contemplation of God through his vestiges in the universe; the second is the contemplation of God through his vestiges in this sensible world — both of which were already discussed in Book One, in those articles concerning the fifth species of contemplation (articles sixty-eight and sixty-nine). The third is the contemplation of God through his image naturally impressed in the soul, namely the rational soul — of which also something was said in Book One, articles seventy and seventy-one. The fourth is the contemplation of God through his image reformed by gratuitous gifts — treated in Book One in those articles concerning the fourth species of contemplation. The fifth is the contemplation of the divine unity through its primary name, which is *Being* — treated in article twelve of Book One. The sixth is the contemplation of the most blessed Trinity through its name which is *Good* — treated in Book One in those articles concerning the first species of contemplation. The seventh is mental and mystical excess, in which rest is given to the intellect, the affection wholly passing over into God — which must be treated more carefully, God granting, in Book Three below.

### The Seven Grades from the Contemplativorum Aquilinos Obtutus

Furthermore, in another treatise beginning *Contemplativorum aquilinos obtutus* (*The Eagle-Gaze of the Contemplatives*), Bonaventure sets out seven other grades of contemplation, more sublime than those already mentioned, which he calls most glorious and known only by the knowledge of experience. He names them: fire, unction, ecstasy, speculation, taste, repose, and glory. For first the soul is set on fire, then enkindled it is anointed, then anointed it is rapt, then rapt it contemplates or speculates, then contemplating it tastes, then tasting it reposes. These six can be acquired on the way, not all at once but gradually; and the soul that is more frequently exercised in spiritual matters experiences them more quickly. The seventh — glory — is conferred in the homeland, the more abundantly as one has more perfectly exercised oneself in the preceding grades.

Fire (*ignis*) is the vehement fervor of divine heat or love, coming from above, which suddenly irradiating the contemplative soul with a very lucid splendor when she pours herself out before God in prayer, sets her greatly aflame, kindles dry movements and affections, burns up and blows away evil ones, warms tepidity, rouses torpor, and presents the soul to God as a most rich holocaust. On this grade has set foot the one who can say: "In my meditation a fire shall flame out" (cf. Ps. 38:4).

Unction (*unctio*) is a kind of rosy moisture that, diffusing itself through the whole soul, instructs and strengthens her, and disposes her to receive and contemplate the splendors of truth. With this was anointed the one who said: "He anointed me with the unction of his mercy."

Ecstasy (*ecstasis*) is, with the exterior person abandoned, a certain voluptuous elevation of the soul beyond itself, stretching itself as far as possible toward the super-intellectual source of divine love. The soul in ecstasy is not its own but God's whom it loves, as the blessed Dionysius, familiar with God's secrets, confesses (cf. *On the Divine Names*, ch. 4, n. 18).

Speculation (*speculatio*) is the pious intellectual and affective investigation of the riches of the Blessed and those celestial things that are fully knowable only by God — a penetrating and loving gaze into the divine depths, when the mind, penetrated by the illumined will, is copiously flooded, instructed, and clarified by divine light.

Taste (*gustus*) is a very sweet and abiding pre-taste of the inestimable divine sweetness, distilled by the munificence of the Holy Spirit into the souls of contemplatives.

Repose (*quies*) is a wonderful and sweet tranquility of the whole soul, created together with the sweetness infused into us from frequent prayer.

Glory (*gloria*) is the happiness of the Saints, "which eye hath not seen, nor ear heard, neither hath it entered into the heart" of wayfarers, as such (1 Cor. 2:9).

### Denis's Explanation of the Distinction of Grades

Denis here notes that it is difficult to elucidate these grades and explain how they differ from one another. For fire, taste, and repose do not seem to pertain to the essence of contemplation, nor are they species of it, but rather forms, complements, or effects accompanying it. And since in every properly so-called contemplation one finds the fire of love, spiritual unction, mental speculation, taste, and repose — how can a distinction of grades be taken from these?

The answer is that the division of contemplation through these is not as a genus is divided into species, but as something is divided and distinguished by degrees of more and less perfect, in the manner in which virtue is divided into civil virtue, purgative virtue, and virtue of the purified soul. The distinction of grades is made through what ornaments, perfects, and forms contemplation, as charity is said to be the form, ornament, and complement of the virtues. And these complements of contemplation, although in some degree and in their common sense they are found in every contemplation properly taken, are nonetheless more excellently present in one act of contemplation than another; and so, as received from Bonaventure here — according to a certain preeminence and strict sense — they constitute distinct grades of contemplation, and each seems, by a certain appropriation, to belong properly to its grade.

This is similar to what the divine Dionysius did in distinguishing the choirs and orders of the celestial spirits: for although fervor of love, fullness of knowledge, and the indwelling of God belong in some degree to every angelic mind, they are distinctly and appropriately attributed to the orders of the highest hierarchy.

## Article IX. On Contemplation according to the Mystical John Ruysbroeck

John Ruysbroeck — wonderful and divinely anointed, magnificently learned — wrote many profound things from his own experience concerning contemplation in his books.

### Three Requirements for Each State

At the beginning of the book *On the Perfection of the Sons of God*, he teaches that whoever wishes to live in the most perfect state of the Church must first be a resolutely active good person: for which three things are required. The first is purity of conscience, free from the accusation of mortal sin. The second is that one obey God, the Church, and right reason in all things — for to these three equal obedience is owed. The third is that in all things one intend principally and finally the honor of God, and that one desire to live according to his most gracious will.

Second, one must be a spiritual zealot: for which three things are required. The first is purity of mind. The second is spiritual freedom in desire, so that the mind be not inordinately attached to any created thing and thereby impeded in the desirous movement of will toward God. The third is to sense the intrinsic union with God.

Third, one must be elevated, contemplative, supernatural: for which also, as he confesses, three things are necessary. The first is that one sense the foundation of one's essence or being as unshakeable, and possess it so. The second is that one's exercise be in every way without mode (*modo carens* — that is, exceeding all measurable, creaturely modes). The third is that one's dwelling be divine fruition.

### The Foundation of Being

Denis confesses the darkness of his own understanding before these words, yet explains as best he can. The foundation of the contemplative's essence or being can be understood in two ways. First, God himself is understood by it — the fontal stabilization and causal foundation of all beings, which is unshakeable and incomprehensible: and the contemplative must attend to this lest he presumptuously intrude upon the divine mysteries, and so become a searcher of majesty and be overwhelmed by glory (cf. Prov. 25:27). He must, therefore, possess this foundation — which is above all things yet not exalted, below all things yet not oppressed, grounding, upholding, and conserving all — by gracious and affectionate contemplation as an utterly unsearchable being, knowing that he can in no way comprehend it.

Second, the foundation of the contemplative's essence can be understood as his own fundamental being — his own proper nature — which (as regards all that pertains to himself) cannot be fathomed by us, especially as regards his supernatural being, namely of sanctifying grace, and most of all as regards that which belongs to him through the singular grace of sublime contemplation. In this grace, the inmost and most secret being of the mind, emptied and stripped of all creaturely things, is so advanced and fixed in God by a most gracious union, that it is found to fail even in its own self-examination and self-distinction.

### The Vision of God's Immensity

From Ruysbroeck: whenever God opens himself to the spirit as an unfathomable abyss — high as heaven, deep as hell, long as the earth, broader than the sea (cf. Job 11:8-9) — the contemplating spirit perceives itself, in this manifestation, supernaturally sublimated, extended, and made deiform; and perceiving this, it acknowledges God's grace, and seeing God as incomparably more unshakeable, approaches his contemplation with fear, reverence, and sobriety.

### Exercise Without Mode

From this arises the second requirement: exercise that is super-rational and without mode. For the unity of God, which every contemplating spirit possesses in love, is itself eternally attracting and drawing inward the divine Persons and all loving spirits. This unity is nothing other than internal and inscrutable love, drawing Father and Son and all who live in them — that is, created minds united to God by the light of grace or glory — into eternal fruition.

The divine and uncreated Persons enjoy one another in love; and this fruition the elect enter and experience. And the contemplative who has renounced himself and all things, sensing no abstraction or distraction from God, since he possesses no thing as his own property — being free and unencumbered from all things — can always (with the help of grace) enter the innermost part of his spirit naked and pure. And there he finds an eternal light opened, namely God revealing and showing himself everywhere present, penetrating all things, and alone flowing into the essences of things: by which he senses that eternal drawing-inward of the divine unity, and himself as a perpetual flame of love; in which sweet burning he desires above all to be one with God and to render what is required of him by God.

### The Divine Unity

The in-drawing of the divine unity (*intromotio unitatis*) fosters in the created contemplating spirit an eternal burning — the fervor of love — from God's side, whose proper nature is to be unfailing (cf. 1 Cor. 13:8). None can sense or possess this simple unity of God unless he excels in immense clarity and in love that is super-rational and without mode — that is, eminently illumined by divine light and vehemently kindled with love of the highest Good.

In the super-information of unity the contemplator is formed from above by the fire of divine love, and marvellously transformed into the property of the divine nature by supernatural assimilation and most gracious imitation. All his spiritual powers fail in their operation, not attending to or transcending it, unable to love or contemplate God as God is visible and loveable in himself — as the Psalmist says: "My soul hath fainted after thy salvation" (cf. Ps. 118:81). And they sense nothing except that they are being burned into the simple unity of God: because they are wholly resolved into God by vehement love — not losing their own being, but loving in a deiform manner, not caring for or attending to themselves, not operating according to their own or natural mode, according to the Apostle: "And I live, now not I; but Christ liveth in me" (Gal. 2:20).

Ruysbroeck says: "In this love we wish to burn and to be consumed forever. For in this consists the happiness of all of us. Wherefore we must found our life on the abyss of infinite depth, namely God: and thus we shall be able to be submerged for ever in love, and from ourselves to be submerged into that inscrutable depth, loving God most acutely and penetratingly; and in that same love we shall be exalted, and from ourselves be exalted into the incomprehensible height of God, and in that love without mode we shall feel, and from ourselves we shall stray; and he shall lead us into the immense breadth of God's love, into which we shall flow, and from ourselves flow into the unknown delights of the opulence and goodness of the divine, in which we shall melt and dissolve, be submerged and deepened eternally in the glory of God, and thereby the fruition of him shall be the dwelling of our soul."

### The Eternal Embrace of the Trinity

Furthermore: the eternal Father with all who live in him, as a living depth, is turned actively and operatively toward his Son as toward his own wisdom; and the Son with all who live in him is reflected actively and operatively back to the Father — the depth from whom he flowed. In this encounter of Father and Son the third Person flows from both; this Love of both, with such opulence and joy, actively and fruitively embraces and penetrates Father and Son, that all creatures must be silent about it: for that wonderful and incomprehensible reality is superexalted above the intellect of all creatures. Where, therefore, that wonderful is understood and savoured without wonder, there the spirit stands above itself, and is one with the Spirit of God, and sees and savours like God the opulence which is God himself, without measure, in the unity or union of the living depth. This wonderful encounter is continuously renewed in us in active mode without intermission. For the Father gives himself in the Son and the Son in the Father in a certain eternal complacency and loving embrace, namely the Holy Spirit. And this also is ceaselessly renewed in the spirit of the contemplating soul every hour.

## Article X. On Contemplation according to the Abbot of Vercelli

Thomas Gallus, the glorious and contemplative Abbot of Vercelli, most studious expositor of the great Dionysius, wrote many sublime things concerning contemplation.

### The Kiss of the Mouth

In his commentary on the verse of the Canticle, "Let him kiss me with the kiss of his mouth" (Cant. 1:1), he writes: The contemplative mind, after it has surveyed all created things, wishing to be separated from the totality of existing things and to be united to the supersubstantial Spouse, begs the kiss — that is, conjunction with the Spouse — so as to be able to say with Job: "My soul hath chosen hanging, and my bones death" (Job 7:15). The hanging (*suspendium*) is the extension of the mind into the superfulgent theoretical or sapiential intuitions of divine things; and the bones are the most robust powers of the mind, namely the speculative intellect and the principal affection; and in "death" there is noted a failure and separation.

When the mind is taken up in the kiss of the Spouse by most chaste prayers, all operation and efficacy of intellect and affection fails regarding created things. The spark of the apex of affection (*scintilla apicis affectualis*) — which is the principal and pure participation in the divine goodness — ineffably separated from all that is lower, and marvellously transformed as into a divine life, is supernaturally deified; and deification is assimilation and union to divine things, insofar as possible. This is the spiritual and good death of which the divine Dionysius speaks in the *Ecclesiastical Hierarchy*: "Death in us is when the soul, departing from other things and releasing itself, is happily united to the supersubstantial Spouse."

Furthermore, according to Vercellensis, the soul uses three artifices to stand before the Divinity: revelation of the mind, aptitude for union, and most chaste prayers. The revelation of the mind consists in the withdrawal of exterior things and whatever causes stumbling. The aptitude for union is acquired by abundant devotion of the mind and interior resolution — for fluid things more easily unite. The most chaste prayers are those in which no temporal goods are sought nor temporal evils removed (as in chaste prayer), nor spiritual evils removed nor such goods obtained (as in the still more chaste prayer), but the Spouse himself is desired and asked to be embraced in himself — by which the best ointments, most gratefully perfusing the contemplative mind with sweetness, are those super-intellectual intuitions (*theoriæ*) that ravish the minds united to them and fill them with a copious effusion of inner sweetness, interior beauty, and intimate clarity.

But since no one comes to that inaccessible light unless the Father draw him, the soul burning for the taste of divine sweetness prays with the Spouse: "Draw me after thee" (Cant. 1:3) — that is: pour into me those rays of thine that will lead me to thee, which will move me upward to where I desire to go but cannot arrive by my own strength; therefore cleanse, illuminate, and enkindle my mind.

The best mode of seeking God is to extend oneself rightly in a super-intellectual manner upward, until the apex of the mind, separated from every created thing, supernaturally apprehends him who is above all things, and knows through unknowing him who is above all knowledge.

When the soul is established in the summit of the affective apex and placed in the superluminous darkness of divine light, she can say that verse of the Canticle: "I am black but beautiful" (Cant. 1:4) — that is, surrounded by the darkness of the divine incomprehensibility, yet adorned by his beautifying clarities. For blackness, being a shadowy colour, aptly designates the concealment of incomprehensible light.

When, through the exercise of contemplation, the soul has drunk the breasts of divine consolation, visible things at once grow dark, carnal things lose savour, all created things become tedious, even temporal life is judged more a death than a life. Hence the soul, intimately delighted in the presence of the Spouse through this briefly lasting contemplation — and attesting as it were with Blessed Peter, "Lord, it is good for us to be here" (Matt. 17:4) — seeks how she may stand incessantly before him and be joined to him most clearly, and says: "Tell me, O thou whom my soul loveth, where thou feedest, where thou liest in the midday" (Cant. 1:6).

### Four Things Required

For the perfect study of contemplation, four things are required, according to Vercellensis: the frequent beautification of the soul, happy alienation through spiritual abstraction, elongation of the alienated mind, and unitive application of the elongated mind. The beauty of the contemplative soul consists chiefly in this: that she preserve chastity and faith in ecstatic love toward the heavenly Spouse.

## Article XI. On Contemplation according to a Devout Man, namely Brother Guigo de Ponte, Monk of the Charterhouse

A devout man, in his treatise on contemplation, sets out twelve grades of contemplation, of which some are preparatory and dispositive to contemplation, others pertain formally or essentially to it.

### The Twelve Grades

The first is purification from sins. The second is the studious anxiety and diligent labor of the mind to obtain the divine unction and grace, without which the mind is arid, as grass without dew and rain. The third is devout compunction of heart, in which love reigns and from which devotion is generated, and sorrow, which is the origin of confession. The fourth is compassion with one's neighbors in their adversities and rejoicing with them in their prosperities. The fifth is compassion of soul in the Passion, affliction, and persecution of Christ. The sixth is the small fire of burning desire, which is strong prayer before God. The seventh is the visitation of the Spouse, or the inflowing of the Word of God, which graciously descends into the thirsting soul like a river of peace, through the infusion of hidden consolation. The eighth is the contemplation of the mind aspiring upward, which faithfully contemplates the citizens of the homeland and the King of glory presiding over them. The ninth is the continual meditation on divine things, as it is written: "On his law he shall meditate day and night" (Ps. 1:2). The tenth is the overshadowing of the divine darkness in the chamber of the heart, when the mind has entered the darkness of incomprehensible light, with the eyes of the soul fixed on the infinite light, possessing God dwelling with her, resting under the shadow of the desired one (cf. Cant. 2:3). This pertains to mystical theology, and is the contemplation of God by negation or denial. The eleventh is mental excess or ecstasy, by which vehicle the devout mind, transported, is frequently joined through ecstatic contemplation to the company of the celestial citizens, and contemplates the face of God as closely and sincerely as can be done. The twelfth is the blessed death and entry into the homeland.

### The Five Dispositive and the Rest

The first five grades are preparatory and dispositive to contemplation. The last five pertain substantively to contemplation, if by the last one is meant the vision of God in his essence, which is consummated contemplation. The sixth and seventh grades participate somewhat of both. For sincere prayer and interior consolation are not without contemplation, since prayer is the ascent of the mind to God, and consolation flows from the consideration of what is fitting.

### Three Species of Speculative Contemplation

Furthermore, this devout man introduces three species of speculative contemplation. The first is called natural or physical: this seeks and inspects the Creator through the mirror of creatures, through whose consideration the philosophers knew God. The second he calls scholastic: this contemplates God through the mirror of the Scriptures and acquired wisdom. This is said to be useful and necessary; yet sometimes it accidentally impedes the influxions of celestial illuminations, when the soul is held captive through excessive occupation in Scriptures and lessons, and is thereby distanced from the fellowship of God and from interior colloquy — which, alas, happens frequently to many religious who study indiscreetly and do not extend knowledge to practice. The third he calls divinely infused: this contemplates God through the rays of wisdom poured from above, and divine droppings touching the highest portion of the soul — as was more fully said in the preceding book, article nineteen.

The devout Guigo writes many other useful things in his treatise, some of which may find a place below.
