# On the Fountain of Light and the Paths of Life

*Denis the Carthusian*

## Proemium

*Who is wise, and he shall understand these things? prudent, and he shall know these things? for the ways of the Lord are right, and the just shall walk in them: but the transgressors shall fall in them.* (Osee xiv, 10)

These words the holy prophet Osee uttered, in their letter, first to intimate the difficulty of his book, and second to mark how few understand his sayings. And adding the reason of both, he said: "for the ways of the Lord are right." In them he declares the just will walk, but transgressors will fall in them: for although meanwhile they may perhaps have begun to set out in them, they do not persevere in them to their salvation. But according to the mystical sense these words are rightly taken of the fitting knowledge of the threefold way by which one goes to the fountain of beatitude, to the last end, to the Creator of all, God sublime and blessed: in whose most clear, loving and fruitive contemplation the happiness of all created minds consists, as the Only-begotten of the eternal Father himself testifies, speaking to the Father: *This is eternal life: that they may know thee, the true God* (John xvii, 3). And again elsewhere: *I am*, he says, *Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the end* (Apoc. xxii, 13). Of whom the prophet Jeremias: *Truly*, he says, *in the Lord our God is the salvation of Israel* (Jer. iii, 23).

And since few walk by right ways to this most blessed and beatifying end, God incomparably glorious, most of all in these days, of which the Apostle said, *Know also this, that in the last days shall come dangerous times: men shall be lovers of themselves, covetous, haughty, lovers of pleasures more than of God* (II Tim. iii, 1, 2, 4); therefore, to intimate how few there are who effectively understand the three ways by which one must tend to the Most High, namely the purgative, the illuminative and the perfective way, I have with reason taken up the prophetic word set out above: "Who is wise, and he shall understand these things?" and so on. For these very things that will be said of this threefold way are known, or can become clear, only to the truly wise. And if any other man says that he knows them, he will deny it by his deeds; and his knowledge will be either nothing, or at best formless, bare and dry, and deserving of a heavier damnation, as the Saviour says: *The servant who knew the will of his lord, and did not, shall be beaten with many stripes* (Luke xii, 47). Besides, that all the wicked are ignorant of the ways of the Lord, holy Scripture teaches abundantly. For it is written: *They have been rebellious to the light, they have not known his ways* (Job xxiv, 13). And all who are in mortal sin are rebellious to the light, that is, to the uncreated light, the holy and true God, and to the judgement of right reason. Hence the Prophet says: *They have not known the way of wisdom, neither have they remembered her paths* (Baruch iii, 23). Therefore all such are called in the divine Scriptures foolish and senseless. Of whom Solomon speaks: *The number of fools is infinite* (Eccles. i, 15). The Apostle too: *God*, he says, *hath made foolish the wisdom of this world* (I Cor. i, 20). For in such there is the vice of childishness, which is also called folly, the contrary of true and saving wisdom. For as little children prefer apples and pears to gold and gems, so the most wretched lovers of this world and the most base lovers of carnal uncleanness choose and set before the highest and unchangeable good, uncircumscribed and incomparable, these perishing, vain and worthless things: than which nothing more stupid, nothing more unreasonable, nothing more wicked can be devised. So it is plain how few are truly wise, namely those alone who stand in charity and grace, whose heart is free from mortal fault. For since the most glorious God is the fountain of all light and wisdom, it is certain that all who are turned away from God live in darkness and folly, as the Psalmist sings: *They have not known nor understood: they walk on in darkness* (Ps. lxxxi, 5). The Apostle too: *Their foolish heart*, he says, *was darkened* (Rom. i, 21).

Since these things are so, I, desiring you, O my dearest in Christ, to be a nursling of true and saving wisdom, and to walk by God's roads to the prize of eternal beatitude, propose, with the help and working of the most sweet Holy Spirit, to write out of love for you something on the pure and shining ways of God, and especially on the threefold saving way by which true and everlasting happiness is obtained. And I shall proceed briefly and succinctly, putting these things together as it were for a daily exercise, as you have now often and devoutly asked. Almost twelve years ago I wrote you a little work on the Narrow Way of Salvation and the Contempt of the World: none the less, in the meantime you have walked by broad and dark ways. But now, come to a riper age, and penitent in heart, you have out of devotion taken holy orders in the city, proposing hereafter to live as a priest and a canon. And since, the Lord granting, within fifteen days you are to sing your first Mass, I, wishing to offer you something at it, and having no gold or silver, thought to offer to your charity at it the present devotional exercise and little work of my poverty, trusting that this will please your probity and prudence more than a few gold coins: praying most affectionately that you exercise yourself in it perseveringly every day hereafter, as you have now firmly resolved, lest you fall back into your former evils. To which many things now surely bind you, namely the canonry, the priesthood, learning, and very many other things of the kind.

### Article I

*Of the manifold way of which holy Scripture treats*

*Ask for the good way, and walk ye in it: and you shall find refreshment for your souls* (Jer. vi, 16).

A way is called a means between two extremes. Some, then, are ways of God by which God proceeds to his creatures, especially the intellectual and rational: not by changing place, but by pouring something into them, or disposing something in them, or exercising judgements. And so the processions and influences, the works, decrees and executions of God are called his ways. Of which the uncreated and begotten Wisdom, which is the eternal Word and only Son of God, confesses through the Wise Man: *The Lord possessed me in the beginning of his ways* (Prov. viii, 22). Of which it is also asserted in Job: *Lo, these things are said in part of his ways* (Job xxvi, 14). And the Apostle: *How incomprehensible*, he says, *are his judgements, and how unsearchable his ways!* (Rom. xi, 33). Other ways of God are those by which the rational creature walks and reaches him: such as the divine precepts and the evangelical counsels of Christ, good works, virtuous desires, holy thoughts, fruitful speech, and adversities borne for God with an even mind. Of which ways it is written: *Just and true are all thy ways, O Lord God almighty, King of ages* (Apoc. xv, 3). Christ too in the Gospel: *Narrow*, he says, *is the way, and strait the gate, that leadeth to life* (Matth. vii, 14). Of the first ways the Prophet says: *All the ways of the Lord are mercy and truth* (Ps. xxiv, 10); of the second: *Shew, O Lord, thy ways to me, and teach me thy paths* (Ps. xxiv, 4).

Besides, a second distinction of the way is that one is the way of the elect, another of the reprobate. Of which Solomon: *The path of the just*, he says, *as a shining light, goeth forwards and increaseth even to perfect day. The way of the wicked is darksome: they know not where they fall* (Prov. iv, 18, 19). The way of the elect is a virtuous manner of life, a penitential life, the keeping of the commandments. Of which the Psalmist sings: *Blessed are the undefiled in the way, who walk in the law of the Lord* (Ps. cxviii, 1). The way of the reprobate is a wicked life, a carnal and unfearing manner of living, the transgression of the divine law. Of which way the Saviour says: *Wide is the gate, and broad is the way that leadeth to destruction* (Matth. vii, 13). Now this way of the elect is rightly called shining and beautiful. First, because it proceeds from the most loving direction of the Holy Spirit and is flooded with his light. For in every meritorious act we need the actual motion and direction of the Holy Spirit, who (as the Apostle testifies) *worketh in us both to will and to accomplish* (Philipp. ii, 13). Second, because it advances from sanctifying grace and charity, which are the spiritual lights of the mind, and is formed and brightened by their splendour. Third, because it arises from the judgement of right reason. For every virtuous action is born of deliberate reason. Fourth, because it joins our hearts to God, who is pure and infinite light, the most splendid and most beautiful end of all things. For *he that abideth in charity, abideth in God, and God in him* (I John iv, 16). And *he who is joined to the Lord, is one spirit* with him (I Cor. vi, 17). Fifth, because it drives the darkness of vices from the soul, and makes it comely and bright and beautiful, so much so that the heavenly Bridegroom says to it: *How beautiful art thou, my love, how beautiful art thou! thy eyes are doves' eyes, besides what is hid within; thy hair* and so on (Cant. iv, 1). And because the way of the wicked lacks these lights, disfigures and darkens the mind and turns it from God, makes it also a dwelling of demons, and drags it to the infernal darkness; therefore it is rightly called dark, according to that word of Jeremias: *The way of the wicked shall be to them as a slippery way in the dark: for they shall be driven on, and fall therein* (Jer. xxiii, 12).

But it may be asked how the way of the wicked can be said to be dark, so far that the wicked are said not to know where they fall, when some sin with certain knowledge and with deliberate reason and purpose: who, as Jeremias testifies, *are wise to do evils* (Jer. iv, 22); who as it were on purpose withdraw from God; who surely know that they do evil, and neglect the things they are bound to, and even fall into the depth of vices, and deserve the torments of hell. And it must be answered that, according to the theologians and the philosophers, everyone who sins is ignorant, errs and judges badly: because although he judges well in general, yet not in particular; rather, by passion, ignorance or malice, the judgement of his reason in certain things to be done or avoided is corrupted, blinded and depraved, and he accepts evil under the appearance of some good. Therefore the divine Dionysius declares that no one acts looking to evil (On the Divine Names, ch. 4). And the Wise Man: *Every way of a man*, he says, *seemeth right to himself* (Prov. xxi, 2); and again: *Fools will mock at sin* (Prov. xiv, 9); and again: *Their own malice*, he says, *blinded them* (Wisd. ii, 21).

Further, a third distinction of the way is that one is an inward and formal way, as a good or bad action. Of which holy Job professes: *Doth not he consider my ways, and number all my steps?* (Job xxxi, 4). Another is an outward and objective way. And so the Son of God asserts of himself, *I am the way, the truth and the life* (John xiv, 6). And Isaias says to Israel: *Thou hast made thy body as the ground, and as a way to them that went over* (Is. li, 23).

A fourth distinction is that one way is evidently evil; another is a way apparently or plausibly good, and yet truly evil. Of which it is read in the Wise Man: *There is a way which seemeth just to a man: but the ends thereof lead to death* (Prov. xiv, 12). This way is wont to deceive even the good, since there are certain vices which are thought to be virtues: as immoderate rigour, justice; laxity, kindness; dissoluteness, cheerfulness and good recreation. So some persuade themselves that a plurality of ecclesiastical benefices is lawful, so that they may be able to exercise hospitality and the other works of mercy more abundantly: not attending to how, as the divine Dionysius teaches, the good must come from a whole cause; nor that good things mixed with evil please God, nor that alms from robbery are accepted, nor that works of piety which tend to the lessening and derogation of divine worship, and to the dismembering of the Church and the abandonment of the dead, are pleasing to God. So the wretched deceive themselves.

Lastly, a fifth distinction of the way is that the saving way is threefold. The first is the purgative way, the second the illuminative, the third the unitive or perfective. Of which threefold way it is our purpose to treat in the present little work.

### Article II

*How our Lord Jesus Christ is the purgative, illuminative and perfective way*

*There is one mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus* (I Tim. ii, 5).

The Lord Jesus Christ, who in the unity of his person is true God and true man, according to his deity is our end, and according to his humanity is the way to the last end. Wherefore he said: *I am the way; no man cometh to the Father, but by me* (John xiv, 6). And elsewhere: *I am*, he says, *the door. By me, if any man enter in, he shall be saved* (John x, 9). He is indeed the objective and exemplary way, since he is the most right exemplar set before us to imitate, as the apostle John writes in his first Epistle: *He that saith he abideth in him, ought himself also to walk, even as he walked* (I John ii, 6). Hence too the most blessed prince of the Apostles testifies: *Christ suffered for us, leaving you an example that you should follow his steps* (I Pet. ii, 21). And Christ himself has spoken: *If any man minister to me, let him follow me* (John xii, 26). So then Christ is the way between the fountain of our beatitude, God one and three, who is the supreme summit and last end of all, and mortal wayfarers, that is, men, whom he reconciled to God. Wherefore he is called the mediator of God and men.

Further, Christ is the purgative way for a fourfold reason. First, because by his terrible threats, his most just rebukes and his most holy teachings he draws us back from sins and amends us; as when he says in the Gospel: *Unless you shall do penance, you shall all likewise perish* (Luke xiii, 3, 5); and again: *Fear him that can destroy both soul and body in hell* (Matth. x, 28); and again: *Then he shall say to them also that shall be on his left hand: Depart from me, you cursed, into everlasting fire which was prepared for the devil and his angels* (Matth. xxv, 41). Second, because by the examples of his most holy manner of living he called us back, snatched us and purged us from the uncleanness of fault. Hence he himself says: *He that followeth me, walketh not in darkness* (John viii, 12). Third, because by the virtue and merit of his saving passion he redeemed the whole human race from the bonds of sins, according to that word of the Apocalypse: *Christ hath loved us, and washed us from our sins in his own blood* (Apoc. i, 5). And Isaias: *He was wounded for our iniquities, he was bruised for our sins* (Is. liii, 5). Fourth, because through the sacraments of the Church he purges his faithful daily. For the sacraments draw from Christ's passion their power of purging and sanctifying, and are the medicinal vessels by which the sicknesses of souls are healed.

Again, Christ is the illuminative way. First, because he instructed us with the wisdom of the evangelical law, and taught us the contemplation of divine things, according to that word of John: *That was the true light, which enlighteneth every man that cometh into this world* (John i, 9). Hence again John in his epistle: *We know*, he says, *that the Son of God is come: and he hath given us understanding that we may know the true God* (I John v, 20). Hence he himself says to God the Father: *I have manifested thy name to the men* (John xvii, 6). Second, because from Christ as from the head the gifts of grace and the light of wisdom descend into his members, by the virtue and merit of his most sacred life and passion in this world, which were wholly meritorious for us. Therefore it is found in John: *Grace and truth came by Jesus Christ, and of his fulness we all have received* (John i, 17, 16).

Moreover, Christ is the perfective way. First, because he gave the law containing the fullness of all perfection, commanded perfect charity, and showed the consummation of all virtues. For he is the supreme Lawgiver, in whose law every evil is driven off, every good taught. Of whom that word in Job is most truly said: *Behold, God is high in his strength, and none is like him among the lawgivers* (Job xxxvi, 22). He it is who taught and commanded that God is to be loved with the whole heart, the whole mind, the whole soul and all one's strength (Luke x, 27), and bade not only friends but even enemies be loved; and among other things also said: *Be you therefore perfect, as also your heavenly Father is perfect* (Matth. v, 44, 48). Second, because he merited for us true and full beatitude, and joins us immediately to the last end. Therefore he says: *I give them life everlasting* (John x, 28). And again he says to the Father: *Father, I will that where I am, they also whom thou hast given me may be with me; that they may see my glory* (John xvii, 24). Third, because to the precepts of the Decalogue he added the evangelical counsels, by which most ardent charity, the highest purity and true perfection are obtained, and one draws as near as is possible in this life to future beatitude. Fourth, because he most perfectly fulfilled charity and every virtue, as he himself declares: *Greater love than this no man hath, that a man lay down his life for his friends* (John xv, 13). Fifth, because by loving us so most ardently and by doing and bearing so much for our salvation, he most greatly provoked us to love of him and to the fervour of divine charity, and, as far as in him lay, set us on fire. Weighing which the Apostle: *I live in the faith of the Son of God, who loved me, and delivered himself for me* (Gal. ii, 20). Hence the evangelical law is called the law of love.

So then Christ is the purgative way as saviour, according to that word: *The Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost* (Luke xix, 10). And so this name, Jesus, befits him, the angel saying to Joseph: *Thou shalt call his name Jesus. For he shall save his people from their sins* (Matth. i, 21). But Christ is the illuminative way as master and teacher, as he confessed of himself in the Gospel: *One is your master, Christ* (Matth. xxiii, 8, 10); and again: *You call me Master, and Lord; and you say well, for so I am* (John xiii, 13). And so he is deservedly called Counsellor, as was foretold in this prophetic presage: *His name shall be called Wonderful, Counsellor* (Is. ix, 6). He is, finally, the perfective way as priest and pontiff and most ardent lover. Of whom the chief Apostle says in his first Epistle: *You are now converted to the shepherd and bishop of your souls* (I Pet. ii, 25). And the pontifical order is especially perfective, as the great prince of theologians Dionysius says (On the Ecclesiastical Hierarchy, ch. 5). And so it befits Christ to be called the Father of the world to come and the Prince of peace, as it is read prophesied in Isaias.

### Article III

*What the formal purgative way is*

*By one man sin entered into this world, and by sin death* (Rom. v, 12).

As holy Scripture teaches, *God made man right* from the beginning (Eccles. vii, 30), because he created the first-formed man in original justice, by which the flesh was subject to the spirit, the sensitive part to reason, and reason to God in all things promptly and without resistance. But when the first parent transgressed the divine command, the flesh at once began to be rebellious to the spirit, and sensuality to reason, and reason to God. Hence all are born in original sin, and the tinder of sin remains in us, and *the flesh lusteth against the spirit* (Gal. v, 17), and reason and will are wounded, and we are prone to every evil, and weak too in resisting. We need, then, first of all, to exercise ourselves in the purgative way, so that we may be purged from all past vices, avoid the innumerable sins on every side, and be cleansed from the remains of vices, that is, from the proneness and inclination to faults, and so return to conformity with original justice.

The purgative way, then, is the exercise and effort or occupation by which, as by a road and a means, we tend and strive towards the expulsion of vices, the bridling of the passions and concupiscences, the reformation of all the affections of the soul, the guarding of hearts and senses, and the avoidance of all faults. This is plainly *not to be conformed to this* wicked *world* (Rom. xii, 2), to put off the old man and be reformed in the inner man (Coloss. iii, 9; Rom. xii, 2), to chastise the body and bring it into the servitude of the spirit (I Cor. ix, 27), to insist worthily on the acts of penance too (Acts xxvi, 20), and so to watch over, attend to and dwell on the purging and purity of the soul. For since the Lord our God is essentially holy and unboundedly pure, good, true and wise, he requires, loves and embraces a pure and virtuous and wise soul; nor does anything separate us from him and hinder us from his graces except sin, by which we are made unlike and hateful to our Creator. Hence, then, first of all we must be cleansed from sins and always avoid them, as Isaias testifies: *This is all the fruit, that the sin thereof should be taken away* (Is. xxvii, 9); and the Wise Man: *To God the wicked and his wickedness are hateful alike* (Wisd. xiv, 9). Hence the Psalmist: *Thou hatest all the workers of iniquity* (Ps. v, 7). And again Scripture says: *God loveth none but him that dwelleth with wisdom* (Wisd. vii, 28).

Yet because daily, alas, we sin, and *in many things we all offend* (James iii, 2) (for *a just man shall fall seven times* (Prov. xxiv, 16)), so that in truth all our justices are *as the rag of a menstruous woman* (Is. lxiv, 6); hence we must exercise ourselves daily in this purgative way, as is read in Ecclesiasticus: *My son, hast thou sinned? do so no more: but for thy former sins also pray that they may be forgiven thee* (Ecclus. xxi, 1). Now, then, the practice of this way is to be touched on briefly.

### Article IV

*How we ought to exercise ourselves daily in the purgative way*

*Wash yourselves, be clean, take away the evil of your devices from my eyes, learn to do well, saith the Lord* (Is. i, 16-18).

In every sin two things are generally found. The first is the mind's turning away from the highest and unchangeable good, God most high and immense. The second is the mind's turning to created goods, perishing, vain, foul and worthless. By reason of the first the sinner greatly dishonours God and holds him cheap, to whom he does not fear to prefer and choose such vain and worthless things; by reason of the second he takes disordered and unlawful delight in created and vain things. And by reason of both a punishment is due to man for his fault: for by reason of his turning away from God the punishment of loss is due to him, namely the lack of the divine and beatific vision; by reason of the second the punishment of sense is due to him, that is, the torment of the infernal fire.

Hence one who wishes to exercise himself in the purgative way, truly to repent and be purged and be preserved from vices, must do three things in order. First, for his contempt and slighting of God, he must humble, cast down and despise himself to the utmost and most deeply, and judge himself most worthy of all confusion and punishment, and the more, the more gravely and frequently he has offended the almighty and incomparable Lord of majesty; and he must with his whole heart extol and praise God. Second, for the lustful and vicious delight which he took in sinning, he must take up heartfelt sorrow and true, condign and proportionate contrition, so that the more enormous and the more his sins were, the more intense his contrition and the more heartfelt his sorrow: because, as St Jerome confesses, contrition must equal the sin or be proportioned to it, so that to the greatness and number of the faults the greatness of the sorrow corresponds, and hence sorrow for sin be so much more intense than for any bodily loss and temporal harm, as the evil of fault is greater than the evil of punishment, and as it is more damnable and unhappy to have lost the immense uncreated good than temporal things, and as it is worse to have wounded the soul and deserved eternal punishment than to have incurred bodily loss or hardship: which is not absolutely a hardship, if it is wisely attended to or borne with an even mind; rather it then profits spiritually and in many ways. Third, for the punishments due to his sins, let him chastise himself with voluntary penance, and humbly allow himself to be rebuked and corrected, and not omit to make satisfaction at the judgement of his confessor and judge.

So then one who wishes to be purged, reconciled to God, to please him and be united to him, let him place himself daily, twice or three times when he has more leisure, especially in the evening or at night, before the supreme Judge in a secret and quiet place; and examining himself, and calling past evils to memory, let him address God with deep humility, heartfelt contrition and a true readiness to make satisfaction, saying or meditating thus:

Almighty and eternal and most kind God, most merciful Lord Jesus Christ, my Creator and the Redeemer of my soul, behold, I am that most impious, most worthless and most wicked sinner, wholly abominable, contemptible and most wretched, made by sinning baser and worse than any brute, who so enormously and frequently, so boldly and shamelessly have dishonoured your majesty and your presence, offended your holiness, despised your goodness, charity and kindness, transgressed your commandments, abused your gifts, been most ungrateful for your benefits. From you, my God, the God of all my salvation, I have turned away my heart, and daily I am so guilty and negligent. Such and such sins I have committed, and so many good things I have omitted.

Then let him specially recall, weigh and ponder some of his graver sins, and some daily ones too, and say: O most kind Saviour and pitier, you know how enormously in this and in this I have offended your holiness. And if he has gone to excess in carnal sins, let him not dwell long on the memory of them, lest some temptation, tickling or delight be born to him from it. And so at the memory of each sin specially, and for all generally, let him inwardly grieve, groan, sigh; and striking his breast with the publican (Luke xviii, 13), and for shame and blushing at the foulness and malice of his vices not daring to lift his eyes to heaven, let him say from the heart this with Esdras: *My God, I am confounded and ashamed to lift up my face to thee: for our iniquities are multiplied over our heads, and our sins are grown up even unto heaven* (I Esdras ix, 6); and this: *O God, be merciful to me a sinner* (Luke xviii, 13); and again: *For my iniquities are gone over my head: and as a heavy burden are become heavy upon me* (Ps. xxxvii, 5). Meanwhile, thinking over his sins in the manner just touched on, let him consider diligently the circumstances and everything by which sins are aggravated, namely place, time, manner, frequency, and whether he was in holy orders, beneficed or learned, or set over others, or sinning of purpose and with certain knowledge.

Finally, these things done, kneeling, or prostrating himself on the ground with his whole body, his hands joined together or stretched out in the form of a cross, let him pray for full remission in these or like words: O most merciful God, supremely lovable, most excellent and above all things most desirable Lord Jesus Christ, for your infinite goodness, charity and mercy, through all that you took on, did and suffered for my salvation, through your blessed incarnation, your most holy manner of living in this world, your most bitter passion, through your dearest Mother, the most sweet and most kind virgin Mary, through the prayers and merits of all the Saints and devout, pardon me, a most grave and most worthless sinner, and pour into me your most loving grace, by which hereafter I may avoid all sins, bear myself before you with fear and virtue, and never again turn my most wretched mind away from you, my God.

Besides, so that the sinner may kindle contrition and compunction in himself more strongly, he may bind his hands like a thief, or turn them behind his back, and standing thus before the Most High say: Behold, O most glorious God, eternal and fearful Judge, I am that worst of robbers and most unjust thief, who by sinning so often and scandalising others have killed my own soul and the souls of others, and wickedly stolen them from you, their Creator and Saviour, and handed them over to demons; and so, as far as in me lay, I have hindered the fruit of your passion and blood, and by pride have sacrilegiously usurped the glory and honour that are due to you alone, and have sinned beyond the sand of the sea. And saying these things let him grieve from his heart, pour out tears, and repent that he does not grieve enough; and let him beseech the divine clemency for the grace of a condign contrition. These now completed, let him firmly resolve to amend, to avoid the occasions of sinning, to confess at a fitting time, to make satisfaction, and to keep watch over himself in all things.

### Article V

*Of the continuation of this exercise; and of the watchful consideration of the general and special benefits of God*

*Keep thyself therefore, and thy soul carefully. Forget not the words* of the Lord *all the days of thy life* (Deut. iv, 9).

Since wounds repeated are graver, and to fall back after pardon has been granted is of greater perversity and ingratitude; therefore it is not enough to accuse oneself, exercise oneself and ask pardon and grace in the manner described, unless after these things a man is continually on guard and untiringly careful not to offend the Lord of majesty again. Therefore he must unceasingly weigh the presence of the Almighty and his strict judgement, guard his inward and outward senses, avoid unlawful words, bridle the movements of the passions, strive to scandalise no one and to edify all, and do the work of God reverently, since *cursed be he that doth* it *negligently* (Jer. xlviii, 10); be moderate in food, drink, sleep and clothing, and in all things let his intention be right. In these and like things belonging to his vocation, and which God requires of him, let him bear himself perseveringly, fruitfully and strenuously. And because on God's road not to advance is to fall back, let him strive every day to grow in virtues, to be established in grace, to be strengthened in devotion. If he acts thus even for a short time, he will beyond doubt experience great advance, tranquillity and sweetness of mind (or at least at times) in God. But because to fulfil these things is not within human frailty, let him not cease to call on God's help, and be always ready to resist every temptation.

Besides, since diligent consideration of God's benefits not only stirs, increases and kindles charity, but also drives off despair and gives confidence, and leads to a heartfelt detestation of vices, and makes one weigh them more fully and see their gravity more clearly; therefore, so that the sinner may more sharply see and penetrate the enormity of his excesses, detest and hate them, and yet not despair but breathe again and trust in the Lord, it is very useful that he vividly recall the benefits of God, bodily and spiritual, natural and supernatural, general and special, and address the Lord thus:

O most high and most gracious and most sweet God, what shall I render you for all that you have conferred on me, most ungrateful? For you formed me out of nothing; you made me not a stone or a beast, but a rational creature, sealing me with your image and likeness; you led me to the grace of Baptism, in which you not only cleansed my soul from original sin, but adorned it too with grace and virtues. You have kept me in being until now, and given me the necessities of life, and preserved me from very many dangers; and you have borne with me so long in my crimes, and as often as I penitently asked pardon, you most mercifully granted it, and always returned good for evil. And though you have already struck many with eternal damnation who sinned much less than I, most unworthy, and who received lesser gifts from you than I, yet you have spared me until now, and after so many and such great crimes have led me to grace, to penance and to this enlightenment, instructed me in holy letters, and conferred various gifts on me. Moreover you, eternal Father, sent your only-begotten Son into the world for my salvation; you willed him to be made flesh, to live among men, to preach and to be crucified; with his most precious body and blood you refresh us unceasingly; and you have also poured out and conferred the Holy Spirit on the faithful. You too, Only-begotten of the eternal Father, deigned to take on, to work and to suffer so many and such great things for our saving, and so give yourself to us in the Sacrament, and through the holy angels and the men set over us, your vicars, you always guard us and draw us back from many and great vices. But, O most kind God, the greater your love, condescension and beneficence towards me, the graver, alas, is my ingratitude, and the more inexcusable and worse my depravity. Yet, because you have so loved us, done and borne so much for us, so most clemently gone before us, so long borne with us in vices, and now out of your immense kindness have led me to this recognition and penance, I neither can nor dare despair: especially since you yourself have most vehemently forbidden despair, and one who despairs most greatly derogates from your uncircumscribable goodness and kindness, since it is infinitely greater than all our malice, fault and ingratitude. Although, then, O most holy God, I have sinned so dreadfully that I am unworthy of the use and service of all your creatures, nay, have deserved this a thousand times, that all created things should rise against me and avenge on me the injury of their Creator; yet I do not despair nor will I despair, but I breathe again in your infinite kindness and pity, I cast myself now into the abyss of your goodness, I hide myself from your wrath in Christ's wounds, through all that he did and bore for me I beseech to obtain mercy and salvation, and I resolve hereafter to cleave to you without turning, to be subject to you, to serve you.

### Article VI

*Of the frequent and affectionate meditation of the Lord's passion, and of certain general remedies and helps against temptations and vices*

*I will be mindful and remember, and my soul shall languish within me* (Lam. iii, 20).

As the philosophers say, from acts often repeated a habit or custom is generated, which is like a second nature, because through it a man works most readily and with delight. In order, then, that we may always have at hand to meditate most readily on Christ's passion, and to look on it too with the eyes of the heart lovingly, fruitfully and effectively, let us accustom ourselves to recall it daily, diligently and fully, and to imagine it so as if we saw Christ really and presently suffering, crucified and dying for us, so that we remember it with inward compassion.

Let us attend, then, to how the Son of God, the most meek Lamb, God and man, our Creator, Saviour and lover, after the last supper, and his threefold prayer, and his very great sorrow and trembling and bloody sweat (Mark xiv, 17 and following; Luke xxii, 44), was taken, bound, pushed, dragged by those most cruel, most impious and most base retainers, and led to the house of Annas and Caiphas (John xviii, 12-24), and there condemned to death, and blindfolded, spat on, struck, blasphemed (Mark xiv, 64, 65); then presented like a robber in bonds to the impious governor, most falsely accused, sent to Herod, mocked by him with a white garment and sent back to the governor (Matth. xxvii; Luke xxiii, 1 and following), stripped, bound to the pillar, torn with scourges, crowned with thorns, struck with a reed, mocked too with purple, and again spat on, blasphemed, and his sacred face drenched with blessed blood; after this sentenced to a most shameful death, and led to it carrying the cross on his own shoulders (John xix, 17 and following), fastened to the cross, hung between robbers, pierced through hands and feet with cruel nails, given vinegar and gall to drink, pierced with a lance, suffering unspeakably with his most sweet Mother, killed by a most bitter and most ignominious death.

Let us behold too how most patiently, most meekly, how silently, how lovingly and how most humbly the Lord Jesus Christ bore all these things: who *was led as a sheep to the slaughter, and was dumb as a lamb before his shearer* (Is. liii, 7); *who, when he was reviled, did not revile: when he suffered, he threatened not* (I Pet. ii, 23).

Besides, concerning this passion of Christ we ought to weigh who, of what kind and how great he is who suffered these things; likewise by whom, and by what kind of men, and for whom, and in what place and time, for what fruit and end. For not anyone whatever, but the Only-begotten of God, true God and true man, the Lord of hosts, the King of glory, suffered these things for us, and that at the hands of a people once peculiarly his own, in the royal city, on the most solemn day, before many thousands of men. All of which, if diligently weighed, increase compassion not a little, kindle love, and edify the one who meditates.

Further, this meditation of the Lord's passion is very just, virtuous and wholesome. First, because the charity by which we are bound to love Christ, and the virtue of gratitude, require that we remember what Christ did and bore for us: otherwise we should be slothfully ungrateful, since forgetfulness of benefits is called the greatest part of ingratitude.

Second, because Christ's passion is like a book of conspicuous shining doctrine, in which all the virtues are most compendiously and best learned, namely: the highest charity, because the Lord bore these things for us, and *laid down his life for his sheep* (John x, 15); the greatest patience, because in bearing so many and such great things he was moved against his enemies by no disordered sadness, no grief, no disturbance; the deepest humility, in that he exalted himself in nothing, but remained most humble in heart, words and bearing; the firmest fortitude, because he bore his most bitter death with a constant and solid mind.

Third, because the memory of Christ's passion is against all temptations and the ranks of vices, against vanities and pleasures and pomps, the strongest armour, an unconquered remedy and the greatest help. For who is so incontinent that he would not restrain himself by recalling Christ's passion in his inmost heart? Who so ambitious, proud and vain that he would not humble his spirit by weighing Christ's insults, injuries, blasphemies, scorns and his most profound humility? Who so wrathful, gluttonous or covetous that he would not be pierced by considering Christ's humility, patience, stripping, poverty, and his drink of vinegar and gall? So in every temptation, especially carnal, in every stirring of anger, pride, gluttony and the other vices, let us set the Lord's passion before our heart, impress it, gaze on it; and so we shall drive from our hearts all useless, fanciful and vicious occupations, thoughts and affections.

There are, moreover, other common and vigorous remedies against temptations and vices, namely: fervent and frequent prayer, according to that word in the Psalm, *Praising I will call upon the Lord: and I shall be saved from my enemies* (Ps. xvii, 4); heartfelt meditation too on the four last things, according to that word, *In all thy works remember thy last end, and thou shalt never sin* (Ecclus. vii, 40); likewise to avoid the occasions of sinning and idleness, that is, place, time, little gifts, incautious looks, unseemly touches, vain conversations; diligent occupation too in the Scriptures, but also invocation of the glorious Virgin and of one's own guardian angel, and of the Saints towards whom one has greater love and devotion, and many other things, of which I have written more at length elsewhere.

### Article VII

*How a priest ought to bear himself before celebration, in celebration and after celebration*

*I will wash my hands among the innocent; and will compass thy altar, O Lord* (Ps. xxv, 6).

What the manner of life of the priests of the evangelical law ought to be, the apostle Paul describes in his Epistles, namely to Timothy and to Titus. For what he says there of bishops is for the greatest part to be understood of priests too, according to all the doctors; and this is most fully treated in the Decretum (part 1, dist. 25 and following). Indeed it is proved from the Gospel how holily they ought to live. For how virtuous and exemplary the life of any Christian ought to be, the evangelical doctrine lays open. For the Christian takes his name from Christ. Nor is anyone truly and worthily called a Christian unless he follows Christ's footsteps, learning from the Saviour that he is *meek and humble of heart* (Matth. xi, 29); who loves friends and enemies; who flees every mortal sin, and fulfils all the commandments of God and the Church; who, *having food and wherewith to be covered*, is *content* (I Tim. vi, 8); who, converted, has become *as a little child* (Matth. xviii, 3), simple, kind, just and innocent, avoiding superfluity, pomp and vanity in dress, furnishings and the rest, chaste, sober, hospitable.

Now priests are bound to a life so much more praiseworthy and exemplary than the common people, as they stand in a more sublime degree and order. And since they daily consecrate, handle, offer and receive the most worthy sacrament of the Eucharist, they must be wholly of a pure and divine life, so that they may be full of the ardour of spiritual love, and wholly intent on the most divine Sacrament, lest they handle it with polluted hands or receive it with a defiled mouth, and lest they give the people any occasion of bearing themselves less worthily towards the most supernatural Sacrament, which the angelic spirits themselves hold in awe.

But on this matter I have already written so many and such great things in other little works (see vol. xxxv, pp. 329 and following, 400 and following, 463 and following), that it would be tedious to write more from it now. None the less, to touch on a few things briefly, it must be weighed that (as holy Augustine asserts) in every sacrifice four things are to be attended to, namely who, what, to whom and why. The priest, then, before he proceeds to celebrate, let him weigh these four. First, who he is who is about to celebrate: in truth a defective and frail man. Therefore let him examine his conscience, sift his life, and whatever fault he finds in himself, let him reprove, repent, beg pardon, confess, and resolve with a whole heart to make satisfaction and amend. Second, let him weigh what it is that he proposes to consecrate and immolate: assuredly the body and blood of the Saviour. So he proposes to offer the whole Christ, God and man. From the consideration, then, of the dignity and reverence of this incomparable sacrament let him tremble wholly in himself, confess his own imperfection and unworthiness, and approach only with immense preparation, deep humility, reverential fear, inward purity and fervent love. Third, let him weigh to whom he offers: namely to God the Father most high, before whom heaven, earth and sea tremble, whom the angelic minds too and all the hosts of Saints hold in awe. Let him not, then, presume to present himself, to stand, to sacrifice before a King and almighty Lord of such infinite excellence, and to offer his only and beloved Son, with a heart unfearing, impure, carnal and cold. Fourth, let him weigh why he offers this sacrament. For the intention of the office of the Mass is twofold. One is the general intention of the Church herself: and so this sacrament is offered first and chiefly for the common good of the whole Church militant, and for the rest, as is contained in the Canon. The other is the celebrant's own intention, who celebrates and sacrifices for various persons and causes: who ought also to have an ardent affection, that he may be heard for all for whom he prays and celebrates. So then he ought to dispose himself for celebration.

Now in the office of celebration itself he ought to bear himself very recollectedly, devoutly and fervently, uttering each word distinctly, weighing the sense wisely and affectionately, sincerely contemplating the dignity of the Sacrament and the uncircumscribable majesty of the eternal Father, so that his prayer may be *directed as incense* in the divine *sight* (Ps. cxl, 2).

But after celebration let him most devoutly give thanks to God, insist on prayers, psalms and holy meditations at least for a time, and the whole day bear himself becomingly, fearfully, guardedly, exemplarily and sincerely, and in this way let the priest's whole manner of life be one continual saving preparation for celebrating; let him never consent to vanity, pleasure or iniquity; nor let empty, unfruitful or unlawful words ever flow from the mouth by which he uttered the most sacred words and received the most venerable Sacrament.

But, alas, there are many priests who, although at the beginning they are somewhat devout, yet as time goes on, the more frequently they celebrate, the less they weigh what they do, and become colder and more lukewarm; and out of a certain dry habit confess without inward contrition, and approach and celebrate without condign preparation, nay without fervour and purity, without reverence and fear. O how great is the impiety of these, how great the shamelessness and folly, how great the ingratitude and blindness! How intolerable too and immense will be their damnation!

But you, dearest priest of Christ, never become such; but the more frequently, so much the more fervently, devoutly and fruitfully confess and celebrate: so that you may daily be more abundantly filled with the graces, virtues and effects of so great a sacrament, and be enlarged in mind with all its spiritual richness, and daily be made more pleasing to the almighty Lord and most bountiful Saviour; nor be broken by the examples of the lukewarm, but be always intent, as far as you are able, on holy occupations, moderate in food, drink, dress, sleep and conversation, exemplary in all words and deeds.

### Article VIII

*What the illuminative way is*

*Come ye to him and be enlightened* (Ps. xxxiii, 6).

Since iniquities alone divide between God and the rational creature (Is. lix, 2), and hold back from it the gifts of the Holy Spirit; therefore the soul cleansed from vices, reformed from the immoderations of the passions, purged of the remains of sins (which are the proneness and strong inclination to sinning), is at once capable of illumination from above, apt to receive the ray of saving wisdom, to be flooded with the brightness of contemplation, to be raised to the contemplation of eternal and divine things. So then, when a man has exercised himself in the purgative way according to the form prescribed (Articles IV and following), he ought at once to enter the illuminative way, and to exercise himself and advance in it.

Now the illuminative way is the study or occupation of the mind about the sublime things of God that are to be contemplated, especially in the contemplation of the most blessed Trinity and of the most simple and most beautiful Deity, then in the consideration of heavenly goods and of the supernatural truths of the Christian faith and of spiritual things. For since in the clear and immediate contemplation of these by sight our whole happiness consists, our advance here is that we should daily draw nearer to that contemplation, so beatific and perfect, by advancing in the knowledge of the highest and uncreated Truth and of divine things, in the illumination of faith, in the gift of wisdom.

Finally, since God, of whose wealth and bounty there is no end, is essentially infinite wisdom and light most splendid, wholly uncircumscribed; when he sees the soul purged in the manner just touched on, formed by humility, patience, meekness, chastity, justice and the other moral virtues too, eager for truth and gaping after the fountain of wisdom, and longing to be enlightened from above, he at once *makes his countenance to shine upon* it (Ps. lxvi, 2), brightens it with the ray of wisdom, showing himself to it more clearly and graciously than usual, and making known to it *the uncertain and hidden things of* his *wisdom* (Ps. l, 8), so that it subtly considers the things of faith, and the reasons of what is to be believed, and the connexion and order of credible things; so that with glad and grateful mind it sings: *I will bless the Lord, who hath given me understanding* (Ps. xv, 7). How desirable and glorious this is, the Prophet confesses, saying: *Blessed is the man whom thou shalt instruct, O Lord: and shalt teach him out of thy law* (Ps. xciii, 12). Hence too St John the apostle writes in his first epistle to faithful so purged: *His unction teacheth you of all things* (I John ii, 27).

### Article IX

*How a man ought to exercise himself in the illuminative way*

*If any of you want wisdom, let him ask of God, who giveth to all men abundantly; and it shall be given him. But let him ask in faith, nothing wavering* (James i, 5, 6).

It is the Philosopher's teaching that the acts of agents are in the patient as it is disposed; and likewise, that it belongs to each thing to act according to the mode of its nature and power; again, that as the sight of the bat is to the light of the sun, so is the human understanding to the beholding of divine things, that is, of immaterial substances, and above all of the most simple and wholly incomprehensible God.

Hence holy Scripture too restrains us from an immoderate, incautious and presumptuous searching and gazing into divine things, saying through Solomon: *As it is not good for a man to eat much honey, so he that is a searcher of majesty, shall be overwhelmed by glory* (Prov. xxv, 27). And in Ecclesiasticus too it is read: *Seek not the things that are too high for thee, and search not into things above thy ability: but the things that God hath commanded thee, think on them always* (Ecclus. iii, 22). Yet there is a certain searching into divine things that is studious, moderate, humble, and leaning on the help of God's grace: which is praiseworthy. Of which the holy man says: *Thy testimonies are wonderful: therefore my soul hath sought them* (Ps. cxviii, 129); and again: *Give me understanding, and I will search thy law* (verse 34); and again: *Blessed are they that search his testimonies* (verse 2). So, as (Scripture testifying) *the light is sweet, and it is delightful for the eyes to see the sun* (Eccles. xi, 7); none the less, for one who fixes his eye immoderately on the sun the sight is dulled, thrown back and hurt: so the highest Truth is sweet, and it is delightful to contemplate the eternal fountain of all light; yet one who presumes to contemplate, search and penetrate immoderately, without fear, irreverently, is the more gravely blinded, cast down, wounded. So then let us strive to contemplate in such a way that we in no wise depart from the truth of Scripture and the doctrine of the Church. The most depraved heresiarchs, not observing this, erred, and sent others into error.

Hence, according to what has gone before, the exercise of the purgative way ought to precede the exercise of the illuminative; and, as our most illustrious father and doctor John Cassian testifies (Conferences xiv, ch. 2), practical and ethical or moral knowledge, which is concerned with the bridling of the passions, ought to go before theoretic, that is, sapiential contemplation of divine things.

One who wishes, then, to exercise himself in the illuminative way, let him begin from humility, so that he presume nothing on his own powers, on natural talent, on acquired knowledge, but let his hope be in the Lord, and let him lean on his help, as is taught through Isaias: *Who hath walked in darkness, and hath no light? let him hope in the name of the Lord, and lean upon his God* (Is. l, 10). Then let him withdraw his mind from outward things, and recollect it, casting off its dispersions and wanderings. And so, according to the teaching of the divine Dionysius (Epistle ix, to Titus), let him turn himself to the ray, longing for the illumination from above and praying for it, and raising himself as far as he can to the consideration or contemplation of God. And then God on high, who is the leader of wisdom and the prince of all illumination, seeing the man's faithful diligence and his devout effort, will apply and stretch out a helping hand, enlightening the mind through the gift of wisdom, as Isaias asserts: *The Lord will give thee rest, and will fill thy soul with brightness* (Is. lviii, 11). For he is the sun of wisdom and understanding: and when he shines and radiates in the soul, all things at once grow clear, comely, tranquil and glad in it; and so it begins to behold the immense majesty of its Creator, his infinite beauty, wisdom, omnipotence, and his beatitude, perfection and eminence wholly without bound, and to wonder at it most vehemently, to gaze on it with delight, to love it fervently, to rejoice inwardly with him, and to rest in it for an hour.

### Article X

*What contemplation is, and of its kinds*

*Behold, God is great, exceeding our knowledge. Therefore men shall fear him, and all that seem to themselves to be wise, shall not dare to behold him* (Job xxxvi, 26; xxxvii, 24).

As blessed Augustine often confesses in the book of the City of God, the understanding is the supreme power of the soul. Therefore beatitude is placed in its act, as was already plain before (in the proemium) from the words of Christ, saying: *This is eternal life: that they may know thee, the true God* (John xvii, 3). Which the Philosopher too proves in the tenth book of the Ethics. Yet happiness does not consist in any act of the understanding whatever, but (as Aristotle says) is placed in the best consideration of the best object of sight. For, by the same witness, beatitude is the best action of the best power on the best object, which is God.

Hence contemplation is rightly defined thus: Contemplation is the affectionate, prompt and sincere knowledge of the highest Deity and of his effects. For first and principally it is concerned with God; secondarily with created things, through their relation to God, that is, as they flow from him as from a fountain, are governed, and are ordered to him as their end: because so God is the end and the reason of the consideration of them, and whatever is considered in them and known of them is referred to the knowledge and love, the glory and honour of the Almighty, who for that reason adorned the fabric of the universe so most beautifully, distinguished it so wisely, ordered it so ineffably, and so wonderfully set and established it upon nothing, and wondrously filled it with such greatness, multitude and power, that in these things his goodness, wisdom and power might shine out, and be honoured, praised, feared and loved. Hence it is written: *The Lord hath made all things for himself* (Prov. xvi, 4). And again Solomon: *God hath made all things, that he may be feared* (Eccles. iii, 14). Another wise man too: *By the greatness of the beauty, and of the creature, the creator of them may be seen, so as to be known thereby* (Wisd. xiii, 5). And Isaias exhorts: *Lift up your eyes on high, and see who hath created these things* (Is. xl, 26).

Finally, this contemplation is the act of the gift of wisdom, which among the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit holds the first place; nor can it be formless, since it is an affective and savoury knowledge of divine things inseparably joined to charity. Nor properly is contemplation the act of any wisdom whatever, but of that which includes a certain perfection, which proceeds promptly and freely into act. Therefore it was said that contemplation is a "prompt" and easy knowledge of divine things, which like fire is borne most swiftly on high, and, the created passed over, reaches God. Therefore Richard says: Contemplation is borne about with wonderful agility in free flight wherever the Spirit carries it. And defining contemplation: Contemplation, he says, is the free penetration of the mind, held in wonder, into the spectacles of wisdom. Hence Hugh of St Victor too defines it thus: Contemplation is the penetrating and free gaze of the soul spread out on every side over the things to be seen. Lastly, contemplation ought to be "sincere knowledge", lest it be fouled by mixture with error, passion or fault, but be strengthened by the certainty and splendour of truth.

Besides, St Bernard sets down four kinds of this contemplation. The first is wonder at the majesty: when, that is, in the contemplation of the divine and uncreated sublimity and in the beholding of the incomprehensibility of the divine substance, the mind itself is intent and held in wonder. The second is the consideration of the judgements of God. In the consideration of which, when a man sees himself fail, he cries out with the Apostle: *O the depth of the riches of the wisdom and of the knowledge of God! How incomprehensible are his judgements!* (Rom. xi, 33). The third is the inspection of the benefits of God. The fourth is the consideration of his promises, which *eye hath not seen, nor ear heard, neither hath it entered into the heart of man* (I Cor. ii, 9).

### Article XI

*Of another assignment and declaration of the kinds of contemplation*

*The Father of glory may give unto you the spirit of wisdom in the knowledge of him* (Ephes. i, 17).

Although the kinds of contemplation are assigned by different authors in different ways, yet the one which I shall now bring in seems more reasonable and more fitting.

The first kind of contemplation, then, is the supernatural contemplation of the adorable and most high Trinity. Whose contemplation I call supernatural, because from natural reason the trinity of persons in God was never plain to men, but first became known from divine revelation. To this kind of contemplation, then, it belongs to behold how in the true and simple God there is an eternal, immaterial, unchangeable, most actual and most pure generation, namely the emanation of the eternal Word from the supreme Father: who, perfectly and eternally knowing and beholding himself, utters and begets in himself the concept and inward Word of himself. For what he knows and beholds in himself and of himself, this he speaks and produces within himself. And because by a single act of understanding he perfectly understands himself and all things, therefore by one inward utterance or mental generation he most fully expresses within himself both himself and all things, and begets one only Word, which is his perfect and natural image, his splendour and Son, and the exemplar, idea and reason of all creatures, and the archetypal form of the whole universe in the divine mind. Of whom Augustine: He who denies, he says, the archetypal world, denies also the Son of God. Hence in this eternal Word the whole nature of the Father shines forth, and his whole majesty and perfection is represented in him: therefore the true deity is contained and consists in him. Hence in the fifteenth book on the Trinity Augustine asserts: The Father, as it were speaking himself, begot a Word equal to himself. Further, to this kind of contemplation it belongs to behold how the Father and the Son, beholding and willing one another, and mutually, incomprehensibly and most sweetly taking delight in one another, produce and breathe within themselves one uncreated, full and infinite love proportioned to their own loveableness, which is the love and bond and kiss of both. For as the word is the emanation of the understanding, so love is the emanation of the will. Again, it belongs to this kind to contemplate how truly most blessed and most delightful the life of this most high Trinity is: how these most venerable, most sublime and most holy Persons behold one another with a beholding wholly comprehensive, most delightful and eternal, love one another too with a love most ardent and immense, take perfect and most joyous delight in one another, and most happily enjoy one another, and how there is in them full communion, most delightful diffusion, most glorious and eternal consistence.

Besides, the causes or reasons of love are knowledge, goodness, likeness, nearness, union, communion, beneficence, and a prior or answering love. And since all these reasons stand most perfectly in the superessential and consubstantial Trinity (for each of those persons knows any other wholly perfectly and comprehensively; each of them too is of immense goodness; and there is between them full likeness and the highest nearness, because the second of them emanates from the first, namely the Son from the Father, and the third from both, namely the Holy Spirit from the Father and the Son; they are also inseparable, because consubstantial; each communicates itself wholly to the other; and the Son received from the Father an infinite good, namely the fullness of deity, as he himself declares, *That which my Father hath given me, is greater than all* (John x, 29), that is, the divine essence, whence he adds in the same place, *I and the Father are one* (verse 30); and this same immense good the Holy Spirit received from the Father and the Son, most truly consubstantial, coeternal and coequal with the Father and the Son; each of them, finally, knows itself to be loved by the other with infinite love), hence it is plain that between them there is a love wholly most burning and immense.

Behold, all these things are to be contemplated humbly and sincerely, quietly and lovingly, with most heartfelt rejoicing at so infinite a beatitude and excellence of the Trinity, exalted above all and adorable.

The second kind of contemplation is the natural consideration of the true and most simple Deity. And I name this natural, not because it is an act of natural and acquired wisdom, or could be had without charity and grace, but because it looks on those things about God which can be known by natural reason, as that God is the first being, eternal, uncaused, independent, perfect, infinite, immaterial, most simple, adorable, the first cause governing all things. Hence the Apostle affirms: *The invisible things of him are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made; his eternal power also, and divinity* (Rom. i, 20). So to this kind it belongs to contemplate how God is pure goodness, everlasting truth, most simple unity, pure act, omnipotent power, exemplary holiness, eternal wisdom, the ideal cause and reason of all things, pure being, subsisting in itself and separate, the principle of all things, the first rule and measure of all rectitude and of all justice, truth and virtue, the being in whom is all nobility, all beauty, all happiness and sweetness, all kindness and justice, the highest charity, full freedom, and every perfection without any measure or end and wholly most simply: so that all these are one and unity in God, namely his most simple and most rich being, so infinitely and incomprehensibly perfect that it comprehends all these in itself most simply and most eminently. To contemplate all these things of God, high and glorious, clearly, lovingly and with rejoicing, belongs to this second kind of contemplation.

The third kind of contemplation is the consideration of the humanity of Christ, namely how it was hypostatically assumed by the eternal Word, and made to subsist in his uncreated personal being; and how at the same instant at which the body of Christ was formed by the Holy Spirit from the most pure blood of the glorious Virgin, his soul too was created and united to the body, and both together assumed by the Word; how likewise at the same moment the soul of Christ was filled and most beautifully adorned with all grace, virtue, gift, fruit and beatitudes, and ineffably graced, filled and exalted above all the Saints with the light of glory and the beatific vision. Moreover, to this kind it belongs to contemplate how Christ was born of the Virgin, circumcised, adored by the Magi, presented in the temple, and all the other things which the gospel history tells of him, namely how he lived in this world in all humility, poverty, patience, meekness and charity, how much he bore, how he redeemed the human race, how many miracles he did, how he rose again, appeared and ascended.

The fourth kind of contemplation is the consideration of the gifts of the grace of God, that is, of his supernatural and gratuitous benefits and gifts: such as the infused virtues, the sacraments of the Church, the various gifts of sanctifying grace and of grace freely given, and the gifts of glory and all that heavenly beatitude.

The fifth kind is the consideration of the gifts of nature, that is, of all natural things. To which kind belongs the consideration of the order of the universe, and of all the separate substances or spirits above, and of the heavenly bodies and the elements and all compounds, above all of man. In all of which the Creator's goodness, wisdom and omnipotence shine forth gloriously, and are shown to be honourable, fearful and lovable.

These I touch only most briefly: for elsewhere, namely in the treatise on Contemplation, it has been written of more fully.

### Article XII

*What the unitive or perfective way is*

*Just Father, I have made known thy name to them, that the love wherewith thou hast loved me, may be in them, and I in them* (John xvii, 25, 26).

In these words Christ intimates that knowledge is referred to love, and without it is formless.

Now the distinction of the threefold way brought in above seems taken from the words of the divine Dionysius. For he, in the book of the Angelic Hierarchy (ch. 3, 10), sets down three hierarchical actions in the holy angels, namely purgation, illumination and perfection. In the book of the Ecclesiastical Hierarchy too (ch. 5) he teaches how one order is purgative, another illuminative, another perfective. Now the angels are said to be purged, not indeed from the uncleanness of fault, but from a lessening of the hierarchical light. They are asserted too to be illuminated and perfected, not that they grow in the beatific contemplation of the divine essence, or advance or are further inflamed in the love of the highest good that follows the beatific vision: for in no way do they obtain increase in the essential reward, but as to that they are wholly at the term. But in the knowledge of the divine effects and in the love that accompanies it they receive increase, and advance in the accidental reward, and this until the consummation of the final judgement. And so is to be understood what in the seventh chapter of the Angelic Hierarchy the most divine and most sacred Dionysius says: The Seraphim, he says, kindle the lower angelic minds, as far as is possible, to the heat of divine love; the Cherubim too abundantly enlighten the heavenly minds below them by communication of the wisdom given them.

But in the ecclesiastical hierarchy purgation, illumination and perfection are found otherwise: since wayfarers need to be cleansed daily from faults, and to advance in the knowledge of God and in his love. And first indeed one must be purged from fault, then illuminated and know the truth, third perfected, that is, kindled with love, and through this united to the God of our salvation. Yet, as in the holy angels the three hierarchical acts just touched on in reality coincide (for by the fact that one illuminates another, he purges him from the lessening of light, and kindles him in love), so in men one of the three ways aforesaid comprehends and includes another in some degree: since the unitive way presupposes the purgative and the illuminative, nor does it happen without illumination that one is united to God, perfected by love, or affected by charity. In like manner the illuminative way in some way comprehends the unitive: since the knowledge of divine things is not properly called either contemplation or illumination unless it is formed and perfected by the affection of love. So too the purgative way has something mixed in of the illuminative and perfective way: since without the direction of true wisdom, charity and grace no one is purged from fault or reforms his passions.

So then the perfective or unitive way is the study, occupation or action tending to the ardour of most sincere charity, to the ecstatic and flaming love of the Deity, to the most burning, most quiet and most secret gaze of mystical wisdom. For this way consists in this, that from the contemplation of God by negation the mind be wholly kindled with divine love, and be made as it were fiery and seraphic, and so, leaving, transcending and forgetting itself and all created things, be united to the super-incomprehensible God, as to one wholly unknown, through the descent of the hierarchical light, through the address of a most hidden inspection, through the super-resplendent darkness and most wise ignorance, as this will be made clear below.

### Article XIII

*Of the twofold contemplation of the Deity; and what mystical theology is*

*Terrible is the Lord, and exceeding great, and* he is *almighty above all his works. Who shall see him, and declare him? and who shall magnify him as he is from the beginning?* (Ecclus. xliii, 31, 30, 35).

As the divine Dionysius says in the first chapter on the Divine Names, we proceed to the knowledge of God from creatures by a twofold way. The first is the way of position or affirmation, when whatever of goodness, perfection and eminence is found in creatures we ascribe to God, as to the first and effective principle of all, most excellently, every imperfection excluded. The second is the way of removal or denial, when whatever is found in created beings we deny of God, as of one super-exalted, superessential and super-incomprehensible, who agrees with created things neither in genus, nor in species, nor in anything univocal, nor does anything belong to him as we are able to comprehend it. Hence in the second chapter of the Mystical Theology St Dionysius: This, he says, is truly to know God, to praise the supersubstantial supersubstantially by the removal of all beings.

Finally, this contemplation of God by negation is more sublime and more perfect than the contemplation of him by position: because in this by negation his infinite excellence and superessential majesty is seen more clearly, more sharply and more deeply than in the other. Therefore in the second chapter on the Angelic Hierarchy the great Dionysius says: Negations in divine things are proper, that is, properly and truly said; but affirmations there are incompact, that is, unfitting, not cohering nor proper. For since every created mind falls infinitely short of the full knowledge of the Deity, the more anyone in this life draws near to God, the more abundantly he is enlightened, the more he is united to and the more sharply fixed on the supreme being that has no cause, eternal and independent, the more clearly he contemplates his incomprehensibility, inaccessibility and infinite super-transcendence. And when in the summit of the mind it has been united, fixed, intent, immersed most nearly and immediately (so far as in this life can be done through a created light and species), it comes to be in an entire ignorance of him as to what he is; but this is for it to see and to contemplate, to behold his incomprehensibility most clearly.

And this contemplation by which God is beheld through the negation of all things is called mystical theology: which is the inward and most hidden speaking of the mind with God, and the loving beholding, through a certain ignorance, of the darkness, that is, of the divine, most splendid, impenetrable and invisible light. Which, as some define it, is an anagogic or upward-leading motion, an elevation or fixing of the mind on God through pure and fervent love. And it is an experiential knowledge of God through the united embrace of love, or a savoury knowledge of the Deity, when the supreme summit of the affective power is united to him through ardent love.

Lastly, this mystical theology or unitive wisdom is in reality the very gift of wisdom, namely that wisdom which among the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit is the first and most worthy: which is a supernatural infused habit, cognitive of God through an inward taste; through which the mind feels and judges well of God through the connaturality and conformity of the affection to him, or through a mental savour. Of which wisdom there is a threefold degree. And it, according to its supreme degree, in which it is present in heroic and perfect men, making them as it were the counsellors and secretaries of the almighty and most wise Creator, is unitive wisdom and mystical theology.

### Article XIV

*That in two ways the soul is led to the vision of mystical theology and the ecstatic love and most ardent beholding of the darkness, or the aforesaid contemplation through the removal of all things*

*I am become as a drunken man, and as a man full of wine, at the presence of the Lord, and at the presence of his holy words* (Jer. xxiii, 9).

Since the loving and mystical contemplation of God just touched on, which also causes rapture and ecstasy, is the most noble and highest, most spiritual and most divine operation of man, there is required for it a special abstraction of the mind from outward things, and its highest recollection into itself, and a most pure simplification of itself in God: so that it be hindered or drawn back from the highest and uncreated object by no disordered affection, no consideration about created things, no distracting occupation.

So then a man reaches this mystical contemplation of unitive wisdom in one way by rousing and disposing himself to it through the abstraction aforesaid, through recollection and simplification, through prayer and meditation, and the consideration of those things which are wont more to provoke and bring forth in the soul the fervour of divine love, until the summit of the will is strongly kindled and purely affected in God, and so the peak of the understanding is enlightened from above to contemplate in the manner touched on. Indeed the divine Dionysius, teaching this manner and its practice in the first chapter of the Mystical Theology: You, he says, dearest Timothy, in the most intent exercise of beholding the mystical spectacles, leave both the senses and the intellectual operations, and all sensible and intelligible things, and all things that are not and that are; and, that you may be joined to him who is above all substance and all knowledge, strain yourself unknowingly, as far as you can. So far he.

In another way a man reaches this contemplation without his own effort and the preparation aforesaid, namely when he is most mercifully prevented from above, and divinely and strongly enlightened, powerfully inflamed, most graciously seized beforehand and too wonderfully bound, so much so that he cannot even resist, and though he wished, could not escape, grace prevailing over nature itself not violently but most fittingly. And the first way is wont to be laborious; but this is most easy. Hence blessed Bernard says: Some are drawn, who can say: *Draw me after thee* (Cant. i, 3). Others are led, who say: *The king hath brought me into his storerooms* (Cant. i, 4). Others are carried off. And the first indeed are happy, who *in* their *patience possess* their *souls* (Luke xxi, 19); the second happier, because of their own will they confess to the Lord (Ps. xxvii, 7); the third happiest, who, the power of choice being now as it were in some way buried in the most deep mercy of God, are carried off into the riches of glory in a spirit of ardour.

These, the very use of things and of the senses despised, are wont at times to fly out through contemplation to the supersubstantial God most high, not by ascending steps but by unlooked-for excesses. One of these was that most devout brother Roger, of the Order of St Francis, who said: It is a very easy thing for a man to be carried off and raised in mind to God. For I know, he said, a man who was carried off several times in one morning; and this man resisted the rapture infinitely often; and sometimes he must use as great force to withdraw from his God and flee, as another uses to draw near. But these things he said of himself. Hence he also testified again: God so communicates himself to my soul that for the excess of sweetness I cannot bear him, but am compelled to say and cry: Lord, withdraw from me, for I cannot bear the multitude of your sweetness. Such was also that holy brother Giles, one of the first companions of the most holy Francis, of whom Bonaventure writes that he saw him carried off so often that he seemed to lead the life of an angel among men; the most blessed abbot Sisois too, of whom it is related in the Lives of the Fathers that when in prayer he stretched out his hands, he at once drew them back, lest he be carried off: otherwise he was carried off at once.

Behold how infinite and admirable is the grace of God in certain of his elect, the vessels of his mercy. Truly, as the Saviour says, *the Spirit breatheth where he will* (John iii, 8); and the uncircumscribable wondrousness and wealth of God is shut in by no bounds, narrowed by no laws, but where and when and how much and in what manner it pleases him, most freely and most mercifully pours itself out. And meanwhile it even offers more, pours in more, than frail man can bear. Which that holy Ephrem, experiencing in himself, was accustomed in his prayers and meditations to cry: Lord, hold back the waves of your grace.

### Article XV

*Of the holy, ardent, divine and deifying love that belongs to the unitive way and to mystical contemplation; and of the rooting out of private love*

*Put me as a seal upon thy heart: for love is strong as death* (Cant. viii, 6).

So great is the love and condescension of the almighty and most kind Creator towards men, that he himself testifies through Solomon: *My delights* are *to be with the children of men* (Prov. viii, 31); and again through Jeremias: *Who is this that shall approach his heart to come near to me?* (Jer. xxx, 21). By which words we are taught how much God, by nature good in every way, desires our salvation, how ready he is to give us the gifts of his graces, how gladly he wishes to be with us, to dwell among us, nay even to dwell in us, provided that we ourselves set no bar, provided that we take care to prepare and apply our hearts to him, by doing what is in us. For hence it is asserted in Job: *If he turn his heart to* God, *he shall draw his spirit and breath unto himself* (Job xxxiv, 14). And Christ: *I stand at the gate, and knock. If any man shall hear my voice, and open to me the door, I will come in to him, and will sup with him, and he with me* (Apoc. iii, 20).

Now by no virtue does a man apply and join his heart to God so directly and intimately, so powerfully, purely and immediately as by charity, which spiritually opens the mind of the lover and makes it capable of the beloved. Since, then, through the most high contemplation of unitive and mystical wisdom the human mind is joined and fixed to God most nearly, as far as can be done in this life, nay is absorbed into him, and is most abundantly flooded, enriched and filled with brightnesses from above, holy ardours and divine gifts; hence the holy Doctors confess that this mystical vision comes about through divine most fervent love, so that the pure and burning love of God disposes immediately and supremely to this unitive and mystical contemplation, being an actual, sincere and fervent affection towards God, which opens the mind to the heavenly Bridegroom and to his influences, flashings, fecundations and embraces.

Besides, this divine love cannot be so pure and fervent unless private love is wholly plucked out. For private love, which is called love of self growing even to contempt of God, makes a man the slave and member of the devil and a citizen of Babylon, that is, of the church of the malignant. But love of God growing even to contempt of self makes a man a citizen of the Saints, a member and soldier of Christ, a friend and son of the Most High. These two loves, of which one is the origin of every sin, and the other the fountain of every good work, are contrary to one another. And the more anyone grows in the one, the more he decreases in the other: so that as private love is the poison of divine love, so the lessening of private love is the advance of divine love, and the sheer plucking out of private love is the perfection of divine love.

Besides, what each of these loves is, I shall touch briefly. Private love, then, is that by which one loves himself in himself, not in the Lord God, that is, willing and desiring for himself those things which are, in themselves and according to his own inclination and affection, delightful, useful or honourable to him. From which love there soon arise the concupiscence of the flesh, avarice, pomp, ambition, and all the defilements of mind and body. But the love of God, which is called charity, is that by which we love God above all things with the whole heart, and ourselves in God, that is, in an ordered way, according to the requirement of our last end, willing and seeking for ourselves those things which truly profit our souls for pleasing our own Creator, for advancing in his charity and grace and in every virtue, for obtaining consummate beatitude. And so from this love proceed glorying in adversities, chastising of the flesh, guarding of the heart, humbling of the mind, contempt of earthly things, desire of eternal things, and every act of the virtues. And since private love is the root of all vices, it is plain that when it is plucked out all sins are uprooted. Which done, there are in the heart inward cleanness and the love of God pure, strong, fixed and fervent. And so the soul is then disposed for every good and for the highest, frequent and almost continual illuminations, for most burning inflamings, for deifying transformation, for rapture and ecstasy, for the most secret exercise, the sweetest tranquillity, nay for a life next to the angels; and it stands as it were in continual contact with the eternal sun, the sun of justice, the sun of infinite wisdom, so as to be most exuberantly and unceasingly enlightened, warmed, penetrated, filled and flooded by it; and like a mirror polished, clear and smooth, luminous and most bright, it is set, laid beneath and applied to the most lucid and immense sun. Therefore that most liberal sun does not cease to pour into it the droppings of grace, the streams of wisdom, the sparks of charity.

Finally, the love of God is accounted pure when we love him not with a view to our own advantage, nor from desire of reward, but for his infinite goodness and loveableness, as he too loved us not for his own profit, he *who hath no need of our goods* (Ps. xv, 2), but that we might be partakers of his goodness and happiness to his glory and honour. Moreover, since to love is to will good to someone, and God most high and holy is unbounded goodness, in every form and simply perfect: we cannot love him more except by willing and favouring with heartfelt affection and rejoicing delight what he has and is, namely his goodness, wisdom, beauty, wealth, omnipotence, beatitude and perfection in every form and immense; then by desiring with our whole heart that he, the Creator and Lord of all, be worshipped, honoured, loved and feared by all in a fitting and due manner, so that we ourselves first of all do this with all our strength, and lead others to it as far as we can.

So then he who is perfect in the love of God in the manner touched on leaves nothing of himself to himself, but spends himself wholly on God, worshipping him with all his power, desiring him with his whole heart, loving him with all fervour, referring all things to his honour, praise and glory, most vehemently desiring the common good, the salvation of all, so that God may be duly honoured in all and by all. Therefore he is inwardly saddened at the injuries and offences done to him, at the ingratitudes, vices and perditions of those who perish; but at the honouring of God, at the reformation of the fallen and the works of the just and the amendment of the erring he rejoices supremely and gives thanks; he intercedes for all, and is wholly turned, melted and transformed into the affection of goodness, into zeal for justice, into the fire of love. Wherefore he is continually moved upwards, and raised to union, to embrace, to mystical vision.

### Article XVI

*Of the mystical, flaming, anagogic and transforming contemplation*

*In his hands he hideth the light, and commandeth it to come again. He sheweth his friend concerning it, that it is his possession, and that he may come up to it* (Job xxxvi, 32, 33).

The incomprehensible, almighty and most free God (who *hath mercy on whom he will* (Rom. ix, 18), and is *merciful to whom it shall please* him (Exod. xxxiii, 19); from whom there is no appeal or escape; whom none can resist (Esther xiii, 9, 11); to whom no one can say: *Why dost thou so?* (Job ix, 12)), enlightens and inflames whom he will, prevents, changes, converts and enriches: who also at times suddenly and most mercifully affects those not yet prepared, turns them wherever he will, and leads them to the grace of illumination from above and of the highest contemplative vision, as is plain in the apostle Paul, whom, raging in the persecution of Christians, he converted on the road, and carried off to contemplate sublime things and to hear secret words (Acts ix, 3 and following; II Cor. xii, 2-4); who also, wishing so to allure the little and imperfect, nay even some still worldly, and to carry them on to higher and more spiritual things, sometimes touches, floods and refreshes them for a little while with the grace of contemplation, with the sweetness of inward consolation. [REVIEW NEEDED: a few words are lost here at the column break of the printed page.]

In these "hands" almighty God "hides the light", because he has and holds in his power both the light of grace and the light of glory, and according to the judgement of his wisdom, the choice of his omnipotence, the purpose of his will, he hides and reveals, holds back and pours out, takes away and gives back the light of grace. Therefore it is said, "and commandeth it to come again": because this light, hidden from the unworthy or withdrawn from the sinner, at God's command comes again and is poured into the penitent, and is increased for the fervent lover, and shown to the one who contemplates. In this return of the light God "sheweth", by an inward address, "his friend", that is, the good man with whom he deigns to work so graciously, that this light [REVIEW NEEDED: the end of this sentence is lost at the column break; the sense, from the verse, is that the light is his possession].

Finally, because this so supernatural elevation of the mind, this so admirable and high contemplation, ennobling and deification of it, is a sign and work of God's infinite condescension and great love towards it; therefore after the words cited above it is at once added: *At this my heart trembleth, and is moved out of its place* (Job xxxvii, 1). For in this most profound contemplation the grace of God, preventing and passing through, binds the sense of the lover, and snatches him from it and fixes him on itself; and, as St Bernard, who had experience of these things, says, carries him off into the day which is away from the tumult of things to the joys of silence, that is, into the most clear splendour of the supreme light, in which he sweetly rests from all the flood of carnal desires, restless fancies and wavering thoughts, falling asleep in the Beloved. And so, according to his measure, for a moment and an instant that very thing is shown him to be seen as it is. For amid these things God makes him into that very thing, so that he is, according to his measure, as that also is. Then for the most part such a contemplator finds himself set in the midst of the kiss of the Father and the Son; so that the mind flows and fails from itself, and, overcome by love and stunned with wonder at the immense majesty and brightness which it contemplates, at the most delightful serenity of the Deity which it sees, knows nothing of itself: for suddenly it is so strongly enlightened and inflamed that, wholly succumbing, it loses its bodily powers and senses. Then the mind, led into the secret of the uncreated light, is plunged into the abyss of infinite light, is carried off and lost in the ocean of eternal happiness, is melted and burned up in the fire of immense love, and in the impassable and immense breadth of a most vast and most level solitude wanders most securely, strays happily, losing itself, not knowing where or how. But in this most tranquil and cherubic beholding the human mind for the most part is not fixed for long; but God draws back the "light" which he poured in a little before, and "hides it in his hands"; and after a little he again "commandeth it to come again" to the mind and to rise, sometimes with greater brightness than before. And when this light shines on the mind, the mind again within grows bright, catches fire, is altered, wonders, is overwhelmed, fails. And then the God of immense loveliness "sheweth concerning it", that is, concerning this light which he hid a little before in his hands, "his friend", that is, the fervent lover, "that it is his possession", because he, despising all carnal and perishing things, has desired nothing besides God and outside him, and therefore, God helping, "he may come up to it", so that, growing in it daily, he may at last stand beatifically intent on the fountain Light, the most sweet God, fruitively united, lovingly and eternally immersed.

Lastly, on this matter John Ruysbroeck wrote deeply enough, according to his experience, in the book on the Perfection of the Sons of God, saying among other things: We shall have to found our life upon the abyss of infinite depth, and so we shall be able to be plunged eternally into love, and to be sunk away from ourselves. And in the same love, lacking all mode, we shall grope and stray from ourselves; and it will lead us and lead us astray into the immense and uncomprehended breadth of the love of God, into which indeed we shall flow, and from ourselves shall flow away into the unknown delights of the divine wealth and goodness, in which we shall melt and melt away, be sunk and sunk deeper into the glory of God. These and many other things he writes: which are to be understood piously and wisely, lest they become to anyone an occasion of error. For clearly they cannot be understood except by those who have experience.

### Article XVII

*On the diverse effects of the heavenly Bridegroom in the contemplative, heroic and loving soul; and on the acts and degrees of most ardent and perfect charity*

*How great is the multitude of thy sweetness, O Lord, which thou hast hidden for them that fear thee!* (Ps. xxx, 20)

As the divine Dionysius teaches, charity is a unitive virtue (*On the Divine Names*, ch. 4). The first act and degree of charity, then, is to join the mind of the lover to God: for it draws even the mind that has turned away and brings it back to him. Hence the apostle John writes in his first epistle: *He that abideth in charity, abideth in God, and God in him* (I John iv, 16). And therefore without charity no virtue properly and simply joins the mind to God.

The second act and degree of charity is to transform the heart of the lover into God: for charity is a conforming and assimilating and penetrating virtue, conforming the will of the lover to the will of the beloved. Nay it in a manner alienates the lover from himself, and transfers and changes him into the beloved. Therefore, as St Dionysius testifies, divine love causes ecstasy, because it does not suffer lovers to be their own, but the property of those they love, into whom as it were set outside themselves they tend, move and are affected. Hence as heat of fire seems to transform iron and coal as it were into fire, so that they put on the property, act and likeness of fire: so charity transforms and transfers the lover into the beloved. Wherefore Augustine asserts that the soul is more truly where it loves than where it gives life. And Christ in the Gospel: *Where thy treasure is* (that is, the thing you love above all), *there is thy heart also* (Matth. vi, 21); and again: *Holy Father, keep them in thy name whom thou hast given me, that they may be one* (John xvii, 11) — beyond doubt through charity, as the Apostle Paul also says: *He who is joined to the Lord, is one spirit* (I Cor. vi, 17). So great a transformation and unity does charity effect! For it is even recorded of the primitive Church, filled with all charity: *The multitude of believers had but one heart and one soul* (Acts iv, 32). For it is the mark of friends to will the same and refuse the same things, as the Philosopher teaches. And Plato and Aristotle have affirmed that a friend is another self. Hence the Bride, transformed into the heavenly Bridegroom through charity, declares in the Canticle: *I to my beloved, and his turning is towards me* (Cant. vii, 10).

The third act and degree of charity is to bind the lover inseparably to the beloved. Hence Isidore said that to love (*diligere*) is as it were to bind two (*duo ligare*). For the more love grows and strengthens, the more powerfully it fastens and binds the lover to the beloved, producing as it were a continual memory and affection for the beloved in the mind of the lover. The force of this charitable affection is so great that no adversity, no prosperity, no event can break, overcome or take it away. Therefore one who loves God thus can say with the Apostle: *Who shall separate us from the charity of God?* (Rom. viii, 35). So that glorious martyr Genesius loved Christ: *There is no king*, he said, *but Christ; for whom if I suffer and am killed a thousand times, you shall never be able to take him from my heart.*

The fourth act and degree of charity is to wound the lover with love of the beloved through the vehemence of affection, according to that word in the Canticle: *I am wounded with charity* (Cant. ii, 5); and again: *Thou hast wounded my heart, my sister, my spouse* (Cant. iv, 9, after the LXX). Surely the lover is wounded in heart, struck, pierced, when that vehement sting of love penetrates his mind to the marrow, so shaking the power of affection in him, so acutely transfixing it, that he can neither restrain, hide nor dissemble the boiling over of his desires, their signs and outward show, but burns with longing, seethes with affection, is hot and panting, sighing most deeply, impatient of separation, and drawing long sighs. This degree of charity admits of interruption for a time, and burns and pricks like a fever, now more intensely, now more remissly, but often returns the stronger. To this degree Augustine had surely come when, confessing to God, he said: *Thou hadst pierced our heart with the arrow of thy love, and we bore thy words fixed in our bowels; our thoughts pierced our inward parts and kindled them, and set all on fire* (*Confessions*, iv, 9).

The fifth act and degree is to make the lover languid with love, according to that word in the Canticle: *Stay me up with flowers, compass me about with apples: because I languish with love* (Cant. ii, 5). From the wounding spoken of above, languor arises when the mind so wounded with love of the Beloved, yet not having his presence at will, is afflicted with a twofold grief and a bipartite longing. For since uncreated Wisdom herself testifies: *They that eat me, shall yet hunger* (Ecclus. xxiv, 29); it is certain that the mind contemplating God in this life, the more clearly it contemplates him, the more vehemently it is inflamed for the face-to-face and beatifying vision of him. And therefore it is saddened at the absence of that vision and the lack of full fruition; and sighing ardently for it, cries out with the Apostle: *Unhappy man that I am! who shall deliver me from the body of this death?* (Rom. vii, 24); and likewise: *I desire to be dissolved and to be with Christ* (Philipp. i, 23); and with the Prophet: *Woe is me, that my sojourning is prolonged!* (Ps. cxix, 5). Again, when the soul has been raised to the actual contemplation of God, clear and delightful according to the possibility of the present life, and is suddenly drawn down from it and dissolved again into its former anguish, distractions, images and temptations: then it is consumed with grief, sighing for its former elevation, which is sometimes withdrawn from it dispensatively by God, now for a shorter, now for a longer time. So this twofold grief and sighing longing is called languor; of which the holy man Jeremias testifies, saying: *The word of the Lord is made a burning fire shut up in my bones, and I was wearied not being able to bear it* (Jer. xx, 9). And another: *My soul longeth and fainteth for the courts of the Lord* (Ps. lxxxii, 3). For the mind, seized and absorbed, introduced and immersed into the riches of glory, into the ocean of the immense Deity, before the wonder of the uncircumscribable majesty and the eternal being wholly independent and needing nothing, of whom there is no cause, in whose hand are all things, flows wholly away and fails from itself, and passes wholly into the Prince of majesty, into the fountain of light, into the abyss of the Deity, resting and sleeping in the Beloved, and attending to nothing else, the beholding of the darkness prevailing in it and the ardour of love wholly pre-occupying it. Which that holy man experienced: *My heart hath been inflamed, and my reins are changed: and I am brought to nothing, and I knew not* (Ps. lxxii, 21, 22). O how good and best it is then for the loving soul! how serene, how joyful, how heavenlike are all things then in it! Where then are the clouds of vices, the storms of passions, the entanglements of phantasms, the variety of distractions, the unrest of temptations? Do not all these flee before the presence of the Sun of justice, before the sight and ardour and brightness of the Sun of wisdom?

The sixth act and degree is to fail and flow away wholly from oneself, so that the lover who is in this state can confess with the Apostle: *I live, now not I; but Christ liveth in me* (Galat. ii, 20). For after languor death commonly follows. So then the most sweet and most dutiful God, seeing the loving soul await him with such grief, seek him with such longing, returns to it with great condescension and admirable liberality, raising it to a clearer contemplation of him than before and a warmer love, so much so that it cannot bear him; as the holy prophet Jeremias testifies: *The word of the Lord is made a burning fire* and so on.

The seventh degree and act is to raise the mind to the theoretical, cloudy and super-splendid vision of mystical theology, of which much has already been said above (Articles XII–XIV). And that vision holds a middle place between ordinary contemplation and the beatifying vision of heaven.

The eighth degree and act is to lead the mind, now so fervent, after the sojourning of this exile, to the face-to-face and most blessed inspection of the first truth, to the vision of the divine essence, to the most full, supremely satisfying and quieting fruition of infinite goodness. Which done, desire is filled and rests, the capacity of the mind is satisfied, and all the potentiality of the soul is reckoned to have been brought to act in respect of its final and chief object. Wherefore it is written: *I shall be satisfied when thy glory shall appear* (Ps. xvi, 15); and *Thou shalt fill me with joy with thy countenance* (Ps. xv, 11).

### Article XVIII

*Continuation of the same matter; and how marvellously and graciously the heavenly Bridegroom acts in the inflamed, sanctified and deified soul*

*I have loved wisdom, and have sought her out from my youth, and have desired to take her for my spouse, and I became a lover of her form, knowing that she will communicate herself to me of her goods, and will be a comfort in my cares. With her I shall have rest: for her conversation hath no bitterness, nor her company any tediousness, but joy and gladness.* (Wisd. viii, 2, 9, 16)

So then the heavenly Bridegroom will visit most sweetly, embrace with familiarity, speak lovingly with, and communicate of his goods to, that soul which can truly say with the Apostle: *God forbid that I should glory, save in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ, by whom the world is crucified to me, and I to the world* (Galat. vi, 14) — so that whatever the world esteems great and sweet and fitting, it counts a cross, and whatever it thinks vilest, it regards as such and looks upon as nothing. The Bridegroom and the bride embrace one another mutually, love, kiss and hold close, despising adulterous love.

So then uncreated Wisdom, which is the eternal God, is rightly called the spouse of the created spirit and fervent lover: since such a lover loves her most sincerely and uniquely, holds her most ardently and intimately, embraces her above all else, and utterly spurns alien and impure love. Again, God who is this Wisdom is called the Bridegroom of the soul which is the rational spirit; and the loving soul is the bride, embraced by God through charity, kissed most sweetly by him through most fervent union, fecundated by the light of his grace, and commanded and required to remain faithful, whole and inviolate to him. This is done while she loves nothing besides God and outside him.

O noble and chosen soul, turned so fully to your Creator, *the King shall greatly desire thy beauty* (Ps. xliv, 12), and will say to you: *Shew me thy face, let thy voice sound in my ears* (Cant. ii, 14). And his delight will be to be with you and to dwell in the midst of your heart. Then truly *thou shalt see and shalt abound, and thy heart shall wonder* (Is. lx, 5) at so great a love and condescension of your God towards you, and shall dilate in love for him with joy. And you shall happily taste *how great is the multitude of his sweetness which he hath wrought for them that hope in him; which he hath hidden from those that stand in his sight, that he doth for them that hope in him* (Ps. xxx, 20) — who refuse to be comforted from without, who seek quiet and solitude; whose minds God the glorious, most sweet and most bountiful Bridegroom, will fill, console, tranquil, illumine with brightness, fervently kindle, sweetly anoint and stably possess in a manner unspeakable; nay sometimes and perhaps frequently will most lovingly and most mercifully wholly prevent, drawing our spirit and breath to himself, unexpectedly raising and without our effort suddenly lifting, changing, kindling and absorbing us.

Besides, since love enters where knowledge remains without, while the mind is intent on mystical contemplation, the love being inflamed and as it were moved and stirred, begins in a way to boil and seethe, and with its holy violence to shake the whole body. Now what seethes is by a kind of violence of its heat and desire cast outside itself and lifted above itself, as though it disdained to be where it so vehemently bestirs itself. So too the mind transfixed with fervour of love strives as it were to fly outside the body.

Moreover, because love of this kind causes the mind to be attentively and for long, if not continually yet most frequently, intent on contemplation of God and love for him, remembering him innumerable times not in divine things only but in every place, act and time, on the bed, at table, in the house, on the road, at rest and at work; so that such a man at the letter fulfils that word of Deuteronomy: *The words of the Lord shall be in thy heart, and thou shalt meditate on them sitting in thy house, and walking on thy journey, sleeping and rising* (Deut. vi, 6–7); this holy and ardent love makes the mind inwardly meditate on the things that are of God, and run through all things diligently; now the incomprehensible goodness and mercy of God, now his infinite wisdom, now his wealth, now his omnipotence, his beauty, justice, beatitude, eternity and other properties, sincerely contemplating, vividly wondering, incomparably loving, embracing, praising and honouring them.

Furthermore, from the supernatural and marvellous workings of the heavenly Bridegroom and of charity in the soul, a most certain argument is drawn of the truth of the Christian faith: so that whoever experiences these things, is so certified in faith by them that, though the whole world were to turn away from the faith, he could not doubt the truth of faith — as I have argued more fully in the treatise on Contemplation and elsewhere.

### Article XIX

*On the effects and signs of ecstatic love*

*Turn away thy eyes from me, for they have made me flee away.* (Cant. vi, 4)

God, sublime and blessed, by the actuality and knowableness of his eternal truth, and the loveableness of his immense goodness, infinitely transcends and surpasses every created understanding and affection: therefore he wholly alienates from herself the soul of the one contemplating him, seizes, absorbs and makes her fail from herself. Wherefore the Psalmist says: *My soul hath fainted after thy salvation* (Ps. cxviii, 81); and again: *My flesh and my heart hath fainted away* (Ps. lxxii, 26). Now spiritual things are declared by bodily things: therefore the properties, acts and signs of spiritual ecstatic love are to be made clear through the acts and signs of carnal love, which is called *eros* or predominating or ecstatic love.

So then the first sign of carnal ecstatic love is the desiccation of the members. For those so in love dry up in their members, grow pale and waste: because from the most intense application of the heart to the beloved, the spirits are multiplied and the natural heat is weakened in its working, and so they incur a desiccation of the body. In like manner, from vehement and inward spiritual ecstatic love and its occupation and engagement with the Beloved, namely God most eminently lovable, the heart dries up from every inflow of carnal pleasure, from concupiscence and every earthly affection — that is, from the movements of carnal humours — the Lord drying the waters of the Red Sea by a strong and burning wind (Jos. iv, 23; Exod. xiv, 21), that is, the Holy Spirit's power working inwardly with grace and power. For the more a soul occupies itself with spiritual and divine things, the more it is drawn away from carnal things and holds them in contempt. This Augustine experienced in himself, saying: *O my God, how suddenly did I loathe all that I had before wickedly loved!* Hence the Bride prays in the Canticle: *Arise, O north wind, and come, O south wind, blow through my garden, and let the aromatical spices thereof flow* (Cant. iv, 16). By the north wind, which is hard and cold, is understood the stupor and heaviness of vices. By the south wind, which is sweet and warm, the Holy Spirit is understood, who infusing himself, the spiritual desires flow from her, the carnal affections driven out, as Gregory says: *Having tasted the Spirit, all flesh grows tasteless.*

The second effect and sign of carnal ecstatic love is the hollowness of the eyes. For those in love have their eyes hollowed and deep, because the eyes follow the spirits, withdrawing to within and running to the organ of the estimative power. So the interior eyes of the soul inflamed with spiritual ecstatic love are deepened in the heart, and such a soul is inward-turned: because within is, within shines and is found all that she seeks, loves, takes delight in, rests in — namely God, the true light, the highest truth, the infinite goodness. To whom she says with Isaias: *Verily thou art a hidden God* (Is. xlv, 15); and again: *My secret to myself* (Is. xxiv, 16); and with the Psalmist: *God of my heart, and the God that is my portion for ever* (Ps. lxxii, 26). Moreover, such a soul deepens the gaze of its inner sight to consider and search whether there be anything in it to offend the heavenly Bridegroom who alone sees the heart, according to that word: *I meditated with my heart in the night, and I was exercised and I swept my spirit* (Ps. lxxvi, 7). Again it deepens its mental sight to observe and know as far as it can the hidden comings and goings of the heavenly Bridegroom. Of which it is written in Job: *If he come to me, I shall not see him; if he depart, I shall not understand it* (Job ix, 11). For although certain such comings and goings cannot be certainly known or observed, they can be known with probability. Nor is there any doubt that such a lover also guards the exterior senses, whose lack of custody is a sign of an uncustodied mind. Hence Solomon testifies: *The eyes of a wise man are in his head, but the eyes of a fool are in the ends of the earth* (Eccles. i, 14; Prov. xvii, 24).

The third sign of carnal ecstatic love is the dryness of the eyes or the absence of tears, unless weeping comes from the side of the beloved. Hence neither the loss of goods nor the death of relatives can draw tears from those in love in this way. In like manner the soul spiritually and ecstatically in love is not saddened for any losses that do not pertain to the most beloved Lord above, as Solomon speaks: *Nothing that befalleth the just man shall make him sad* (Prov. xii, 21). For he so seeks and loves and esteems the essential Good that he sets little store by other things. Hence the holy man Job, enduring most grievous trials and suffering adversity with greatest patience, said: *The Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away: as it hath pleased the Lord so is it done: blessed be the name of the Lord* (Job i, 21). So too of Tobias it is written: *Tobias was not sorrowful against God because the plague of blindness had befallen him, but he continued immovable in the fear of God, giving thanks to God all the days of his life* (Tob. i, 13, 14). Let us not therefore be saddened for base things, let us not be moved for small and passing ones. *The just man shall be as an everlasting foundation* (Prov. x, 25).

The fourth sign of carnal ecstatic love is a deep concern and thought turned to what is within — not indeed to spiritual or divine things, but rather to vain and base phantasms enclosed within, in which the mind beholds the thing loved and imagines carnal things: so that one in love this way seems to wear the image of one asleep, scarcely able to wake to outward things, except when something is said about the beloved, because all his thought, intention and affection are fixed on the beloved. In like manner the soul spiritually and ecstatically in love is so concerned and intent about the most secret God that it closes its sense to worldly, empty, impertinent words, to vain rumours and tales, and hears such things unwillingly, saying that prophetic word to God: *The wicked have told me fables; but not as thy law* (Ps. cxviii, 85). But if the subject of speech is Christ and things pertaining to salvation, it at once wakes and attends, according to that word: *He that is of God heareth the words of God* (John vii, 47).

The fifth effect and sign of carnal ecstatic love is an inordinate pulse. For those in love have either too slow or very quick a pulse, according to the different apprehensions and affections they conceive about the beloved. For when they despair of obtaining him, they presently have a slow pulse; but quick, when they are confident of receiving the return of his love. So the soul spiritually and ecstatically in love has its pulse — that is, its affections — wonderfully quick and swift, when it is confident of obtaining its Bridegroom, when it experiences being visited by him, filled, consoled, embraced, when it rejoices at his gracious presence, or is in some measure assured of having the beatific fruition of him, as the Apostle says: *The Spirit himself giveth testimony to our spirit, that we are the sons of God* (Rom. viii, 16). Such an inward pulse had the holy man who said: *My heart and my flesh have rejoiced in the living God* (Ps. lxxxii, 3); and again: *I have run the way of thy commandments, when thou didst enlarge my heart* (Ps. cxviii, 32). But when the soul falls into spiritual dryness and is deprived of its usual alacrity and consolation, when it is gravely anxious about obtaining beatitude or fears to be among the reprobate, it presently has a slow pulse, saying with the holy man Job to the Lord: *I shall cry to thee, and thou wilt not hear me: I stood, and thou didst not look at me. Thou art changed to be cruel to me, and in the hardness of thy hand thou art against me* (Job xxx, 20, 21).

The sixth work and sign of carnal ecstatic love is that one so in love, on seeing something resembling the thing loved, is inflamed, shaken, looks at it, and seems as it were to be mad. So the soul spiritually and ecstatically in love, unable in this life to see and taste God in himself, when it contemplates and tastes his goodness, sweetness, beauty and excellence in his effects, and in a certain foretaste through the created genus and species, is straightway alienated, shaken, altered, so as to seem almost beside itself; nay is judged mad and foolish by the inexperienced and rash; while the soul, tending upward and leaving its body, the body inclines or is moved in various ways and threatens to fall.

The seventh sign of carnal ecstatic love is defective and truncated speech, and little conversation with the beloved when present. For those in love in absence conceive much; when they meet, they utter little, and that imperfectly: since the greatness of affection breaks off words and absorbs the mind, rushing to embrace and kisses. So the soul spiritually and ecstatically in love uses defective and truncated speech in speaking with the Bridegroom, as when it says: *My beloved to me, and I to him* (Cant. ii, 16). In which words reason must supply and explain the language of love, as eloquent Aaron was the mouth of the inarticulate Moses. So when the Bride says, *My beloved to me*, it is to be understood: *he shows himself graciously and pleases me above all things, and is most dear*. And *I to him*, supply: *I am intent on him, I desire to please him, I yearn to be united to him.*

### Article XX

*That much of what the doctors have written scattered about is reduced to the aforesaid threefold way*

*How great are thy works, O Lord! Thou hast made all things in wisdom.* (Ps. ciii, 24)

As Aristotle says in the first book on the Heavens and Earth: *By this number* (namely the ternary) *let us magnify the one God eminent above the properties of things created.* He said this because in all created things a certain perfection appears in the ternary, as in beginning, middle and end. Therefore the ancients honoured God with prayers and sacrifices trebled, as the Commentator expounds there, and Thomas cites on the first of the *Sentences* (dist. 3, q. 1, a. 4). And so this ternary number imports a certain perfection and excellence, is found in all created things, and in it consists the perfection of creatures. Hence the footprint of the most blessed Trinity is said to shine in all things.

In the rational creature in like manner almost all things are reduced to a certain threefold: as also the ways and exercises and means by which God is reached are reduced to the purgative, illuminative and perfective or unitive way. For to the purgative belongs the state of beginners, to the illuminative the state of those who advance, to the unitive the state of the perfect. Again, to the purgative belongs the animal state, to the illuminative the rational, to the perfective the spiritual. Likewise to the purgative belongs the beginning of the reformation of reason, will and memory; to the illuminative the progress of that reformation; to the unitive its perfection.

Moreover, since truth is threefold — namely truth of life, truth of doctrine, and truth of justice — to the purgative belongs truth of life, to the illuminative truth of doctrine, to the perfective truth of justice. Furthermore, to the purgative belong the political virtues, to the illuminative the purgatorial virtues, to the unitive the virtues of the purged mind. Finally, to the purgative belong faithful servants, to the illuminative secret friends, to the perfective hidden sons. One may also say that to the purgative pertain the moral virtues, especially those that look to the sensitive appetite and concern the restraint of the passions; to the illuminative the intellectual virtues; to the unitive the theological virtues, especially in their perfected state, and above all charity.

### Article XXI

*Declaration of the foregoing; the distinction between servants, hirelings, secret friends and hidden sons*

*I will not now call you servants, but friends: because all things whatsoever I have heard of my Father, I have made known to you.* (John xv, 15)

These are the words of Christ, by which he distinguishes between servants and friends, though all who are good and virtuous are his servants. The abbot Cassian puts hirelings before servants. But that most divine John Ruysbroeck places servants before hirelings. The same John again places hidden sons before friends. But the Chancellor of Paris (Gerson) preferred friends to sons. We must therefore consider how these are to be distinguished, understood and reconciled.

Now, according to Cassian (*Conferences*, xi, ch. 7), there are three incentives or motives for avoiding sins and doing good. The first is fear of punishment, namely of eternal damnation or of temporal harm and torment. This is pure servile fear, which charity excludes, as the apostle John writes in his first epistle: *Fear is not in charity: but perfect charity casteth out fear, because fear hath pain* (I John iv, 18). For pure servile fear and charity cannot exist at all together: since one who fears only servilely does not love the good of virtue, nor forsakes vice and does good out of regard and love for God, but from private love of himself, lest he suffer what he dreads and what burdens him. From this mutual, base and reflexive love, servile fear wells up, since to desire and love one's own good and to flee and fear the contrary evil proceed from the same root. And therefore one who fears only servilely loves himself more than God, and sets his end not in God but in a created and perishable good: hence he is in mortal fault and a state of damnation. Hence the great doctors — Alexander of Hales and William of Auxerre — assert in their *Summae* that one could suffer martyrdom for the faith and yet be damned, namely if he bore it from servile fear alone. Nor is this surprising, since the Apostle himself confesses: *If I should deliver my body to be burned* (that is, be burned with fire for the faith), *and have not charity, it profiteth me nothing* (I Cor. xiii, 3). For although servile fear avoids fault and does what is good by its nature, yet none of these acts it makes well and as is fitting — with right intention and from a good and living root. There is, however, another fear, the initial, by which one avoids sin and does good partly from fear of punishment and partly from love of God and reward. This fear is good, though imperfect. The third is filial fear, which is the flight from offending God, avoiding fault and doing good lest God be offended and forsake the man: and thus this fear proceeds from charity.

The second incentive to those who avoid evil and do good is love of God, desire for the good of honour, and zeal for justice. The third is hope or desire of reward.

Now those who flee from fault and do good from servile fear Cassian calls servants — from servitude, namely of servile fear. In this sense the Saviour says: *A servant abideth not in the house for ever; but the son abideth for ever* (John viii, 35). Cassian names hirelings those who serve God with a view to reward and in hope of wages — not, however, from that alone, but also at the same time from love of God and zeal for justice, as he says there. But those who minister to God from pure love Cassian calls friends and sons: between whom he makes no distinction, as neither does holy Scripture.

Now John Ruysbroeck had another consideration. For he called servants those who observe the divine commandments from charity, declining mortal sins and exercising the works of the active life — namely acts of mercy — yet not giving themselves to the savour of the contemplative life and the observance of the evangelical counsels. And these are servants by the servitude of initial and partly filial fear. In this sense Christ speaks in the Gospel: *Well done, good and faithful servant, enter thou into the joy of thy lord* (Matth. xxv, 21, 23). And even perfect men and Saints in glory are called servants of God by the servitude of filial and chaste fear, which perseveres even in glory. Hence in the Apocalypse it is read: *The servants of God shall serve him, and they shall see his face, and they shall reign for ever and ever* (Apoc. xxii, 3–5). Nay Christ himself as man is called the servant of God in the Scriptures. Of whom God the Father testifies through Isaias: *Behold my servant, I will uphold him; my elect, my soul delighteth in him* (Is. xli, 1); and again: *By his knowledge shall this my just servant justify many, and he shall bear their iniquities* (Is. liii, 11).

Ruysbroeck calls hirelings those who minister to God chiefly with a view to reward and from desire of wages. These are therefore not in charity and grace: since to obey God chiefly from charity and love of the good and honour of God is necessary, so that God is the end, not the one acting or his own good.

Finally, the same Ruysbroeck calls friends those who not only observe the commandments from charity and exercise works of piety, but also give themselves to contemplation, cleaving to the Lord by inward and loving adhesion, extending themselves to spiritual savour, and observing also the evangelical counsels. Yet they are not yet made fully naked, stripped of all propriety (that is, of turning to themselves and to spiritual gifts): because they aspire and are more affected toward inward consolation, sensible devotion and sweet adhesion than perfection admits. Hence when such things are present with them, they are alert; but pusillanimous when such things are absent. They are therefore not yet perfectly dead to themselves, nor turned wholly and entirely to God.

Hidden sons of God he calls those who are dead to all the foregoing and stripped of all propriety toward self and spiritual gifts, and most fully turned to God, looking at him alone — not at his gifts — seeking, weighing him, except in so far as by them they can honour and attain God, which they can in no other way. Hence these most please God and are visited, filled, seized and supremely beatified by him; and whether they experience sensible devotion and inward consolation or not, they accept it with equal mind, cleave to God, desiring nothing but his glory, honour, good pleasure and union and most sincere love.

### Article XXII

*On the threefold degree of inner reformation*

*Be renewed in the spirit of your mind, and put on the new man, who according to God is created.* (Ephes. iv, 23, 24)

Since in the three supreme powers of the soul — intellect, memory and will — the image of the highest and uncreated Trinity is to be seen in the rational soul, something brief must be said of the reform of these powers.

The beginning of the reformation of the intellect or reason, then, is to be purged of errors and ignorance contrary to salvation by the truth of faith, the doctrine of holy Scripture and the teachings of the Church, and to obtain the knowledge of things necessary to salvation. The advance of this reformation is to see by the gift of understanding the reasons of credible things, to be instructed from above in the Scriptures, and to take increase in the saving knowledge of the highest truth; as the Apostle says to the Colossians: *We pray that you may be filled with the knowledge of his will, in all wisdom and spiritual understanding, that you may walk worthy of God, growing in the knowledge of God* (Coloss. i, 9, 10). For although the Christian faith is utterly above reason and concerns incomprehensible things, yet the mind, sincerely illuminated by God, seeing the connexion, reasons and order of what is to be believed, perceives that nothing is more reasonable than the Catholic faith when all things are considered. The perfection of the reformation of reason is to be caught up above itself by an excess of mind, and to contemplate the Most High not by arguments and reasonings nor by the enigmas of bodily likenesses, but by a pure and anagogic understanding, according to that word of the Apostle: *But we all beholding the glory of the Lord with open face, are transformed into the same image from glory to glory* (II Cor. iii, 18).

Further, the beginning of the reformation of the will is to resist vices and subject it to the righteousness of the divine commandments, and not to be subject to the passions but to prevail over them. The advance of this reformation is to have the sensitive affections subject to the will, and all the desires of the will gathered together and ordered in God. The perfection of this reformation is to be one with God in spirit through love, so that one can now scarcely or not at all will anything except God and what is pleasing to him, and is unturnable from him. This belongs chiefly to those confirmed in good, and in some degree befits perfect men, who can say with the Apostle: *I am sure that neither death, nor life, nor any creature* and so on (Rom. viii, 38, 39).

Moreover, the beginning of the reformation of the intellectual memory is to draw it back from its wanderings to the remembrance of God by praying, reading and meditating. The advance of the reformation of the memory is to keep the mind fixed on God without troublesome wandering in those very prayers, readings and meditations, and to walk abroad in the breadth of the heart with God. The perfection of the reformation of the memory is to be so absorbed in God that one forgets himself and all other things, and rests sweetly in God without the noise of rolling thoughts and images.

Behold, to these all our good exercises are to be ordered and powerfully prosecuted.

### Article XXIII

*On truth and the manifold distinctions of truth; especially on the threefold truth belonging to the threefold way set out above*

*God is true; and every man a liar.* (Rom. iii, 4)

Since all perfection is in God, how much more truth? Again, being and true are convertible, as the Philosopher says: *As a thing stands in respect of being, so does it in respect of truth.* God therefore is true and truth itself. First, because he has being most stable, perfect, unchangeable, uniform and solid. Wherefore St Jerome says: *God alone truly is, compared to whose essence our being is nothing.* And Avicenna in the eighth book of his Metaphysics affirms: *Whatever is necessary being, is truth.* Second, because the divine being is pure and unmixed, having nothing of non-being, nothing of vanity or falsehood mingled: which belongs to no created being. Third, because God shows through his essence what he is. Fourth, because through it he is conformed to his own understanding. Fifth, because he is the fountain-cause of all created truth.

Moreover in the divine understanding there is truth, because it understands the divine essence and all other things as they are, and is the author, measure and rule of all created truth. To created things truth belongs by comparison with the divine understanding, in so far as they conform to the ideal reason they have in the eternal mind, and take on the nature which God the Creator gave and granted to them. Hence Richard of Middleton testifies on the first of the *Sentences* (dist. 8) that truth consists in the conformity of a thing with its exemplar.

There are therefore many divisions of truth. The first, that one is created truth, another uncreated. The second, that uncreated truth is held to be twofold: the truth of the divine essence and the truth of the divine understanding — which two truths are really the same, distinct by reason alone. Moreover, although truth belongs to God absolutely and is common to the three most blessed Persons, nay all three are one truth as they are one essence; none the less truth is appropriated to the Son, as charity to the Holy Spirit, because the Son is the emanation of the understanding, to which truth belongs, as the Holy Spirit is the emanation of the will, to which charity belongs. Hence art, truth, wisdom, ideal reason, exemplar, beauty, species are attributed to the Son. Accordingly, uncreated truth is further said to be twofold — as is uncreated wisdom — namely: generated truth, which is the Son; and ingenerate truth, which is the divine essence.

Furthermore, there is a threefold division of truth: the truth of life, of doctrine and of justice; of which something is now to be touched.

Truth of life is life or conduct or vital action regulated according to the righteousness of the divine law, and exhibited and directed according to the tenor of the divine commandments. For the truth of life consists in this, that the rational creature conforms its actions to the judgement of the divine mind, acting according to the assessment and commands of the eternal mind's rule, doing what God approves and avoiding and repudiating what he repudiates. Of which truth the Saviour says: *He that doth truth, cometh to the light, that his works may be made manifest, because they are done in God* (John iii, 21); and the Apostle: *Charge the rich of this world not to be highminded, nor to trust in the uncertainty of riches, but to be rich in good works, to lay up in store for themselves a good foundation against the time to come, that they may lay hold on the true life* (I Tim. vi, 17–19). God too speaks through Isaias: *I will give their works in truth* (Is. lxi, 8). This truth excludes fiction and simulation, so that a man is sincere in his life, not a pretender and dissembler.

Truth of doctrine is the doctrine itself conformed to the divine and uncreated truth and wisdom — that is, free from error. Of which the Apostle writes: *Though we, or an angel from heaven, preach a gospel to you besides that which you have received, let him be anathema* (Galat. i, 8).

Truth of justice is said in two ways: first, as justice itself is a certain righteousness measured according to the rule of divine and uncreated justice; second, as one out of zeal for justice confesses truth, as happens in a court of judgement. Hence some say thus: Truth consists in this, that a man in his words and deeds conforms himself to the rectitude of the divine law and wisdom, and imitates her, to whom man ought to conform himself: first in those things that pertain to knowledge, which belongs to truth of doctrine; second in those things he must do of himself, which pertains to truth of life; third in those things he must observe toward others or promulgate as to be observed, which certainly pertains to truth of justice.

Of this threefold truth the adversaries of truth said to Christ: *Master, we know that thou art true* (Matt. xxii, 16) — behold truth of life — *and thou teachest the way of God in truth* — behold truth of doctrine — *and thou carest not for any man* — behold truth of justice.

### Article XXIV

*That God is the primary, most actual, most wealthy and most blessed life; and on the manifold distinction of life, especially on the threefold life*

*With thee is the fountain of life; and in thy light we shall see light.* (Ps. xxxv, 10)

Since the divine being as a certain sea contains and includes all the fullness, nobility and excellence of being, it is certain that the being of God is living and intellectual being. And since God is truly and to the very end of all simplicity most utterly simple — in whom, as is proved in the *Book of Causes*, there is no composition of any modes — it is clear that he is of himself and essentially living, and is the most actual life itself: nay in him being, living, understanding and loving are absolutely one. Moreover, since the most glorious God, as Aristotle demonstrates in the twelfth book of the First Philosophy, is pure act, which it belongs to move, transmute and perfect all other things, and to be moved, changed and perfected by none; it is plain that the fontal fullness of living and the most wealthy possession of life belongs to him. Finally, in created things those are said to live that move themselves to act. Since therefore the most excellent God moves himself most freely, according to the judgement of his wisdom and the exemplary and ideal reason of his work, to every operation that he effects in creatures, it is most clear that he is most truly affirmed to live, especially since he is, according to the philosophers, the understanding universally active, in whom all things intellectually and ideally consist, as John too asserts: *What was made* (every created essence certainly), *in him was life* (John i, 3, 4), that is, it was vitally and exemplarily and wisely in God. Hence the only-begotten Son of God himself testifies in John: *As the Father hath life in himself, so he hath given to the Son also to have life in himself* (John v, 26). And the prince of the Apostles said to the Saviour: *Thou art Christ, the Son of the living God* (Matth. xvi, 16).

Most wealthy therefore and most noble and most delightful is the life of the most high being, God sublime and blessed, who is the first truth itself, infinite wisdom, most free will, everlasting charity, most holy mind, incomprehensibly and uncircumscribably perfect. So most blessed is his life, in which every perfection consists so infinitely and most simply, since happiness is called the state perfected by the gathering of all goods.

And instead of the false and earthly happiness sought in riches, power, pleasure, fame, praise and honour, he has for riches the most immense perfection and wealth in himself and most dominating possession of all things; for power, omnipotence; for pleasure, infinite joy in himself, and delight in his effects; for fame, the wonder of all intellectual creatures; for praise, the perpetual glorifying of the Church triumphant and militant; for honour, the worship of *latria* from all the company of the devout.

Moreover, from the aforesaid most actual life of God flows all created life: the life of nature, the life of grace, and the life of glory. The life of nature is divided into the vegetative, the sensitive, the locomotive, and the rational or discursive and intellectual. The life of grace is any virtuous action proceeding from grace, or a good manner of living, or the very habit of sanctifying grace — which makes its possessor pleasing, acceptable and spiritually alive to God — or any virtue formed by charity, or charity itself, which is called the life of the soul. Finally, the life of glory is eternal beatitude, or the light of glory disposing the minds of the Blessed for the beatifying vision of God, or any action of the citizens of heaven rejoicing and glorying.

### Article XXV

*On the good industries by which one advances in virtue and arrives at the perfection and grace of contemplation*

*Thou shalt find him, when thou shalt seek the Lord thy God; yet so if thou seek him with all thy heart, and all the affliction of thy soul.* (Deut. iv, 29)

As the holy Gregory says, the art of arts is the governing of souls. So likewise the science of sciences can truly be said to be: to serve God, to grow in the virtues, to prevail over temptations, not to fail or grow lukewarm in the good one has begun, but to persevere in it worthily and stoutly, and so to reach the perfection and prize of the heavenly calling (Philipp. iii, 14). To accomplish all this we need many labours, many good industries, great diligence, great discretion. For although God, who is most free, to whom it is permitted to do whatever he will, most mercifully prevents, touches and converts some even while they are intent on their wickednesses; yet speaking regularly and generally, a man must prepare himself for the gifts of God's grace by doing what is in him. Hence in Zacharias it is read: *Turn ye to me, and I will turn to you, saith the Lord* (Zach. i, 3). And the apostle James: *Draw nigh to God, and he will draw nigh to you* (James iv, 8).

There are now some things briefly to be touched on that are accommodated to making progress and sufficiently necessary.

The first is frequent and intent prayer. Since all our sufficiency is from God, without whom we can do nothing good, we must invoke God's help continually and fervently, saying with the Psalmist: *O God, come to my assistance; O Lord, make haste to help me* (Ps. lxix, 2), or similar words. Hence in the Gospel the Saviour says: *It is necessary to pray always, and not to faint* (Luke xviii, 1). And to this also pertains to weigh one's own imperfection, frailty and defectiveness, to presume nothing of oneself, and to place all one's hope in God, who speaks through Osee: *Thy destruction is from thee; in me only is thy help* (Osee xi, 9).

The second is to examine one's own life vigilantly every day, to accuse oneself sincerely before God, to humble oneself deeply, to ask pardon, and to propose amendment. For as it is found in Ecclesiasticus: *The prayer of him that humbleth himself shall pierce the clouds* (Ecclus. xxxv, 21). And the Psalmist, speaking of God: *He hath had regard to the prayer of the humble, and he hath not despised their petition* (Ps. ci, 18); and likewise: *A sacrifice to God is an afflicted spirit* (Ps. l, 19).

The third is to ponder wisely the presence of the omnipotent God who beholds all things, and so to be covered with shame before God at the foulness of sin. Hence the Prophet said to the Lord: *I have kept thy commandments and thy testimonies, because all my ways are in thy sight* (Ps. cxvii, 168). And as Boethius says: *A great necessity of acting well is laid upon us, since before the eyes of a Judge who beholds all things we do everything* (*Consolation*, bk. I, prose 6).

The fourth is to consider deeply the rigour of the divine judgement and the bitterness of the infernal fire, the immensity too of the happiness of the just, and the shortness and uncertainty of the present life, the horror of death, and the eternity of the world to come. Wherefore it is written: *Remember thy last end, and thou shalt never sin* (Ecclus. vii, 40).

The fifth is to avoid occasions of sin, especially evil company, since Scripture says: *With the perverse thou wilt be perverted* (Ps. xvii, 27); and *He that toucheth pitch shall be defiled with it* (Ecclus. xiii, 1).

The sixth is frequently to recall the Passion of the Lord according to the form set out above (Article VI).

The seventh is diligent custody of the heart. For this to be guarded effectively, it is necessary to restrain the tongue from evil speaking, to keep the belly from gluttony, and to repress the exterior senses, so that one does not look on another incautiously and lustfully, nor hears unlawful words if one can avoid them; and if one cannot, hear them with a sad countenance and unwillingly, since Solomon testifies: *By the sadness of the countenance the mind of the offender is corrected* (Eccles. vii, 4). Disorderly touch must be entirely avoided.

Besides these, certain praiseworthy industries are assigned to making progress.

The first is to provoke oneself to devotion by bodily posture: by prostrating oneself on the ground, or bending the knees, or striking the breast. Yet in the presence of others nothing singular in such things is to be done.

The second is to seek a secret and quiet place in which one can pour out his heart before God with confidence, and give himself to meditation and prayer without the disturbance of others. Hence, as the Evangelist says, Christ went up into the mountain alone to pray (Matth. xiv, 23). And he himself teaches: *When thou shalt pray, enter into thy chamber, and having shut the door, pray to thy Father in secret* (Matth. vi, 6). Hence too at the approach of his Passion he was separated even from the three most beloved and familiar disciples, to pray apart (Luke xxii, 41) — not indeed for his own need, but for our instruction.

The third is to observe the time most suited. For the night-time is more fitted for giving oneself to devotion. Hence the Prophet exhorts: *Rise up, give praise in the night, at the beginning of the watches: pour out thy heart like water before the face of the Lord thy God: lift up thy hands to him for the life of thy little ones* (Thren. ii, 19). The time of dawn too, while the senses are sober, is suitable. Wherefore the Wise Man says: *The just man shall give his heart to resort early to the Lord that made him* (Ecclus. xxxix, 6). And Isaias touched on both these times, saying: *My soul hath desired thee in the night: yea and with my spirit within me in the morning early I will watch to thee* (Is. xxvi, 9). None the less, acts and prayers to which certain fixed times are assigned must be paid at the hours appointed.

The fourth is to invoke the intercession of the Saints, especially of the glorious Virgin and of one's own angel. Hence Job exhorts: *Turn to one of the Saints* (Job v, 1).

The fifth is to proceed with discretion in all things, so that (according to the apostolic teaching) *all things be done decently and according to order* (I Cor. xiv, 40). Hence the holy father Antony asserted that discretion is the most necessary and useful of all the virtues. For it is the charioteer of virtues, observing the mean and the mode, and walking the royal road. Therefore the Apostle said: *Your reasonable service* (Rom. xii, 1).

The sixth is to take good note of one's natural or ingrained disposition, and to fight specially against the vices to which one is most prone, and to cut away the root of vices.

The seventh is to weigh one's own vocation, and according to its demands to study to walk worthily before God, and to fulfil more carefully those things which God requires first and principally of one. Hence the Apostle exhorts: *I beseech you in the Lord, that you walk worthy of the vocation in which you are called, with all humility and mildness, with patience, supporting one another in charity, careful to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace* (Ephes. iv, 1–3). Hence too in Ecclesiasticus it is said: *The things that God hath commanded thee, think on them always, and in his many works be not curious* (Ecclus. iii, 22).

The eighth is to show mercy to all as far as one can, so that it be done to oneself from God in like measure. According to that word of the Saviour: *Blessed are the merciful: for they shall obtain mercy* (Matth. v, 7). And briefly, let one so act toward neighbours as one desires the Lord to act toward oneself. Hence Tobias teaches: *Turn not away thy face from any poor man: for so it shall come to pass that the face of the Lord shall not be turned from thee* (Tob. iv, 7).

The ninth is to desire always to please God, and to accustom the heart everywhere and always, or at least wholly frequently, to lift itself to him. To accomplish this, use brief and inward prayers, saying: *Create a clean heart in me, O God* (Ps. l, 12), or something similar; and breathing toward God through affectionate words of this kind: *O most sweet God, when shall I love thee with all my heart? when shall I lovingly hold thee close? when shall I be kindled with thy love? when shall I cleave to thee stably?*

The tenth is to propose and impress on one's mind that to which all its good exercises are ordered, and as often as it feels itself removed from it, fallen, distracted, to grieve at once and turn one's mind's gaze back to it. Now this is the actual union of the mind with God, or purity of heart.

The eleventh is not to cease to continue its good and usual devotional exercises, whether it experiences sensible devotion and alacrity or not: nay to apply itself more to the detestation of vices and to the readiness of the mind for what belongs to the worship and honour of God, than to experimental and sensible devotion and fervour; and with wholly pure affection and intention to aspire to God, to venerate him with all its powers, and to desire that all honour him, to intercede for all the living and the dead — especially for relations, subjects and benefactors, and above all for the common good and for parents. Let one finally study to spend every space and moment of time fruitfully, to the praise and glory of the Almighty, who is above all things God sublime and blessed. Amen.

*Here ends the little work On the Fountain of Light and the Paths of Life.*
