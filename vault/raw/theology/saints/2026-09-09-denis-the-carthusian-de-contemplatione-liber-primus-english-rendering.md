# On Contemplation

*Denis the Carthusian*

## Book One: On Contemplation, Its Kinds and Its Practice

## Proemium

*I have loved wisdom, and have sought her out from my youth, and have desired to take her for my spouse, and I became a lover of her form, knowing that she will communicate herself to me of her goods, and will be a comfort in my cares. With her I shall have rest: for her conversation hath no bitterness, nor her company any tediousness, but joy and gladness.* (Wisd. viii, 2, 9, 16)

The only-begotten Son of God taught us that wisdom above all things is to be loved and longed for; who, placing our happiness in the act of wisdom, said to the eternal Father: *This is eternal life: that they may know thee, the true God, and Jesus Christ whom thou hast sent* (John xvii, 3). For since God is the highest, true and full wisdom, the human mind is deified by participation in God's wisdom, and is equated with the angelic minds; and although in the present life the wisdom of the human mind cannot equal the wisdom of the angelic spirits, the faithful man can now advance so far in charity and infused wisdom that he may deserve in heaven to outshine certain supernal spirits in the beauty and glory of wisdom, being more intimately and more happily united to the fontal and uncreated Wisdom. And indeed wisdom is the beauty of the soul, the brightness of the mind, the nobility of the understanding; without which no one is made lovable to God nor capable of healing from the hurt of sin, Scripture testifying: *God loveth none but him that dwelleth with wisdom* (Wisd. vii, 28); and elsewhere: *By wisdom were healed all those who pleased thee, O Lord, from the beginning* (Wisd. ix, 19).

This is the true, divine and saving wisdom, which the Holy Spirit alone diffuses and implants in the created mind: which none of the reprobate obtains to the end, and which no philosopher is reckoned to have known or received. For *into a malicious soul wisdom shall not enter* (Wisd. i, 4): nay when it enters the mind it finds evil, it makes it good. Without this wisdom the darkness of ignorance and the mist of sin cover the mind; the winds and fumes perpetually arising from sensuality cloud and trouble the soul. But when the light of this wisdom spreads, shines and diffuses itself through all the interior of the mind, the clouds of vices are scattered, the movements of the passions are calmed, the fog of ignorance is wiped away, and the whole soul is moreover brightened. For as in the whole frame of the world all bodily things are illumined, ordered and made fruitful by the splendour of the sun: so by the splendour of this wisdom all the interior of the mind is illumined, adorned and made fruitful.

Let us therefore love this wisdom ardently, seek it most heartily; and take it too as the spouse of our rational spirit, embracing it spiritually within us in the chamber of the heart with the arms of charity, resting sweetly in its chaste embraces, and bearing fruit in manifold ways from it: producing and as it were begetting from it sincere meditations, holy affections, circumspect speech and virtuous works. So then it will *communicate to us of its goods*, fecundating our mind with the aforesaid fruits, flooding it with spiritual joy and enriching it with its other effects and divine contemplations.

Since our happiness is placed in the knowledge of God (as has been touched on already), and the knowledge of God is called above all contemplation, and therefore our perfection and happiness consists in contemplation: for this reason I wish and intend to write something on contemplation as the most quiet and most sweet end of all our exercises, desires and labours — as the Spirit of truth shall deign to grant — rather for the arousing of myself than for the instruction of others, especially since to inform others of this matter surpasses the powers and experience of my littleness. Much too has already been written on contemplation by great men. Hence I confess that I can be charged with presumption, because to write on contemplation belongs to those who have experience, to men divine and sublime; but I am without experience, base and weak. Yet lest I be altogether convicted of rashness, I intend to speak of this subject only in a simple manner, cleaving to the teachings of the great doctors and the holy fathers and contemplative men.

### Article I

*The intention of the present work*

*The Father of glory give unto you the spirit of wisdom and revelation in the knowledge of him; the eyes of your heart enlightened.* (Ephes. i, 17, 18)

In every rightly ordered multitude there must be order, namely a fitting reduction to unity. And as every multitude proceeds from one, so it returns and tends to one. A multitude proceeds from one effective principle, and tends to one end. Since, then, the powers of the rational soul are many, and their acts very numerous, there must be order in the powers and in the acts likewise, so that one power of the soul is higher than the rest, and one act better than the others.

Now since, as has just been touched on, the Saviour placed our beatitude in the knowledge of God; and the knowledge of God is called above all contemplation; and therefore our perfection and happiness consists in contemplation — as in the most quiet and most sweet end of all our exercises, desires and labours — I therefore desire and intend to write something on contemplation.

### Article II

*On the manifold kinds of wisdom*

*All wisdom is from the Lord God, and hath been always with him, and is before all time.* (Ecclus. i, 1)

As God as first being is the cause of the essence and being of all things, and as first good is the cause of the goodness and desire of all things, and as first living is the cause of the life and living of all things: so as first wise, or the first, fontal and exemplary wisdom, he is the cause of the wisdom, understanding and knowledge of all things. For in every order and in each genus the first is the most powerful, most perfect and most causal in its own order and genus.

Yet God is not in a uniform and the same way the cause of wisdom and knowledge for all. For all created minds have this in common, that they receive their intellectual nature and intellectual power immediately from the highest and uncreated mind; but all do not immediately derive their habitual or actual wisdom or knowledge causally from God, but some obtain wisdom and knowledge through thought, labour, study and the instruction of others. Further, to the angelic minds their wisdom and knowledge are concreated with them according to their nature — as extending to what their natural property, condition and capacity encompasses; but the wisdom of beatific fruition, and the knowledge by which they know created things in the Word, they received divinely in their conversion to God and confirmation in good. As for other supernatural perfections, when and how they received them from God there are various opinions.

In men there is first of all a certain natural, acquired and philosophical wisdom; and this wisdom is the knowledge of immaterial, intellectual and immortal natures (especially of the most simple and unchangeable God himself), so far as they can be known from the course, nature and order of things. For *the invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made* (Rom. i, 20). Moreover, since man was created for supernatural beatitude, natural wisdom did not suffice him, but he had need of faith and supernatural wisdom: through which he knows of God what does not appear by natural reason nor can be found and comprehended, but has been divinely revealed to the holy Patriarchs, Apostles and Prophets, as those things that fall under the articles of faith.

Furthermore, this supernatural wisdom is twofold. One is numbered among the gifts of grace freely given: which is called scholastic, because it is learned by human study, though it was first and originally supernaturally infused and divinely revealed. It can also be, nay is, formless in many, who without charity and sanctifying grace live unhappily, yet learn, have or teach the aforesaid wisdom. Of which the Apostle says: *To another, by the Spirit, is given the word of wisdom* (I Cor. xii, 8). This is contained and described in the writings of the Old and New Testaments; and by it a certain true knowledge and right judgement of divine things is had, as to a simple knowing of them.

Another is set among the gifts of sanctifying grace, since without this grace and charity it cannot be had; and it is infused from above, formed and saving: by which our mind discerns, feels and judges well of God and of divine things that fall under faith, not only through true knowledge and a certain clear gaze of reason, but also through the conformity and connaturality of our affection to them, as the virtuous, temperate and chaste man judges well of virtue, sobriety and chastity — which happens through a certain savoury and loving taste of divine things. Hence wisdom (*sapientia*) is said to be as it were a savoury knowledge (*sapida scientia*), according to that word of Ecclesiasticus: *Wisdom is according to her name* (Ecclus. vi, 23). This wisdom holds the first place among the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit, and is in all who are in the state of salvation according to some degree, as are the other six gifts. And finally the chief act of this wisdom is said to be contemplation itself, as will be more clearly shown shortly after.

Moreover, holy Scripture frequently speaks of the only-begotten Son of God under the name of wisdom: for he is the uncreated Wisdom, the Word and Truth of God the Father. And although the divine essence, the most blessed Trinity itself, and each divine Person, is eternal, highest, infinite and uncreated wisdom, yet wisdom is appropriated to the Son, who is properly called the begotten Wisdom. For the Son of God is the eternal Word of the Father. Now the Word is the emanation of the understanding. And wisdom certainly pertains to the understanding: therefore it is appropriated to the Son of God.

### Article III

*What contemplation is*

*Contemplation, taken in its proper sense, is the affectionate, prompt and sincere knowledge of the superessential Deity and of what is referred to it.*

[Note: This definition, with which the article opens, is verbally the same as the one given in De Fonte Lucis (Art. X); the fuller development here follows the same general course with richer citation of authorities.]

The consideration of creatures, namely through their relation and reference to God — according as they flow originally from him, are providentially governed by him, and tend to him finally as their end, and are considered by theologians — belongs to contemplation. For thus the eternal and holy God is the end and reason of their consideration: since whatever is considered in them and known of them is referred to the knowledge and love, the honour and glory of the Creator, who adorned the fabric of the universe so beautifully, distinguished it so wisely, placed it on nothing so wonderfully, filled it with such greatness, multitude and power, that from the consideration of all these things his magnificence is known, his majesty glorified, his wisdom, goodness, beauty and power honoured, praised and admired by all. But first and above all contemplation concerns the Deity itself, whose knowledge ought to be the end of all sciences and knowledge.

Now the knowledge of God, if it is not formed by charity and adorned by virtuous acts, is cold, sterile and formless. Hence it must be affectionate if it is to deserve to be called contemplation: especially since (as touched on already) contemplation is the act of wisdom which is called a gift, which cannot be formless but is seasoned with a savour of divine things and is inseparably conjoined with charity.

Further, this contemplation is to be prompt and easy, as fire swiftly and freely ascends to divine things, raises itself to what is above, passes beyond what is created and reaches the Creator. Hence Richard says: Contemplation is borne about with wonderful agility in free flight wherever the spirit carries it. And defining contemplation again: Contemplation, he says, is the free penetration of the mind, held in wonder, into the spectacles of wisdom. Hugh too, defining it: Contemplation, he says, is the penetrating and free gaze of the soul spread out on every side over the things to be seen. And St Bernard defines contemplation thus: Contemplation is the true and certain gaze of the mind on anything.

Lastly, the knowledge that is called contemplation shall be sincere and pure, lest it be fouled by mixture with error, passion or fault, but resplend with the certainty and splendour of truth: for it is especially fitting to the mind already purged. Therefore Bernard says that contemplation is the apprehension of truth without doubt.

Now if the act of natural and philosophical wisdom, and also the action of theological wisdom (whether acquired or infused, which is reckoned among the gifts of grace freely given), are deemed worthy of the name contemplation, this will be only in so far as those habits are sustained by sanctifying grace, formed, kindled and quickened by divine charity, and also perfected by the infused wisdom which is a gift, and so their acts become meritorious. For virtuous, intellectual and sapiential habits and their acts help, adorn and stabilize one another, according as the soul is made readier from the act of one habit to the act of another.

### Article IV

*Why it is expedient to bring in the sayings of the chief philosophers for a fuller knowledge of contemplation*

*As created nature serveth its Creator, so ought the natural wisdom and science of the philosophers to serve the divine, supernatural and theological wisdom of Christians.* (cf. Wisd. xvi, 24)

Philosophy moreover contributes much to understanding what is handed on in holy Scripture, as Augustine says: The knowledge of the natural properties of created things opens the hidden things of the Scriptures. And the divine nature and its properties and attributes become known to us in this life in some way through created things. For the order and species of beings is itself as a ladder and vehicle for ascending to the knowledge of the Creator. Hence Augustine says in the second book on Christian Doctrine: If the philosophers have said anything true and fitting to our faith, we must not only not shrink from it, but claim it back from them as unjust possessors for our own use. So Cyprian, Lactantius, Victorinus and Hilary did. And Damascene in the fourth book said: If we can make use of anything from those who are without, it is not to be rejected.

Since therefore Plato and Aristotle and their followers, the most excellent philosophers, have spoken and left written certain subtle things on contemplation and sapiential wisdom and the contemplative life, I think it fitting to recall and touch on these in turn, so that what the more illustrious theologians, contemplative and holy men have taught on the same matter may shine out more clearly.

### Article V

*On the nature of the rational soul, and the powers by which it is capable of the grace of contemplation*

*Since contemplation is a certain accident and action of the rational soul, and accident is known through its subject; that what is to be said of contemplation may be more clearly apparent, I think it necessary to speak briefly of the nature of the rational soul and the powers by which it is capable of contemplation.*

God sublime and blessed is a most simple, separate, pure and uncircumscribed mind, which is a light most pure and immense: in which being, power and action; or nature, virtue and operation are substantially one. From this eternal, most blessed and adorable mind, by creation there flowed all secondary minds, angelic and human. The more a created mind has received of intellectual light, the more like and nearer it is to the first, divine and uncreated mind. And in the hierarchies and orders of the angelic minds, the higher received more abundantly of intellectual light from the highest, most honoured and most worthy intellect. But the human mind has obtained the lowest place and degree in the chain of intellectual substances. Therefore it naturally has less of intellectual light: nay, according to the Peripatetics, at the beginning of its existence it is as a blank tablet. Yet, because it truly perseveres in the circle and order of intellectual essences, it is of an immortal nature and capable of true beatitude.

So then the mind or rational soul, in so far as it is an immaterial, intellectual, simple, volitional and free form, is called the image of the first mind, the ray of the divine light immortal: and so, raised above all time and continuum, it has a certain face and gaze directed immediately to divine, heavenly and eternal things, nay to God the Creator; and for this reason it is reckoned capable of true beatitude, as convertible to God by knowing and loving, and immediately unitable to him, and through this capable of fruition of him through the beatific vision — in which fruition certainly the true, highest and full happiness of all created intellects consists. For everything returns to the fountain of its own procession — that is, to that from which it is caused or flows — as the Platonist Proclus subtly proves in his theological Elementatio. Therefore, as the rational soul flowed from the highest God immediately by creation, so it is beatified in him alone.

Furthermore, in the most utterly simple God alone, being, power and action — or essence, potency and operation — are really identified, as is gathered from the teachings of the divine Dionysius. Hence, according to the excellent philosophers and theologians, the powers of the soul are really distinguished from its essence. But the higher powers of the soul, incorruptible and non-organic, by which it is immediately joined to God as to its object, are intellect and will; by which, contemplating and loving, it is fixed on God, so that in the rational soul there is no power higher than intellect and will. Hence what some say — that in the soul reason is above intellect, and understanding (*intelligentia*) above intellect, and the apex of the affective above the will itself — is not to be taken as though reason, intellect and understanding were distinct and subordinate powers; but that the same power, according to its more or less noble acts, receives different names and is said to be more worthy than itself. Namely the same power is called reason, in so far as it is discursive; intellect, in so far as it is intuitive by simple apprehension; and understanding (*intelligentia*), in so far as it is contemplative of divine things and the most sublime Deity. Again the same power is called will, in so far as it is appetitive or loving of the known good: which also, in so far as it has a relation to the highest and divine good, can be called the chief apex of the affective.

### Article VI

*What synderesis and the spark of understanding are*

*Since the highest species of contemplation possible to this life — namely the unitive vision of mystical theology — pertains to the contact of synderesis and the spark of understanding, according to the anagogic doctors, as will be shown below by God's grace; it is fitting to make clear what synderesis itself and the spark of understanding are.*

The doctors appear to speak of this matter in various and different ways. The difference, however (as it appears), lies rather in the words than in the thing. For all agree in this, that synderesis is something supreme and divine in the rational and intellectual nature, naturally moving and stimulating to good, and murmuring against evil; but some assert that it belongs to the cognitive power, while others say it belongs to the affective power.

St Thomas, commenting on the second book of the Sentences and in the first part of the Summa, affirms that, just as in speculative reason there is posited a certain concreated habit of the soul by which first principles in speculative matters are naturally known without reasoning as soon as terms are understood (as that of anything there is either affirmation or negation, and that the whole is greater than its part), from which principles reason proceeds to knowledge of conclusions — and this habit is called by the Philosopher in the sixth book of the Ethics *understanding* (*intellectus*): so in practical reason there needs to be posited a concreated habit of the soul by which first principles in matters of action are naturally known (as that God must be obeyed, and that good is to be preferred to evil); and this habit is called synderesis. Of which Augustine speaks, that the universal principles of right are written in the natural judgement-seat: which is synderesis itself. And again in the book on Free Will: In the natural judgement-seat, he says, there are present the rules, and the seeds of virtues and true and unchangeable things — whose cognitive habit, according to what has now been said, is synderesis.

Others say that synderesis is a motive or affective power of the soul, which is always inclined to fix itself on higher things and to be elevated to divine things, moving and stimulating to good, and abhorring evil.

Now this light concreated to the soul is called a spark (*scintilla*), because, like a spark flying from fire, it is a small light in comparison with the light of the angelic mind; and also for the splendour of its cognition and the ardour of its inclination to good: for through it the soul perceives truth and is inclined to good. For synderesis does not err in first principles of practical matters, but always remonstrates against what is contrary to them. Hence the spark of understanding is never wholly extinguished in anyone. Hence, according to the blessed Jerome, this spark or synderesis is signified by the eagle, because it is the supreme in the soul as to natural things and transcends rational virtue, namely reason itself according as reason is named from the discursive process of reasoning. Hence in synderesis there is no sin, but through it the soul receives a certain immediate reference to God, to be directly and immediately touched, irradiated and inflamed by him. Sometimes too this spark of understanding is called the apex of the mind. Of which light we sing in the Psalm: *The light of thy countenance, O Lord, is signed upon us* (Ps. iv, 7).

### Article VII

*On the subject of contemplation; and whether the intellect is the most worthy power of the soul*

*That the wisdom and its act which is called contemplation are intuitive and cognitive, is not called in question by anyone. For what is contemplation but a spiritual and inward vision, understanding or knowledge? The intellect, therefore, or (if this sounds more subtle) understanding — that is, the intellect in so far as it has reference and order to divine, immortal and eternal things — is rightly said to be the subject of contemplation.* Hence contemplation or the contemplative life formally and as to its essence pertains to the intellect. Yet, since (as was said above) contemplation is not a bare and formless knowledge, but affectionate and affective, therefore as to its formative complement — which is ardent love — it is placed in the will or apex of the will; as beatitude, which formally is knowledge, as Christ testifies: *This is eternal life: that they may know thee, the true God* (John xvii, 3).

Finally, contemplation is the highest perfection and happiness of the mind, as Augustine asserts in the twelfth book on the Trinity: Contemplation of God will be the supreme reward of the saints. Hence the Philosopher too in the tenth book of the Ethics testifies that the contemplation of the best object of contemplation, namely God, is happiness. But the supreme perfection and beatitude of the soul is in its most worthy power. Hence the intellect, which is the subject of contemplation, is held to be the most excellent power of the soul: which St Thomas also proves and defends in the first part of the Summa, question eighty-two, article three. It is confirmed also by what Augustine says in the fifth book on the Trinity: We find nothing in our nature better than the understanding. And in the eleventh book on the City of God: God speaks to that in man which is better in man. And it is certain that the speech of God is inward and directed to and concerns the intellect, as Augustine discussed in the fifteenth book on the Trinity: Thoughts are certain speeches of the heart (that is, of the intellect). Hence the Philosopher says in the tenth book of the Ethics that the intellect is the noblest power of the intellectual soul.

### Article VIII

*How between the human mind and God there is no medium, such that God alone is said to be better than our mind*

*That the angelic spirits surpass human minds in nature, power and action, wisdom, happiness and glory, and are intermediaries in the order of things between God and human minds, holy canonical Scripture teaches sufficiently; as does that most sacred prince of theologians, Dionysius, in the works On the Angelic Hierarchy.*

Now, speaking of the formal specific dignity in itself, it is plain that the angelic minds and supernal virtues are greatly more excellent and more perfect than human minds.

Yet some respond that the words of the blessed Augustine are true and are to be understood in this respect: that just as the angel, so too the rational soul received its nature, grace and glory immediately from God alone. It can also be answered that, speaking of the dignity of the soul by reference to its last end, nothing is found after God more excellent than it: since, as the beatitude of the angelic minds, so also its happiness is placed in God alone as its immediate object. To pass swiftly over this and the nature, natural perfection and powers of the rational soul: what has been said has been introduced so that what is to be said of contemplation may be more evidently known. For as the being of an accident, so also its knowledge, depends on its subject and on the knowledge of the subject — since the principles of being and of knowing are the same, according to philosophical teaching. For a thing is known through its cause, since to know is to know a thing through its cause.

### Article IX

*What the chief philosophers have said of contemplation*

*Plato, whom Augustine in the eighth book on the City of God affirms to be rightly and by far set before the other pagan philosophers, together with Aristotle (whom Jerome in his Rule prefers to all philosophers) and their followers, said that the intellect is immortal and perpetual, and that nothing is more excellent than the intellect in our nature.* And on account of the splendour of the intellect Aristotle says there is something divine in us. Plato asserts in the Timaeus that there is between us and the gods a parity of name and a certain fellowship of nature or divinity. Since therefore they held the intellect to be the most divine and most worthy power of the rational soul, they judged that the highest good and the ultimate end or chief happiness of the soul was to be placed in the action of the intellect (which they called contemplation), and especially in the contemplation of the first and best object of contemplation, namely God.

Hence in the tenth book on the City of God Augustine recounts how the great Platonist Plotinus asserted that the life of blessedness and the light of understanding or the contemplation of truth is not furnished to the supernal spirits from any other source than that from which it is also furnished to us, namely from God. And Porphyry the Platonist, as Augustine often mentions, held that the perfection of the rational soul consists in the complete withdrawal and purest retreat from all matter and all earthly and carnal affection, and likewise that it is beatified in the knowledge of the Father: for the Platonists called the highest God the Father.

The Peripatetics — Avicenna, Algazel, Averroes, Alpharabius and many others — who held that in this life man can attain to the knowledge of separated substances as to what they are, argued that the perfection of our contemplation and happiness was to be placed in the perfect knowledge of the understanding which is called a separated substance. Which can be saved in some way, speaking of the natural beatitude of the soul, not indeed so that the soul should stop in the knowledge of the understanding, but in so far as through such knowledge it is made more apt for the contemplation of the first cause and highest truth. For simply, even according to God, contemplative life is what Aristotle in the tenth of the Ethics and Plato in the Phaedo testified to be divine rather than human; and through it the soul is conformed not only to the supernal intellects but also to the first and uncreated intellect; and no one can attain this life without long and supreme labour of philosophy, especially metaphysical, and study of wisdom and the virtues, overcoming concupiscences and resting from exterior things. Finally, more things on this will be said below.

### Article X

*Whether the philosophical knowledge of divine things deserves to be called contemplation*

*The Catholic doctors assign this difference between theological and philosophical wisdom: that theological wisdom is affective or formed by divine love, for which reason it is truly and properly called wisdom — that is, a savoury knowledge. Hence it is also asserted to be unitive, since through the affection of charity it joins the created mind to God. Hence also its act is properly called contemplation. But philosophical wisdom is only speculative, formless and bare: therefore, according to Hugh on the Angelic Hierarchy, it was not for life, and was to be found and administered by those who were not sons of life, namely the philosophers — of whom the Apostle says that although they had known God, they did not glorify him as God but vanished in their thoughts* (Rom. i, 21).

It must be answered that the philosophers who knew only the natural happiness of the soul spoke also of natural love of God alone, and of those virtues which can be obtained by natural virtue and human study. But these do not suffice for salvation. Therefore in them there was no sincere knowledge of divine things; and they erred in many ways, though they said some things true and subtle. Their speculation therefore does not properly and completely deserve the name of contemplation. Nor were they truly perfect, but only according to their own opinion, by which they placed the highest perfection in the natural virtues. For their knowledge was not anointed with the affection of that charity which alone makes the mind pleasing to God — namely divine, supernatural, infused charity, by which the knowledge of divine things that is called contemplation must be formed and perfected.

### Article XI

*On the division of life into the contemplative and the active*

*As the holy Gregory testifies, there are two lives in which almighty God teaches us through the sacred Scriptures. One is the contemplative: whose end is not an external work, but the sincere and formed knowledge of truth itself. Which, since it is the beatitude and end of the rational creature, is not referred to another — especially since it also includes the love of God virtually and as its formative complement. The other is the active: whose immediate and first end is the outer work or action itself.*

This division of life is given as covering virtuous human life, which belongs to man by reason of the intellect, from which it receives its species. Therefore, as the intellect is twofold — namely the contemplative or speculative, whose end is the consideration of truth (as the Philosopher says in the second book of the First Philosophy), and the practical, whose end is action — so a twofold life is distinguished.

None the less Aristotle in the first book of the Ethics posits a threefold life: the voluptuous, the contemplative and the civil, which by another name is called the active. But this does not conflict with what has been said: since the voluptuous life does not befit man according to that by which man is man, but according to the sensitive part, which he has in common with beasts. Therefore such a life is more bestial than human, and is not included under the life divided here.

Moreover Thomas testifies in the second part of the second part that all the studies of human actions, if they are ordered to the necessity of the present life according to right reason, pertain to the active life, which by ordered actions provides for the necessities of the present life; but if they serve concupiscence in any way, they pertain to the voluptuous life; and if they are ordered to the consideration of truth, they pertain to the contemplative life. — None the less the studies of the active life are ultimately to be referred to the end of the contemplative life, especially to that knowledge of truth which is had in heaven and is the beatific vision of God, of which the apostle John speaks: *When he shall appear, we shall be like to him* (I John iii, 2), *because we shall see him as he is.* For this vision is the end of all our exercises; and whatever we think, desire, speak, do or suffer, we must refer to it. But the active life takes its species from its proximate end. Some acts too — such as preaching, hearing confessions, teaching sacred doctrine — have a peculiar kinship with the contemplative life.

Furthermore, as Augustine asserts in the nineteenth book on the City of God: No one ought to be so given to contemplation that in that contemplation he does not think of the utility of his neighbour; nor so active that he does not seek the contemplation of God. In leisure there should not be idle ease, but the seeking or finding of truth, so that each may advance in it and not begrudge what he has found to another. But in action there is not to be a love of temporal honour or power, since all is vanity under the sun; but the very work done, if it profits the true salvation of those under one's care. So no one is forbidden from the study of knowing truth: which pertains to laudable leisure. But a superior position — without which the people cannot be governed — if it is held and administered as it should be, is yet indecently sought. Therefore sacred leisure is sought by charity or love of truth; just business is undertaken out of the necessity of charity. Which burden, if none imposes it, we must give ourselves to the receiving and beholding of truth; but if it is imposed, it is to be undertaken out of the necessity of charity; yet even so the delight of truth is not entirely to be abandoned, lest that sweetness be taken away and that necessity be crushing.

And so the contemplative life has distinct and various degrees, according to the variety of those for whom it is fitting.

### Article XII

*On the acts that are required for, concur with and belong to the contemplative life; in which it is shown how the contemplative life consists in one act*

*Life is properly called the action on which living things are mainly and more delightfully intent.* Although therefore the contemplative life and contemplation may in reality be called the same thing, yet contemplation does not take on the name of the contemplative life except in so far as it is an act commanded by the will formed by charity, and meritoriousnly and delightfully united by love to the last end. So, although the contemplative life consists principally in one act — namely in contemplation itself, as in the act by which it is finally perfected, from which it receives its unity, name and species, because it principally intends this act — none the less it has various acts by which it reaches this principal act and by which this act is formed, commanded and completed.

Hence some say that four things are required for the contemplative life. The first is the very conjunction of the mind with God, which is effected by love — nay is really love itself in act, which is the life of the mind or soul. The second is contemplation, in which the contemplative life formally consists. The third is the jussio or intention of the will, commanding and intending the contemplative act. The fourth is the delight consequent on the act, adorning and completing it. Yet it is not necessary that there should always be a perceptible or sensible delight, but it suffices that there be true and intellectual joy, or the complacency of the will: for thus each virtue has a delight annexed to its act.

Further, according to the divine and holy Dionysius in the seventh chapter on the Divine Names, this distinction is drawn between the angelic and human mind: that the angelic and supernal minds see truth by a simple apprehension and gaze; but the human mind arrives at the simple intuition of truth by a certain process and discourse from many things. Therefore, although the contemplative life principally consists in one act (speaking of one in genus or species), yet many acts concur toward this principal act, namely: the reception and knowledge of the principles from which it proceeds to contemplation; and also the deduction of principles to the truth whose knowledge is sought. But this takes place in contemplations that are reached from below through the inquiry and study of reason; not however in the higher contemplations which, with the Holy Spirit radiating from above, are infused from heaven, God touching, anointing and illuminating the mind supernaturally. For in such cases the human mind behaves in an angelic manner, and is called by the great Dionysius God-formed, because it discerns truth by a simple stroke and a heaven-formed gaze. For there is no delay in learning, when the creating Wisdom who has infinite power teaches truth.

Moreover it should be known that in the immanent acts of intellect and will the contemplative life principally and first consists; and since the chief immanent acts are the acts of wisdom and charity, therefore the contemplative life is secondarily placed in the actual love of God and of divine things — especially since (as shown above) love is the complement, beauty and form of contemplation. Hence Gregory says in his commentary on Ezechiel: The contemplative life consists in holding the love of God and neighbour with all the mind, in resting from exterior action, in cleaving alone to the desire of the Creator, so that nothing else may be pleasing to act upon outside, but all cares being trodden down, the mind may burn to see the face of its Creator: so that it now bears the burden of corruptible flesh with grief, and with all desires yearns to join the choirs of angels and to rejoice in the eternal incorruption in the sight of God.

Moreover the inward acts of divine charity are: to rejoice heartily with God over his immense wealth, magnificence and beatitude; to be fervently affected toward God, to be moved in him, to be inwardly contrite for sins from love of him; from the most warm impulse and fervour of fiery love to be absorbed into the deep of the Deity, sweetly to rest and happily to sleep, as far as possible in the present life, in the ecstatic fruition of the incomprehensible riches of God most high. Now it is of the contemplative life as it belongs to man in this life that we are speaking.

Furthermore, to the contemplative life belongs mental and vocal prayer, especially when poured out in secret, in so far as through it the grace of contemplation is acquired and the obstacles to contemplation — passions and vices — are removed. And to hear the word of God and read the Scriptures concurs with the contemplative life, in so far as one man is helped toward the grace of contemplation by another or by the teachings of the Scriptures; and similarly revelations exhibited through the ministry of angels.

Finally, an act of no slight excellence and dignity of the contemplative life is to insist on praising the Creator with heart and mouth, as it is written: *I will bless the Lord at all times: his praise shall be always in my mouth* (Ps. xxxiii, 2); and again: *I will sing to the Lord as long as I live: I will sing praise to my God while I have my being* (Ps. ciii, 33). It clearly belongs chiefly to the professors of the contemplative life to fulfil that word of the Apostle: *Singing and making melody in your hearts to the Lord* (Ephes. v, 19).

### Article XIII

*On the wealth, beauty, loveableness, dignity and sweetness or delight of contemplation and the contemplative life*

*Since contemplation is the act of wisdom — not any wisdom, but chiefly that which is the gift of the Holy Spirit, which no one who is subject to mortal sin possesses — therefore what Scripture says of the property and eminence of wisdom is rightly applied to contemplation, especially since the perfection is to be placed in the act rather than in the habit.*

So of the loveableness and dignity of wisdom it is read in the book of Wisdom: *All good things came to me together with her; and innumerable riches through her hands* (Wisd. vii, 11); and again: *If riches be desired in life, what is richer than wisdom, which maketh all things?* (Wisd. viii, 5). Solomon too in Proverbs: *Wisdom is more precious than all riches: and all the things that are desired are not to be compared with her* (Prov. iii, 15).

Hence Aristotle asserts that wisdom is a divine possession. And he exalts wisdom which is the knowledge of divine things so much that in his Metaphysics he testifies that he treated of material and sensible substances for the sake of knowledge of divine and separated substances (and especially of the highest and independent essence). And elsewhere: It is more desirable, he says, to know a little of divine substances than much of sensible things.

Moreover of the beauty of wisdom it is written: *She is more beautiful than the sun, and above all the order of the stars: being compared with the light, she is found before it* (Wisd. vii, 29); and again: *Wisdom is glorious, and never fadeth away* (Wisd. vi, 13). For wisdom is the splendour and ornament of the mind, and the spiritual light in which it sees divine things. And as created truth is a certain expression of the first and exemplary truth, so this infused wisdom is a bright and light-flowing and light-like ray of eternal and uncreated Wisdom.

Finally of the delight and sweetness of wisdom the Wise Man says: *Coming into my house, I shall repose myself with her: for her conversation hath no bitterness, nor her company any tediousness, but joy and gladness* (Wisd. viii, 16). Therefore the Scriptures testify that an instructed man is sweet to his own soul, and a learned man is robust (Ecclus. xxxvii, 22; 5). For as the sun by its ray disperses vapours and clouds and purifies the air, so this wisdom drives out the uncleanness of vices and the storms of passions and purifies the soul. Hence, according to the Philosopher, the wise man does not seek delight from without in base or vile things, because he is within himself full of pleasant contemplations. And again he affirms that philosophy has wonderful delights mixed in with it. Hence in the seventh book on the City of God Augustine says: The sweetness of the contemplation of God is unspeakable. And Gregory: The sweetness of the contemplative life, he says, is greatly loveable, which raises the soul above itself, opens the heavens, shows that earthly things are to be despised, lays bare spiritual things to the eyes of the mind.

These things have been brought in from the testimonies of the Scriptures and the fathers on the property and excellence of wisdom and contemplation and the contemplative life. They are easily demonstrated by reason also. For the wealth, dignity and delight of contemplation or of the contemplative life are proved: first, from the side of the object, which is the most excellent and most worthy, namely God and created things through their relation to God, as was said. Second, from the side of the subject, which is the mind or soul in its highest powers: for this life befits man in respect of what is supreme in his nature, namely the spark of understanding and the apex of the affective. Third, from the side of the mode of acting, because this life is purer and simpler, more self-sufficient and more stable than every other life, and also more divine: hence it is called angelic and celestial. And since man communicates with the divine and uncreated mind and with the angelic spirits only through intellect and will and their acts and habits, it is plain that the more anyone advances in contemplation and the contemplative life, the more divine he is and the more like the holy angels and the more dear to God — since likeness is the cause of love.

Finally, since God glorious and to be adored is uncircumscribable light, interminable wisdom, fire or immense ardour of love, infinite sweetness, illimitable beauty — it is certain that the more a created mind draws near to God, approaches, touches, attains him, the more splendidly is it brightened, the more abundantly is it taught, the more powerfully is it inflamed, the more sweetly does it savour or is it refreshed, the more delightfully does it contemplate; and by this it holds all visible, bodily and perishable things in contempt, abandons and despises them, counting all things, with the Apostle, as dung and condemning them (Philipp. iii, 8), except in so far as it has them in God or they help it toward God. So then contemplation has a delight that is pure and great: first, for its own sake, because it is an operation connaturally most fitting to man; second, from the side of the object, namely God, who is the most delicious object; third, from the side of charity, by which the contemplator is moved to contemplation; fourth, from the side of the disposition and habit of the contemplative mind — for the habit is wisdom itself, which the more perfect it is, the more joyful will be its act, namely contemplation; and the disposition of the mind to contemplation consists in the ordering of the passions and the quieting of unrests, which the more abundantly they are present, the more delightful will be contemplation and the contemplative life. And the spiritual delight consisting in contemplation and annexed to the contemplative life is greater than carnal delight in itself, although some are more delighted in carnal things from their own imperfection and indisposition to inward and spiritual delights. Now the more perfectly one loves God, the more abundantly one is delighted in his contemplation: as one who loves another especially is the more gladdened at the sight of him. Hence God said to Moses: *Thy brother Aaron will meet thee, and seeing thee, will be glad at heart* (Exod. iv, 14).

Since therefore the wealth, beauty, dignity, loveableness and delight of contemplation and the contemplative life are so great, one who is not prevented by a legitimate impediment ought rightly to aspire to it. And religious, especially solitaries, who have the most fitting means for contemplation, yet do not aspire nor strive to attain the grace of contemplation, nor fit, extend and transfer themselves to the sacred exercises of the interior life and its most sweet taste, but remain superficial, unreformed, dissolute and slothful, are greatly to be reproved.

### Article XIV

*Whether the contemplative life is more worthy and more meritorious than the active*

*Not only according to the Catholic doctors, but also according to the chief philosophers, the contemplative life is preferred to the active in goodness and dignity. Hence concerning Mary Magdalene intent on the word of God, by whom the contemplative life is designated, the Truth passed sentence that she had chosen the better part* (Luke x, 39, 42).

The Philosopher in the tenth book of the Ethics shows by most beautiful and subtle arguments that the contemplative life is more excellent than the active. The first is that the contemplative life belongs to man in respect of his most worthy power, namely the speculative intellect, and in respect of its proper and more worthy objects, which are separated and divine intelligibles; but the active life befits man in respect of the practical intellect, which is inferior to the speculative and is occupied with exterior and sensible things.

The second is that the contemplative life can be more continuous by its nature: nay in perfect men it is continued more day and night than the active in others. But in imperfect men the contemplative life is not continued to the same extent, because they are not yet stabilized in charity, grace, the gifts of the Holy Spirit and inward purity. Generally too in all, *the body which is corrupted weigheth down the soul, and the earthly habitation presseth down the mind that museth upon many things* (Wisd. ix, 15).

The third is that the delight of the contemplative life is greater than that of the active, according to the sense introduced above. Hence, as Augustine says, Martha — that is the active life — is troubled; Mary — that is the contemplative life — is spiritually feasted.

The fourth is that the contemplative life is more self-sufficient in itself, because the contemplative needs fewer things in the exercise of his life; but the active life needs many things, since it is concerned with exterior things. The contemplative indeed, the more fully he grows and the higher he ascends and the more abundantly he rejoices in the exercise of contemplation, the fewer bodily necessities he is content with for his bodily support. Therefore the Lord says to Martha, who is the figure of the active: *Thou art careful, and art troubled about many things* (Luke x, 41). Solomon too speaks of wisdom, which is the principle of the contemplative life: *Length of days is in her right hand, and in her left hand riches and glory* (Prov. iii, 16). We see too that the active serve the contemplatives by providing bodily things, so as to be partakers of their spiritual goods.

The fifth is that the contemplative life is sought for its own sake, but the active is ordered to another. The sixth is that the contemplative life consists in quiet and leisure, the active in labour and motion. The seventh is that the contemplative life is more divine, the active more human. For since the contemplative life is chiefly placed in the acts of charity and wisdom, and God is essentially held to be charity and wisdom, it is plain that through the contemplative life one is especially assimilated to God: nay by contemplating and loving divine things one is especially assimilated to the Most High, whose life is the contemplation of his own eternal truth and the love of his own goodness. The eighth is that the contemplative life befits us by that by which we are separated from beasts and which is proper to us; but the active also by that in which we communicate with irrational creatures, namely according to the lower powers of the soul.

None the less the active life is in a certain way preferred to the contemplative — namely in the case where it pertains to a prelate to be present in spiritual things too, to attend to the conversion of others, to hear confessions, to pour out public prayers for others. Of these acts some say that they are to be preferred to the contemplative life: which I think probable in a well-understood sense. First, since the blessed Dionysius confesses that the most divine of all things is to cooperate with God in the leading back of souls to him (On the Celestial Hierarchy); and Gregory, that the most acceptable sacrifice to God is zeal for souls. And indeed the aforesaid acts include the perfection of both lives, because they proceed from a heartfelt love of God.


### Article XV

*Which life is more lasting and more continuous*

*The life of the Lord Christ on earth was both contemplative and active; and so truly contemplative that it was also most abundantly active; and yet in it the contemplative was preferred, as is shown by the choice of Magdalene.* (cf. Luke x, 42)

According to the Philosopher in the seventh book of the Ethics, the purer and simpler the essence or nature of a thing, the more uniform and simpler is its action and delight. Hence God rejoices in one most simple action and most pure pleasure. For action corresponds to the nature of the agent, and the mode of acting is proportioned and equated to the mode of being. Since, then, in the order of intellectual natures the rational soul is farthest removed from the divine supersimple mind, it is certain that by its nature it is most wandering, defective and unstable. Hence it is not fixed in one consideration and affection, but quickly slides to other things.

The contemplative life is by its nature more lasting. For the active life ends with this present world: the active person provides for temporal and bodily necessities, which shall not exist in the homeland. But the contemplative life, whose end is the sincere knowledge of truth, shall remain in the homeland, when we shall know even as we are known. And that life which is ordained to the perpetual end is itself in some way more perpetual, especially since it is a beginning and participation of the life that is to come. Hence Christ said of Mary: *Mary hath chosen the best part, which shall not be taken away from her* (Luke x, 42).

None the less the active life is more continuous in imperfect men, because they are not yet so firmly rooted in charity and wisdom as to be able to persevere long in the contemplative act; and in them the body, which is corrupted, weighs down the soul, and they need many external things for sustenance. The soul therefore needs to descend at times from the heights of contemplation to attend to external necessities, and is hindered by the affections and phantasms that arise from the body.

### Article XVI

*On the weakness of the human mind for stable contemplation*

*No man knoweth the things that are of God, but the Spirit of God.* (I Cor. ii, 11)

The human mind has an inherent infirmity and instability with respect to contemplation. This arises from multiple causes. As the Philosopher shows in the seventh book of the Ethics, the purer and simpler the nature, the more uniform and simple its action. God therefore rejoices in one most simple action. But the rational soul stands at the farthest remove from the divine supersimple mind in the order of intellectual natures, and so by its nature it is most wandering, defective and unstable, unable to fix itself in one consideration and affection but quickly sliding to other things.

The bodily nature too presses the soul down. The body which is corrupted weigheth down the soul, and the earthly habitation presseth down the mind that museth upon many things (Wisd. ix, 15). The soul is hampered by the affections of the sensitive part, which drag it down to things below; and the agitations of the imagination fill it with phantasms, drawing the mind away from its fixed gaze on God. Hence even those most fervent in contemplation find that they cannot long sustain the pure elevation of the mind, but are compelled to return from the heights to their ordinary manner of acting.

### Article XVII

*On the causes of weakness and instability of the human mind for contemplation*

*Out of the depths I have cried to thee, O Lord: Lord, hear my voice.* (Ps. cxxix, 1–2)

There are six principal causes of the human mind's weakness and instability for contemplation.

The first is its distance from God and from the divine works of God, from whose full knowledge the soul falls short. Nay, for the contemplation of the Deity and of separated intellects it is as the eye of an owl to the splendour of the sun, according to the Philosopher. For God holy and adorable, by the superinfinite and most resplendent brightness of his uncreated light, beats back, reverberates and as it were blinds the gaze of the mind as to complete comprehension.

The second cause is the soul's very nature, which (as was said) stands at the farthest remove from the divine supersimple mind in the order of intellectual natures. By its own nature it is most wandering, defective and unstable; hence it is not fixed in one consideration and affection, but quickly slides to other things.

The third cause is the corruption and heaviness of the body, which weighs down the mind and hampers its upward flight, filling it with phantasms from the senses.

The fourth is the restlessness of the imagination, whose phantasms bring distraction to the mind.

The fifth is the multiplicity of the soul's powers and their operations: for while the soul is intent on the operation of one power, it is frequently diminished and impeded by the acts of the other powers — though sometimes the operations of the sensitive part serve contemplation, in so far as intellectual cognition takes its beginning from the sensitive.

The sixth is the sublimity of contemplation itself, which, being proper to the first and eternal mind, in some measure surpasses the soul's capacity, so that the soul is brought to it with difficulty and easily cast down from it.

### Article XVIII

*Whether the active life continues in the homeland, and whether it befits God and the angels*

*God is not the God of the dead, but of the living.* (Matt. xxii, 32)

The holy doctors Augustine and Gregory, and others in common agreement, say that the active life ends with the present way of pilgrimage and does not remain in the homeland; from which it seems to follow that it is not present in the divine mind nor in the angelic spirits. This appears also from the words of the Philosopher in the tenth book of the Ethics, who asserts that only speculative happiness befits the gods.

Yet there are objections to this. According to Dionysius, the higher angels purify, illuminate and perfect the lower; Christ as man illuminates all the Blessed; and God most truly teaches them all. The governance of the material world is administered through the angels (Augustine). Angels work miracles, restrain demons, offer prayers to God and procure divine grace for men (Gregory). Furthermore God each day creates rational souls, justifies sinners, transubstantiates bread and wine, and governs the whole world. And the ecclesiastical hierarchy is governed by the angelic hierarchy.

Some respond that when the works of the active life are referred to contemplation, they belong to the contemplative life; but this does not seem sufficient, since prelates who refer all their external works to contemplation are still said to lead the active life. The better answer is that as the contemplative life befits God and the Blessed in a more sublime manner than it befits pilgrims, so too the active life, freed from every imperfection, befits them — not so that one life hinders the other, nor so that they prepare for the contemplative life through struggle against sins, but because their action flows from the fullness of contemplation. The words of the saints saying that the active life does not remain in the homeland are to be understood of the works of the active life that pertain to the moral virtues and to the corporal works of mercy. Aristotle too removes this kind of active life from the gods.

### Article XIX

*How we are led to contemplation by the threefold way*

*Create a clean heart in me, O God: and renew a right spirit within my bowels.* (Ps. l, 12)

God sublime and blessed, being the fountain of purity and holiness, essentially holy and infinite holiness itself, requires a pure mind and loves what is holy. Nothing else makes us hateful to God and alien or removed from his splendour and grace but sin. Sins, as Isaiah speaks, divide between God and us (Is. lix, 2); and they also, as Jeremiah testifies, prevent good from coming to us (Jer. v, 25), that is, they render us unworthy of God's grace. Hence throughout his works Augustine confessed that by dissimilarity alone we are distant from God. Yet even by the dissimilarity of nature and condition as creatures we are distant from God — but this does not cause displeasure, aversion or contempt in the divine mind toward us, as the dissimilarity of sin does; for God the maker loves nature however fallen, but hates fault, and on account of it detests and despises the wicked.

Therefore one who desires the quiet of contemplation, the most bright visions of divine things, to draw near to God, to please him and to be united with him, must take his beginning from the purgative way. The purgative way is the soul's effort and study, by which as through a road and a certain medium it tends toward the cleansing of the heart from passions, ignorances, errors and sins, by devoting itself to penitential acts, subjecting also the body to the spirit and the sensitive part to reason. This is not to be conformed to this world, but to put off the old man with his acts (Rom. xii, 2; Col. iii), and to abstain from carnal desires and all vain affections, which war against the soul (I Pet. ii, 11).

When, then, the soul has been so cleansed, it is capable of the splendour and ray of saving wisdom, which does not enter a malicious soul (Wisd. i, 4), but whose seat and throne is the soul of the just. The illuminative way is the occupation, effort and study of our mind in the contemplation of divine things, in the speculation of spiritual, supernatural and celestial goods, in the participation of divine illumination, in the receiving of radiant, supernatural, created and infused wisdom: by whose light, clarified, the mind gazes on divine things, and becomes the bridal chamber of the Bridegroom, the resting-place of Christ, the throne and repose of the exalted, ever-living and adorable God. Nay, since God is naturally, essentially and infinitely good, the sun of understanding, and is by essence the exemplary, above-good, most communicable, most liberal, wholly infinite wisdom, penetrating through essential and most intimate inflowing into all minds: it is plain that he teaches the mind purified from passions and sins most sweetly of all things, so that it rejoices and testifies with abundant thanks: I will bless the Lord, who hath given me understanding (Ps. xv, 7).

Finally, when the soul has been so reformed in the sensitive appetite and adorned in the intellect, it is now apt to enter the third way, which is called perfective or unitive, consisting in this: that the mind, by contemplating divine things, is vehemently kindled by love, made wholly fervent, inflamed and fiery — enkindled in the immense fire of the Deity through a certain inflowing of the spark; and thus, transcending and abandoning all created things, forgetting itself and all things, is sincerely, entirely and most freely borne, dissolved, melted, transformed and absorbed into God through the most ardent affection of burning charity toward him. This is the unitive way, binding the mind to God most inwardly and above-inwardly and supernaturally — in which consists that mystical theology described by the great Dionysius, which is the most secret super-mental conversation of the mind with God and the above-mental vision, which is consummated in the apex of the affective and works ecstasy — of which, if the Holy Spirit shall grant it, there will be treatment below.

Finally, each of the three ways includes and contains another to some degree. The third presupposes the first and second, and the second presupposes the first. Similarly the second includes the third to some degree: since the knowledge of divine things does not deserve to be called contemplation unless it is formed by the fire of love. But the first also has something of the second and third mingled in it. For how does one who purges himself from sins and reforms passions, without the affection of charity and the actual intuition of true wisdom, consider, beseech and strive to be reconciled to God who has been offended by him?

### Article XX

*That the active or ethical must precede the theoretical; and how the moral virtues pertain to the contemplative life*

*And thou shalt love the Lord thy God with thy whole heart, and with thy whole soul, and with thy whole mind, and with thy whole strength.* (Mark xii, 30)

The actual and ethical or moral science is called the science that deals with the moderation of the passions and the stilling of exterior disquiets, so that the end of this science is the work, not mere consideration. Hence, as the Philosopher testifies, we examine what virtue is not finally so that we may know, but rather that we may become virtuous. And he confesses in the second book of the Ethics that for the moral virtues, merely knowing has little power, but action is necessary.

Hence, according to the same, the moral virtues are ordered to active or political happiness: for it belongs to them to bridle the passions and to rectify exterior operations.

Thus these virtues pertain to the active life essentially and directly, to the contemplative life dispositorially: since they remove the obstacles to contemplation — namely the violence of the passions and the disquiet of occupations, by which the soul is drawn away from attention to spiritual and intelligible things — and make the mind more capable of greater grace. For the passions do not merely impede the perfection of contemplation when they actually arise and are present; but the very deformity or proneness of the soul to immoderation of passions and vices makes the mind less loveable to God, less like him and less near, and consequently less receptive of grace.

Therefore one who desires to please God most singularly, and wishes that his works be most gratefully accepted by God, must apply himself most fully to the reformation of the passions. It is certain that the acts and exercises of the perfect please God far more sublimely than those of persons prone to passions and vices, even though the latter accomplish promptly and pleasurably what the former accomplish with difficulty and pain.

Therefore, according to our illustrious father John Cassian, actual or ethical science ought effectively to precede the theoretical — that is, the sapiential and contemplative speculation of divine things. And as St Gregory teaches, the active life ought to precede the contemplative. He says in the sixth book of the Morals: Whoever despises earthly desires, whoever stretches himself through the works of the active life, it does not suffice him to do great things externally, unless he can also penetrate internal things through contemplation. For by the name of sepulchre the contemplative life is signified, which buries us as dead to the world, dividing us entirely from all the actions of the world; as Paul said to some: *You are dead; and your life is hid with Christ in God* (Col. iii, 3).

Lastly, Prosper in the first book on the contemplative life says: The follower of the contemplative life should read God's precepts indefatigably, fulfil them efficaciously; remote from the clamour of secular affairs, ardently think upon those things by which to enkindle his soul in desire of future reward; watch over spiritual studies by which he may daily become better; love holy leisure, in which he exercises the business of his soul. Let him reckon the world dead to himself, and show himself crucified to the allurements of the flattering world. Never, not for a moment, turning aside from the consideration of heavenly goods to come, let him look back at earthly things; let him fix the gaze of his mind uninterruptedly on that to which he desires to arrive.

It appears from these things that the mind must first be purged of all disorderly affection toward sensible and earthly things, so that it does not love, desire, delight in, presume, fear or be saddened inordinately; but all affections of this kind must be moderated according to the judgement of right reason and subjected to the divine law, so that one is neither more nor less affected or moved by external, carnal and perishable things than is expedient for the soul, for true happiness, and for the honour and glory of the divine majesty. Then, freed from carnal concupiscence, the mind is to be raised to the contemplative summit.

### Article XXI

*On the exercise of the soul in the purgative way*

*Wash me yet more from my iniquity, and cleanse me from my sin.* (Ps. l, 4)

Since in sin the sinner prefers to the highest and unchangeable good — to the most glorious, all-powerful and adorable God — a vain and perishable, carnal, sensible, earthly or temporal good, which certainly pertains to the contempt of the Deity and the greatest injury to God (so much the greater and graver, as God is better, more loveable and more honourable than any creature), hence first of all it is necessary that for this contempt and belittling of God the sinner humble himself with his whole heart, despise himself, hold himself in contempt, count himself worthy of every confusion and punishment, and devoutly exalt and magnify God above all things.

Again, since in sin the wicked person delighted in vain and perishable things, who should have delighted in the Creator alone, therefore secondly it is required that for such libidinous, disorderly and vicious delight he grieve heartily, so that the greatness of sorrow corresponds to the magnitude of the fault. And he ought to have greater sorrow for sin than for any temporal loss or bodily harm, by as much as the evil of fault is worse than the evil of punishment, and by as much as it is more unhappy to have lost the highest and immense good than temporal things.

Moreover, since punishment is owed to fault, by which fault is ordered and corrected, hence thirdly it is necessary that he willingly sustain worthy and due punishments for his faults, that as much as he glorified himself in delights, so much torment and mourning he inflict on himself.

Therefore one who aspires to touch the summit of contemplation — since he does not merely desire to appease God in any fashion, but aims at his familiarity, his taste, his embrace and his kiss — ought to place himself before the Most High with most profound humility, heartfelt sorrow and all readiness to make satisfaction and amendment; and twice or three times each day, when he has the best leisure, especially in the evening or at night, in a secret and quiet place, thus accuse himself before God, with mind and gaze directed to heaven, nay to the God of heaven:

*O most merciful, supremely loveable, supremely desirable and in all infinite sweetness most sweet Lord Jesus Christ, for the sake of your immense goodness, charity and piety, through all that you have done and borne for my salvation, through your blessed incarnation, your most holy life in this world and your most bitter Passion, through the blessed Virgin Mary and the prayers and merits of all the Saints, forgive me, most unworthy and most grievous sinner, and pour your most loving grace into me, by which I may henceforth avoid all sins, flee whatever displeases you, and never henceforth turn my mind away from you, my God.*

Then let him think of some graver sins, and for each individually and for all collectively let him grieve and sigh in his mind as much as the Lord shall give; let him be grieved that he does not grieve enough, and pray for the grace of worthy sorrow. And then with knees bent, or whole body prostrated on the ground, with hands joined or extended in the form of a cross, let him pray God for the full remission of all his sins in words such as these:

*O omnipotent God, most benign and eternal, most merciful Lord Jesus Christ, my Creator and Saviour of my soul, behold I am that most vile and most wicked sinner, utterly abominable, contemptible and most miserable, made worse than any brute, who so many times and so gravely, boldly and shamelessly dishonoured your majesty, transgressed your precepts, not caring for or attending to them, nay violating them heard and known; I offended your goodness, holiness and charity, turned my heart away from you my God, committed these and these sins, omitted so many and such great goods, was most ungrateful for your benefits, abused your gifts.*

Thereafter let him think of some graver sins, and for each individually and for all in general let him grieve and sigh in his mind as much as the Lord shall give; let him not grieve that he does not grieve sufficiently, and implore divine grace for worthy sorrow.

This devotional exercise of the purgative way is to be continued with great and intense attention for a time, until the sinner feels that a fuller grace is being infused into him, making him fit for the exercises of the illuminative way. Yet discretion must be used, lest through excessive exercise the head be weakened.

### Article XXII

*How those who aspire to the summit of contemplation ought to exercise themselves daily; and on what is especially necessary for them*

*Seek ye therefore first the kingdom of God, and his justice, and all these things shall be added unto you.* (Matt. vi, 33)

As blessed Bernard testifies, whatever you offer to the Holy Spirit, neglecting the things to which you are bound, is unwelcome to him. Therefore one who desires to attain more perfect things and the heights of contemplation must first of all most diligently fulfil, know and observe those things to which he is bound — for instance, the divine precepts, and if he is a religious, the Rule or statutes of his Order. He must always obey God, the Church and right reason according to the demand of his state; and since accursed is he who does the work of God negligently (Jer. xlviii, 10), let him minister to the great, immense and holy God with reverence and fear. Let him recite his Hours or prayers distinctly, attentively and devoutly; let him always think of God as present, looking upon all his works, thoughts, affections and words and judging them; and let him be ashamed to comport himself fruitlessly, dishonestly and blameably before the presence of the divine eternal and most awe-inspiring countenance; with burning zeal let him desire to honour God in all things and to be honoured by all.

Furthermore, since sanctifying grace is the primary principle of all meritorious action, placing the essence of the rational soul in the supernatural and divine being fit for merit, and immediately perfecting it and making it acceptable to God, he must continually pray with heartfelt affection that the most loving God may daily preserve, increase and perfect this grace in him, so that by this he may each day become more efficacious and ready for all the goods of eternal life.

Moreover let him pray that God may most graciously perfect and illuminate his faith in the reasons for believing through the gift of understanding, whose office is to penetrate what belongs to faith, understanding the reasons for believing — in which a great part of contemplation consists. And since the Most High is not to be approached presuming on one's own merits, but through the merits of the saints whom one cannot equal by one's own (according to Job v, 1), let him earnestly pray with burning desire that God through the merits of the saints may grant him what he cannot obtain by his own.

Furthermore, since charity is the incitement, form and consummation of contemplation, let him pray most devoutly and incessantly that God kindle his mind with the fervour of charity, fill it, occupy it unceasingly and possess it entirely, and drive from him all disorderly love.

Moreover, since the gifts make the mind well and readily movable, docile and governable by the Holy Spirit — without whose special gift no one ascends to the summit of contemplation — let him pray most affectionately to the immense munificence of the most merciful God that the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit may daily be strengthened and perfected in him, so that through these he may most frequently be moved and inflamed to most bright and fiery contemplations by the Holy Spirit. Especially let him pray for the complement of the gift of wisdom, whose act is called above all contemplation (as was said). For this wisdom is a supernatural, created, infused and most worthy gift, by which the mind obtains a right and savoury judgement of God and divine things through connatural conformity of affection to them, not through bare, formless and mere knowledge.

Furthermore, since in contemplation the human mind ascending high above itself is prone to falling, so for this it is greatly necessary to have profound and firm humility as a foundation. And indeed, the more it humbles itself under the most magnificent majesty of the Creator, the more sublimely it ascends to his contemplation. Also, lest through the flight and freedom of contemplation it be relaxed to vanities, it must immovably ground itself in the chaste, holy and filial fear of God.

Finally, since self-love — the private, self-turned and improper love by which the rational creature loves itself in itself, willing, desiring and seeking its own comfort and delight in itself — is the origin of all vices, one must pray God urgently and labour strenuously for the eradication of this most pernicious love, so that the one wishing to make progress may love himself in God, loving God above all things purely and fervently, and other things in him or in so far as they help toward union of the mind with him. For thus all adversities, hardships and difficulties will be borne equanimously, since they will be seen to serve the remission of sins, the obtaining of grace, the attainment of blessedness, the imitation of Christ; and similarly vigils, abstinences, disciplines, solitude, silence, self-denial, and the other penitential acts and virtuous labours will be observed with joy for God's honour.

Let one who ardently desires to gain the gift of contemplation do these things strenuously each day, until he rejoices that he has obtained them.

### Article XXIII

*Confirmation of what has been said from the words of St Gregory; where it is shown that not all are fit for contemplation, and that a salutary fear sometimes arises from contemplation*

*Happy is the man who is always fearful: but he that is hardened in mind, shall fall into evil.* (Prov. xxviii, 14)

As Gregory teaches in the sixth book of the Morals, the temperaments of souls are very different from one another. Some are so given to idle ease, or naturally so quiet, that if the labour of occupation receives them, they succumb at the very beginning of the work; and some are so restless that if they have leisure from labour, they suffer the worse, because they endure the more violent tumults of the heart, the more freely time is given them for thought. Hence it is necessary that a quiet mind should not spread itself to immoderate exercise, nor a restless mind narrow itself to the study of contemplation. For often those who could contemplate God while at rest fell when pressed by occupations; and often those who could live well occupied in human uses were destroyed by the sword of their own quiet.

Hence it comes about that certain restless spirits, seeking more in contemplating than they can grasp, break out to perverse doctrines. Therefore it is necessary that whoever hastens to the study of contemplation first subtly question himself how much he loves. For the power of love is the engine of the mind, which, extracting it from the world, lifts it to the heights. First, then, let him examine whether, seeking the highest things, he loves; whether, loving, he fears; whether he knows how either to comprehend the unknown by loving, or to venerate what is not comprehended by fearing. For in contemplation, if love does not excite the mind, the torpor of its lukewarmness darkens it; if fear does not weigh it down, the senses carry it through vanities to the fog of error; and when the closed gate of secrets is opened to it too slowly, it is driven the farther from it by its own presumption.

Hence when the law was received, the people were forbidden access to the mountain: namely that those who in their weak minds desire earthly things should not presume at the sublime. For a beast touches the mountain when a mind subjected to irrational desires raises itself to the heights of contemplation.

From these words of blessed Gregory it appears that those who are naturally of quiet and modest spirit are more apt for the contemplative life. Hence let them use well their natural gifts, and let them continually pray the Lord to perfect through the gifts of grace the gifts of nature in them, so that they may persevere in contemplation with holy discretion. Moreover, if any who are naturally restless have been placed in a state of perfection, they ought not on account of natural inaptitude to cease altogether from contemplation, but to pray to God incessantly for the grace of amendment and reformation of their defects, and to do a good violence to themselves by attending to solitude and quiet, prayer and meditation, until the increase of grace supplies what natural inaptitude lacks.

Gregory further says: But among these things it must be known that often love excites even sluggish minds to work, and fear restrains restless ones in contemplation. For the anchor of the heart is the weight of fear. Hence the restless mind is often shaken by the wave of thoughts, but is held back by the chains of its discipline.

Furthermore, the more clearly one contemplates the majesty, holiness and justice of the divine nature, the more limpidly one sees one's own smallness, defectiveness and contemptibleness; and through this one humbles oneself more to oneself, and perceives and ponders more fully the enormity of one's iniquities. Hence Gregory protests in the fifth book of the Morals: The more the uplifted mind considers the power of God, the more does the subdued mind fear his strength. For the human mind, the more it is elevated and sees what is eternal, the more gravely does it tremble with fear on account of its temporal acts: because it sees itself the more truly guilty, the more it perceives how far it has departed from that light that shines above it. And so it comes about that, illuminated, it fears the more because it sees more how far it departs from the rule of truth; and its very progress itself shakes it with grave dread, whereas before it saw nothing as if more securely. Thus in the contemplation of God's greatness it shudders with awe, and returning to itself, loves him the more closely, whose marvellous sweetness, which it could not endure, it scarcely tasted under imperfect vision.

### Article XXIV

*On the praiseworthy industries by which the soul is disposed toward the theoretical summit of contemplation*

*The prayer of him that humbleth himself shall pierce the clouds: and till it come nigh he will not be comforted.* (Ecclus. xxxv, 21)

Although the most merciful Creator of the universe, out of his supernatural and most loving kindness, anticipates, touches, changes, affects and converts some utterly unprepared, undeserving and unworthy ones, even when they are intent on their own wickedness (as is plain in Paul the Apostle), none the less speaking regularly and commonly, for the reception of grace it is required that a person do what is in him, adapting and preparing himself for it: which when he has done, God forthwith grants the grace. Hence in the Scriptures the Holy Spirit urges us to this preparation, according to the word of Zechariah: *Return to me, and I will return to you, saith the Lord* (Zach. i, 3); and James: *Draw nigh to God, and he will draw nigh to you* (James iv, 8). God thus grants grace to those acting thus according to the capacity and diligence of their study.

To accomplish this certain industries contribute. The first is that the servant of Christ should not cease from his customary exercises of devotion and from the continual imploring of divine goodness, whether he feels actual fervour or devotion or not. Nay, if he perceives himself to be without devotion or cold, and unable to be moved to spiritual things, let him strive to overcome himself — crying to God with sighing, sobbing and the beating of his breast, and casting himself before his presence on the ground, saying: *Do not leave me, O Lord my God, depart not from me; attend unto my help, O God of my salvation* (Ps. xxxvi, 22–23).

The second industry concerns the posture of the body: namely to place himself before God as he thinks more effective or fitting for exciting, preserving and perfecting devotion and contemplation. I do not think a universal rule can be given here, although some assert that for contemplating the more fitting posture is sitting, others standing, others kneeling. None the less the holy Fathers in the desert used to pray and contemplate standing with hands raised to God. But I think that for one and the same person it is now more fitting to pray or contemplate standing, now sitting, now prostrating oneself on the ground and spreading one's arms in the form of a cross, according to how one is disposed in various ways in heart and body. None the less, all else being equal, for contemplating I think the position of standing is more fitting, so that with the elevation of the soul and its interior eyes, also the body and its eyes may be raised to heaven. At times, however, it is not expedient to keep the bodily eyes open — as when the pure fixing of the understanding on God is impeded by the apprehension of sensible things.

The third industry concerns the place. For apart from what is to be sought in the church from the ordering of the holy Fathers, the more fitting place for devoting oneself to prayer and contemplation is solitude, or a secret and quiet place, in which a person can place and pour out his heart before God with all confidence without shame or the noise and fear of others. Christ in the Gospel went up into the mountain alone to pray (Matt. xiv); and at the time of the Passion he was separated even from his three most familiar disciples by about a stone's throw (Luke xxii, 41) — not out of his own need, but for our instruction. He himself also in the Gospel: *When thou shalt pray, enter into thy chamber, and having shut the door, pray to thy Father in secret* (Matt. vi, 6).

The fourth industry concerns time. The nocturnal hours are reckoned more fitting for the elevation of the mind, and also when food has been digested. None the less in the diurnal exercises requiring special devotion — such as celebration of Mass, communion, recitation of the Hours, hearing of Mass — a person ought to extend himself to singular devotion, especially in the celebration, and when going to bed and reciting Compline. It is also expedient that he continue each work of virtue in its proper time, so that the act, turned into habit, is practised more delightfully.

The fifth industry is that, humbly pondering his own unworthiness, he turn to some of the saints, especially to the Virgin Mother, and the saints whom he venerates with special honour, earnestly beseeching them, that what he cannot obtain by his own merits, he may obtain through theirs.

The sixth is that, according to the apostolic teaching, he do all things decently and in order (I Cor. xiv, 40), doing in its place and time what is at hand to be done, whether in interior or exterior exercises of devotion, or in the body's necessities. For thus, conducting himself in an orderly fashion and directing himself on every side with the light of discretion, he will not be disturbed.

The seventh is to wisely observe the natural temperament of his constitution, and to fight more strenuously against those defects and vices to which he is naturally prone, with unceasing imploration of divine help; and also to give himself manfully to those virtues to which he is well disposed, lest he be found abusing the Creator's gifts.

The eighth is that he diligently consider his vocation and his profession, if he is a religious, every day; avoid curious things and things not pertaining to him; use food, drink, sleep and clothing with temperance.

The ninth is that when he feels himself wearying in any one devotional or contemplative exercise, he turn to another: so that if he cannot be constantly fixed in the contemplation of the Deity, let him now think of the glory of the Blessed, now of God's strict judgement, the pains of hell and purgatory, the brevity, vanity and fallacy of the present life, the uncertainty and bitterness of death, the histories of the Saints, and especially the life of the Lord Jesus.

The tenth is that he always with sincere and intimate affection desire to please God, and with compassionate and fervent prayer to succour those who are afflicted and living ill, and similarly to intercede for the departed.

Let one ardently panting for contemplation exercise himself diligently in these things every day; and he will experience in a short time that God in the abundance of his kindness exceeds both the merits and the vows of supplicants, provided he prays with filial affection and full perseverance.

Finally, this too is to be added: that, according to the teaching of Cassian, he impress some sacred thing firmly on the mind, always gazing and intending it; and whenever he sees himself removed from it, let him grieve and turn back the gaze of the mind to it, striving to persevere in the same. So let this be the elevation of the mind to God, or actual union with God; let him always desire and intend it, and persist in it with all his powers. And when he feels his heart distracted or uselessly occupied, let him remember his intention — namely union with God — and resume it. And so that he may be able to resume it more readily, let him always accustom himself to revolve in his mind some devout verse from the Psalms, and praying, join the heart to God by saying it, such as: *Create a clean heart in me, O God* (Ps. l, 12); or something similar more pleasing to his affection, which by pronouncing it he may be affected toward God and remember him. And indeed, to more effectively curb the wandering of the heart, as often as he feels himself distracted or vilely occupied, let him inflict some sensible punishment on himself, pressing a finger firmly, twisting with the nails. At times also it helps to cast off distractions if he changes his bodily position or place a little, raises his hands to God, looks upon the form of the Crucified.

And let him accustom himself always to addressing God with mind and intention in words such as these: O Lord God, when shall I bind you in most sweet love? when shall I love you ardently with strictest love? when shall I cleave to you totally? If he has taken the habit of saying these words from the heart, he will feel himself in a short time changed in mind, turned from the world and moved toward God. Moreover let him frequently raise himself to mental and fervent prayers, in which he may praise and glorify God as greatly as he can, and feel and despise himself as most vile, saying: O God supremely most worthy, have mercy on me, most unworthy; O most blessed God, help me, most miserable; O most holy God, be always with me, most impious; etc. — as the anointing of the Holy Spirit shall teach, if he has observed what has been set out. Nay, progressing, he will begin to address, praise and embrace God most ardently by his names alone, saying: O most benign, most sweet, most loveable God; etc.

### Article XXV

*That prayer, memory of the Lord's Passion, and psalmody or divine praise are the best paths to attaining the summit of contemplation*

*Ask, and it shall be given you: seek, and you shall find: knock, and it shall be opened to you.* (Matt. vii, 7)

The prayer of one who humbles himself penetrates the heavens (Ecclus. xxxv, 21). And so, the more the sublimity of contemplation surpasses the inclination of our intellect, the more urgently must one pray for it. And we know that a fervent and humble, reasonable and unfailing prayer God does not despise, although he sometimes usefully delays on account of our better good. Finally, in praying for this, one must not speak much, but fiery words must be poured forth in mind, or also with the mouth.

Therefore one who wishes to grow in the love of God and the grace of contemplating must pray in all the exercises already prescribed, nay in every hour; and the more faithfully and urgently we pray, the more fully shall we be heard.

Furthermore, as the Apostle testifies, if we suffer with Christ, we shall also be glorified with him (Rom. viii, 17). That we may therefore be partakers of the grace of Christ in the present and of glory in the future, let us remember his Passion daily more affectionately and compassionately as we are able, and most devoutly invoke Christ for the grace of doing this. Let us consider what, how great and what manner of things the only-begotten Son of God, true God, King of glory, Lord of majesty, did and bore for our salvation, what most ignominious death he chose to suffer for us — in which appears how great is the gravity of fault; how great is divine justice, which preferred that Christ suffer thus rather than that sins go unpunished; how great also is God's love toward us.

For the Passion of Christ is the most resplendent mirror and the most illustrious book, in which we can learn all virtues and the perfection of all virtues: where we learn to despise the delicacies of food and drink, remembering that he himself was refreshed for our sake with vinegar and gall. If we consider how he was mocked for our sake with a white robe and a purple garment, we shall despise the most vain ornaments of clothing. If we weigh how he prayed for those who crucified him, we shall love our adversaries. If we look upon how, before Caiaphas and Pilate, most gravely and most falsely accused, he was peacefully silent, we shall bear patiently the injuries of words. If we ponder how he obeyed the Father unto death, we shall embrace obedience. If we weigh how, most ignominiously trampled and suspended between thieves, he bore it humbly, we shall preserve humility. If we recall that throughout the most bitter Passion he remained most meek, and, being spat upon, struck with fists, and suffering other unspeakable evils, never had the least movement of indignation or anger — we shall aspire to meekness. Let us therefore daily revolve these things in our heart and press the image of the Crucified most firmly upon our heart; and when at the time of divine service or at other times the disorderly affection or memory of any thing or person arises in the mind, let us immediately raise the heart to the gaze of the Crucified, and thereby fly to the contemplation of the Deity and be fixed there.

Finally, revolving in the soul the torments of Christ's Passion, let us at the same time weigh who, of what character and how great is he who bore them, and with how great love, for whom too and of what manner, namely for us most poor and most vile. Considering these things, therefore, let us not neglect ourselves.

Finally, the most celebrated work of the contemplative life is to persevere with a pure mind in hymns, psalms or praises of the most sublime and glorious Creator. This drives away demons, most abundantly merits the desired grace, and is a prelude to the celestial life.

Let no moment of time pass the contemplator without fruit, but let him continually turn over something divine in his mind — praying, giving thanks, chanting, meditating or contemplating, even at the hour of refreshment and labour and in his bed. Every carnal and base consolation he must abjure, and with most eager mind seek the light of wisdom.

Let him also aptly note that (as Richard says, and before him his master Hugh of St Victor) for the knowledge of sacred Scripture, for the contemplation of divine things, for the obtaining of saving wisdom, inward compunction is more necessary than deep investigation, sighs than arguments, frequent groaning than copious argumentation, tears than sentences, prayer than reading. Of this Cassian speaks more fully, teaching how one who wishes to make progress in the knowledge of the Scriptures and in the speculation of heavenly things must before all things strive for purity of heart, inward compunction and union of the mind with God. Hence some of the elect are read to have been led to knowledge of the Scriptures and possession of wisdom by praying rather than studying. None the less, study or reading of divine volumes is not thereby condemned; but the caution is that one should conduct himself in an orderly fashion, and first be cleansed from vices, and rely more on the help of grace than on one's own talent.

### Article XXVI

*On the species of contemplation*

*How great is the multitude of thy sweetness, O Lord, which thou hast hidden for them that fear thee!* (Ps. xxx, 20)

Up to this point contemplation and the contemplative life have been treated in general; now it remains to descend to the species of contemplation. And although the Catholic doctors and the saints assign various species of contemplation (as will be shown below), I shall first introduce one distinction of these species which seems for the present reasonable and fitting; then the species or degrees of contemplation according to the documents of others will be treated.

There are then five species of contemplation. The first and highest is the supernatural contemplation of the most blessed Trinity. The second is the natural contemplation of the superessential and simple Deity. The third is the speculation of the assumed humanity of the Word. The fourth is the intuition of the gifts of grace. The fifth is the consideration of the gifts of nature.

Furthermore, contemplation of God is twofold: one by way of position or affirmation — and so the divine Dionysius treats of the most adorable and most glorious Trinity in the book on the Divine Characteristics, and of the supersimple Deity itself in the book on the Divine Names and in the book on Symbolic Theology. The other contemplation of God is by way of the negation or removal of all beings — of which contemplation the Theologian treats in the book on Mystical Theology.

There is moreover a threefold theology. The first is symbolic, in which God is treated as known through the properties and likenesses of bodily things, and is named by the names of such things — namely lion, stone, sun, lamb. The second is proper theology, in which God is treated as known and named from his nobler, intellectual and spiritual effects — namely good, being, life, wisdom, understanding. Of this is the book on the Divine Names. The third theology is more sublime — namely mystical — in which God is treated as he is known through the negation of all beings, and in the most bright darkness, by the above-mental, fiery, experimental and most ardent love of deifying charity, through the elevation of the mind above all created things, and likewise through immediate and most certain ecstatic union with the most high God. And this is the highest and most excellent species of contemplation possible to this life, and in a way a mean between the contemplation of the homeland and the contemplation of God in the way by affirmation. Of this supernatural and mystical species of contemplation, there is intention to speak a little (if God shall grant) at the end of this work.

Since therefore the most blessed Trinity is known by faith alone, we rightly call its contemplation supernatural — though to that knowledge of the Trinity which is called scholastic, one can attain by natural power. Nay, many who are sordid with mortal vices know many subtle things to say of the Trinity: whose knowledge, since it is formless, lean and bare, is unworthy of the name of contemplation, being insipid, not anointed, but cold.

### Article XXVII

*On the first species of contemplation — namely the supernatural contemplation of the most blessed Trinity*

*Holy, holy, holy Lord God of Sabaoth, the whole earth is full of his glory.* (Is. vi, 3)

Of what befits God, some things are known by natural reason and became known to the philosophers: as that God is eternal, perfect, unchangeable, infinite, pure act, a separate mind, the fontal cause of all things. Those things pertaining to the unity of the divine essence can be known by natural light, from the nature, property, governance and order of created things: since, as the Apostle testifies, the invisible things of God are clearly seen from the creation of the world, as also his everlasting power and divinity (Rom. i, 20). But some of those things transcend all human reason and are known and held by faith alone, through divine revelation — such as what we believe of the most happy Trinity, whose knowledge was supernaturally revealed to the holy Apostles and Prophets; leaning on whose faith and writings, we most faithfully believe, knowing how ineffably, eminently and in manifold ways the true and holy God marvellously glorified them, showing by innumerable means, signs and miracles that they and their posterity (who followed them in faith, charity and morals) belonged to him.

Since therefore the most blessed Trinity is known by faith alone, we rightly call its contemplation supernatural. One who aspires and girds himself to ascend to the contemplation of the most excellent Trinity must first note that, the higher, more incomprehensible and more pure the Trinity itself is, the more abundant the reverence, the more profound the humility and the more sincere the cleanness of mind with which one must ascend to its contemplation. As Augustine says in the first book on the Trinity: Nowhere is error more dangerous, nowhere is anything sought with more labour, nowhere is anything found more fruitfully, than where the unity of the Trinity is sought. And again: This highest good is seen only by most purified minds. Hence wanderings, disquiets and phantasms must be driven far away, and with a collected, loving and fearful mind must one approach, with the truth of faith always supposed as the immovable and most certain foundation.

Striving therefore to extend the gaze of the mind to that most pure light of the gracious Trinity, and leaving behind all created bodily things, with a purified and simplified intellect, and also prepared by preceding devotion and prayers — let the contemplator see how our God, the true and highest God, is truly one and triune, so that the trinity of persons in no way detracts from the unity and simplicity of essence, nor does the simplicity and unity of essence wipe away or diminish the personal distinction.

The Word of God the Father is the perfect and natural image of God the Father, in whom God the Father fully contains, represents and shines back all that he is. Therefore this Word is not only the perfect and natural image of God the Father, but also the exemplar and reason of every created being. Hence this Word, or the Son of God, is called the archetypal world and the uncreated art full of the rational natures. Hence Augustine says in the seventh book on the Trinity: The Father always and immutably speaks himself through the Word equal to himself; and again in the fifteenth book on the Trinity: Speaking as it were himself, the Father begot the Word equal to himself in all things.

Furthermore this Word is rightly called the Son of God the Father, who proceeds immediately from his substance, according to the manner of likeness, in the identity of nature. For knowledge or understanding is accomplished through a certain assimilation, according to the Philosopher. Since therefore this Word emanates intellectually from the Father as his proper, full, natural and definitive image, it is certain that it is truly and properly the Son of God.


### Article XXVIII

*Fuller treatment of this first and most worthy species of contemplation*

*In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.* (John i, 1)

As the most illustrious and divine lord Bonaventure elegantly deduces, God is that simply best good than which nothing better can be thought; hence God cannot be thought not to be, for it is altogether better to be than not to be. Wherefore if he is thought not to be, something already thought contrary to what is his proper nature — just as if a man is thought not to be rational, which indeed cannot be thought if what man is be understood.

Furthermore, good is said to be that which is diffusive of its own goodness; hence the highest good is supremely diffusive of itself. But the most supreme and full diffusion cannot be unless it is actual and intrinsic, substantial and hypostatic, natural and voluntary, generous and necessary, unfailing and perfect. Such a diffusion is therefore to be ascribed to the highest, perfect and infinite good. And since the diffusion and emanation of creatures from God is but a point or centre in comparison with the immense goodness of God, beyond this one must confess in God an inward, full and perfect emanation and diffusion, in which the produced hypostasis is altogether equal to the producing Person. Which cannot be better accomplished than by the generation in which God eternally begets the beloved — namely the only Son — and likewise by the spiration in which the Father and Son together produce the co-beloved, the Holy Spirit: so that the generation of the Son is natural diffusion, since it is after the manner of nature, as splendour flows from light; and the procession of the Holy Spirit is after the manner of the will.

Moreover one must confess that both diffusions and emanations are actual, intrinsic, substantial, hypostatic, unfailing and perfect — and also necessary, so that they are not contingent nor can fail to be. For no greater diffusion can consist than that whereby the one diffusing communicates his whole self or whole nature to another. Hence if it is said that such diffusion does not befit God, the nature of the highest good will not befit him.

Let the contemplator therefore see through the communicability of the highest good that there must be posited a trinity of Persons — Father, Son and Holy Spirit — in which from supreme goodness follows supreme communicability; and from supreme communicability, supreme consubstantiality, because the whole essence is there communicated; and from supreme consubstantiality, supreme configurability or likeness or identity. From which it is further concluded that there is supreme coequality, coeternity and co-intimacy of the divine Persons: since they are in one another through circumincession, and each divine Person operates outwardly with the other, because of the altogether undivided substance, power and operation.

### Article XXIX

*That in the contemplation of the most holy Trinity wise and humble moderation is to be observed on account of the sublimity of the object*

*Seek ye the Lord, and be strengthened: seek his face evermore.* (Ps. civ, 4)

If, as the Philosopher testifies, our intellect stands in relation to the knowledge of separated substances as the eye of the owl to the vision of the sun's brightness, it is evident that from the complete contemplation of the most holy and uncircumscribable Trinity it falls short in infinite degree. For as the book of Wisdom says: We find with difficulty those things that are on earth, and we find with labour what is in our sight (Wisd. ix, 16). If therefore we are so darkened in acknowledging these inferior and baser effects of the most high Trinity, are we not incomparably weaker and more obscure in beholding, investigating and scrutinising the superexalted and immense sublimity of the Trinity itself?

Therefore when the devout contemplator in the contemplation of the most holy Trinity encounters something incomprehensible, and reflects that he is incited to immoderate scrutiny, let him respond with blessed Hilary, who says in his book on the Trinity: I know not, nor do I seek; yet I will console myself. The Archangels know it not, the Angels have not heard it, the ages do not contain it, the Prophet did not perceive it, the Apostle did not ask it, the Son himself did not publish it. Let the pain of complaint cease. Let no man think his understanding can attain the sacrament of the generation.

The human mind, therefore, the lowest in the orders of intellectual souls, falling infinitely short of the comprehension of the first mind, of the most holy Trinity, of the eternal and uncreated truth, when it sees in the contemplation of the most high Trinity whatever transcends its capacity, must not attempt in vain to dispute it, but, stable in most certain faith, must reverently commend it to the Holy Spirit, and giving thanks to God from the heart, must be content with what is granted to it.

### Article XXX

*On the incomprehensible things in the contemplation of the most adorable Trinity*

*Great is the Lord, and greatly to be praised: and of his greatness there is no end.* (Ps. cxliv, 3)

It is not necessary to lay open the incomprehensible things of God to simpler souls; but lest either the subtle or the simple vainly glory and think that they can comprehend the Almighty fully, something is to be touched briefly on the mysteries of the most sweetly delicious Trinity that transcend all minds.

Who can grasp how in the most simple God, who is pure unity, there is true, real and personal distinction; how three Persons are substantially the same and are personally distinguished, since in God there is but one simple essence, the same really as person; how the Son and Holy Spirit are truly produced, since they are really the same as the divine essence, which is not produced; how in pure act, namely God, there is active and passive generation; how in God there is true emanation without imperfection and dependence; how in three Persons there is numerically one nature; how the relations and properties are founded or consist in the divine essence, yet do not determine it; how in the Father, Son and Holy Spirit there is numerically the same intellect, and each perfectly understands himself, yet only the Father utters and begets the Word within himself; how it does not befit the Holy Spirit to produce within himself an offspring proportioned and equal to him, eternal and immense — since the goodness, fullness and perfection of the Father and of the Word are so great?

From the contemplation of these things one may rise to pious admiration, not to presumptuous discussion, pondering that as God eternal or his essence is entirely incomprehensible and exceeds our capacity infinitely, so too is his action, especially within: and therefore it is most foolish to wish to comprehend it or measure it or discuss it by human talent.

### Article XXXI

*That notwithstanding the incomprehensibility of what pertains to the first species of contemplation, we must always strive for fuller knowledge of them*

*Seek and you shall find.* (Matt. vii, 7)

Augustine says in the fifteenth book on the Trinity: God sought can be found. Why then is it said "seek his face evermore," unless because the one found is to be sought? For in this way the incomprehensible are to be sought, lest one who has been able to find and recognise how incomprehensible is what he sought should judge himself to have found nothing. Why then does he seek further, if he has found that what he seeks is incomprehensible, unless because he must not cease as long as he is making progress in the very inquiry of incomprehensible things, and the one who seeks so great a good is always becoming better — a good that is sought to be found the more sweetly, and found to be sought further?

And therefore the holy Apostle urges: *Growing always in the knowledge of God* (Col. i, 10); and: *I count all things as dung on account of the eminent knowledge of Christ Jesus* (Phil. iii, 8). And the Psalmist says: *Let the heart of them rejoice that seek the Lord: seek the Lord and be strengthened, seek his face evermore* (Ps. civ, 3–4).

The contemplator therefore, who wishes to strengthen the radiant light of saving wisdom in himself, must daily and continually implore the immense mercy of God, heeding that word of Wisdom: *Who shall know thy sense, O Lord, unless thou give wisdom and send thy Spirit from on high?* (Wisd. ix, 17).

### Articles XXXII–XXXVII

*On further aspects of the first species: the Trinity's perfections, the processions, and practice of this contemplation*

[Articles XXXII–XXXVII continue the development of the first species of contemplation of the most holy Trinity, treating the eternal processions of Son and Holy Spirit, the attributes of each Person, the mutual indwelling of the Persons, and the practical manner in which the contemplative soul is to raise itself to this most divine of all contemplations. Denis draws throughout on Dionysius, Augustine, Bonaventure, Aquinas and the tradition of the mystical theologians, and exhorts the contemplator to combine the most exact doctrinal faith with the deepest humility and the most ardent charity, approaching this contemplation always with purified intellect, composed and collected mind, and heart wholly set on God; and to bring the contemplation to its conclusion in praise, thanksgiving, and the most ardent self-surrender to the most Holy Trinity.]

### Articles XXXVIII–XLIV

*On the second species of contemplation — natural contemplation of the superessential and simple Deity*

*I am who am.* (Exod. iii, 14)

The second species of contemplation is the natural contemplation of the superessential and simple Deity: not that it is possible by purely natural power, but that the things here contemplated are knowable in principle by natural reason illuminated by faith, unlike the Trinity which is known only by revelation. This species concerns the attributes and perfections of the divine essence as such: God's infinity, eternity, immutability, simplicity, omnipotence, omniscience, omnipresence, justice, mercy, beauty, goodness, truth and love.

In this contemplation the soul considers how God is being itself, the first, supereminent, self-subsistent being from which all else derives; how in God essence and existence are one; how he is utterly simple, containing no composition of any kind; how he is immutable, wholly actual, entirely perfect. The soul contemplates God's eternity — not mere endless duration, but the simultaneously whole and perfect possession of unbounded life — and his immensity, by which he is wholly present everywhere. It considers God's omniscience, by which he knows all things, past, present and future, in one eternal and most simple act. It contemplates his providence, by which all creatures are governed toward their ends.

The soul also contemplates the divine beauty: for God is beauty itself, the cause of all created beauty, surpassing and containing it. It considers how all the perfections scattered and divided among creatures exist in God most eminently, simply and infinitely. By this contemplation the soul is lifted above all created beauty to the Beauty which has no image, above all created truth to the Truth which no falsehood can touch, above all created love to the Love which is its own object and its own act.

This second species also includes the contemplation of God known through the negation of created perfections — not that God lacks what we affirm of creatures, but that he transcends them infinitely and in a manner wholly beyond our comprehension. And in this apophatic ascent the soul learns to know God better by unknowing than by knowing, better by silence than by speech, better by surrender than by understanding.

These contemplations are to be practiced with the same humility, purity, faith and ardour of charity that govern the first species; and they open into one another, for to contemplate the divine unity is inseparable from faith in the Trinity, and to contemplate the divine perfections is inseparable from love of the God who is their living source.

### Article XLV

*On the third species of contemplation*

*And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, and we saw his glory.* (John i, 14)

The third species of contemplation is the speculation of the assumed humanity of the Word. Of this Augustine says in the seventh book on the City of God: We have from God a great sign of his love for us, in that though burdened and oppressed with sins, turned away from the contemplation of his light and blinded by the love of darkness, that is of iniquity, he did not altogether desert us, but sent us his Word, who is his only Son; by whose birth and Passion for us assuming flesh, we might recognise how much God prizes man, and by that singular sacrifice be cleansed from all sins, and by his Spirit shed abroad in our hearts in love, with all difficulties overcome, might come to eternal rest and the ineffable sweetness of his contemplation.

Therefore the contemplative soul, whose mind is illumined by the most clear faith and the gifts of the Holy Spirit, whose affective part is kindled by the most warm love of the Deity, and which has been purged from the fumes and fogs of passions and vices, and also by the singular grace of God tranquillised from cares, distractions and disquiets, approaches to the contemplation of Christ the man, or the incarnate Son of God and his most excellent humanity, with grateful, sapient and recollected mind.

First let it faithfully contemplate that the only-begotten Son of God, true God, equal to the eternal Father in all things, truly became man by taking on human nature — assuming it to himself, and immediately and hypostatically joining it to his divinity or proper personality, so that in him the human nature was assumed to the personal, uncreated and eternal being of the Word, and in him is truly subsistent, not having created and proper hypostatic being of its own. Therefore Christ, God and man, is one person only.

Let it also contemplate how, at the very moment of the Blessed Virgin's consent — when the archangel announced and promised the incarnation of the Son of God, and she said: *Behold the handmaid of the Lord: be it done to me according to thy word* (Luke i, 38) — in that same moment the Holy Spirit, nay the whole most glorious Trinity operating outwardly inseparably, from the most pure blood of the sacred Virgin formed and organised the body of Christ and then created his rational soul and infused it into his body, and immediately united both to one another naturally toward the constitution of one specific nature, and finally in the same instant hypostatically joined this nature to the eternal Word. For it befitted the divine power, which is infinite, to accomplish all these things instantaneously.

Moreover let the illuminated eye of the mind contemplate most affectionately that in that same instant the soul of Christ began to see God by vision fruitively, as joyfully, happily, immediately and clearly as it now sees him in the homeland; and that in the same instant it was filled with habitual grace, with the gifts of the Holy Spirit, and with all wisdom and virtue having no imperfection in its reason: for Christ had neither faith nor hope, since he always saw the Deity in the beatific vision. In the same moment too it was filled with the gifts of grace freely given — namely the grace of prophecy, of miracles, of tongues, of discernment of spirits: so that in all these he never progressed as to the infused habits themselves, nor as to the essential reward, nay not even meriting anything for himself as to that reward.

Furthermore, in the same moment just mentioned, the humanity of Christ was placed before every creature, and received from the Word all power in heaven and earth: because, as Damascene asserts, it was altogether fitting that as soon as that immense good — the personal or hypostatic being of the Word, by which man is truly God — was communicated to the humanity, all the other goods already mentioned, which are created and finite, should also be communicated to the same humanity. None the less, as to the sensitive and lower part of the soul and the passibility of the body, Christ was a wayfarer; and in this respect he merited for himself in an accidental way.

### Article XLVI

*Speculation on the beauty, excellence and loveableness of the human nature assumed by the Word*

*Thou art beautiful above the sons of men: grace is poured abroad in thy lips.* (Ps. xliv, 3)

That the devout contemplator may rejoice the more heartily and be fixed the more delightfully in the consideration of Christ's humanity, let some things be said of the beauty and excellence of the assumed humanity.

First, therefore, let it be contemplated that the eternal Word, the only-begotten Son of God the Father, neither could nor would hypostatically join to his most holy divinity, whose beauty is altogether immense, a human nature other than the most beautiful, and completed and adorned with all the elegance that created nature can sustain: not because the union of the two natures in Christ was made through some created intervening grace or grace preceding the hypostatic union of the humanity with the Word in any way, except in the preconception of the divine mind, according as God decreed to adorn with every gift and beauty of grace the humanity to be assumed in the very moment of union. Hence some theologians say that the grace of union in Christ is something created, really identical with his habitual grace, so that this grace is called that of union, in so far as in the preordination of the divine mind it preceded the hypostatic union.

Furthermore, let it be contemplated that God loved the assumed humanity more than the whole universe: for he bestowed on it a greater good — the subsistential being of the Word — which is an uncreated and infinite good; and it is incomparably more worthy to be hypostatically united to God than to have grace only. Again, the humanity of Christ alone is filled with greater and fuller grace, more ample and more happy glory, than all the elect taken together with their grace and glory equally summed. Hence it alone sees God more clearly and loves him more ardently than all created minds taken together.

From these things one may in some measure perceive how incomprehensible is the beauty, excellence and loveableness of the assumed humanity. For grace is a supernatural ornament, a light-flowing splendour and most bright light of the created mind, adorning its essence. Also in the soul of Christ from the very beginning of the Incarnation was the light of glory, by which it was apt for the beatific vision; and this light is of ineffable elegance. And the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit and the other perfect virtues were and are in the soul of Christ in so eminent a degree that it alone surpasses excellently all the gifts and virtues of all others.

Therefore let us heartily congratulate the blessedness and beauty of the humanity of Christ, ineffably humbled and suffering for us; and kindled by the most fervent love of him, let us sigh with all our marrow for his happy vision, in which the accidental reward is situated.

### Article XLVII

*That in Christ's Incarnation and Passion, God's goodness, mercy, wisdom, justice, love and power can be evidently and eminently considered and contemplated*

*God so loved the world as to give his only-begotten Son.* (John iii, 16)

It is a property of the good to communicate itself and to diffuse its goodness abundantly. To God therefore, naturally, totally and without limit good, it was fitting to communicate himself most fully to his creature, as far as it was receptive. This was done in the Incarnation of the Word, in which the same subsistential being in which the Word subsists eternally was communicated to human nature in Christ. Therefore, O contemplator, most sweetly and most affectionately weigh the immense goodness of the only-begotten Son of God, who communicated himself to his creature so most liberally.

In this also appears, inseparably, the goodness, condescension and love of the Father and of the Holy Spirit. For God the Father so loved the world that he gave his only-begotten Son (John iii, 16); and his own Son he spared not, but delivered him up for us all (Rom. viii, 32). And in the Holy Spirit no less condescension, piety and love are to be noted, in that he deigned to cooperate in his Incarnation, from whom he himself truly and eternally proceeds, and sanctified the holy Virgin to bear him from whom he himself flows.

Furthermore, in this that God redeemed the human race in so fitting a manner, and found the most fitting solution to a most difficult knot, the immensity of his wisdom is declared. For he became man, that we might be deified; and by such a process freed man, in which the divine goodness, mercy, charity and the rest now mentioned are laid open most evidently and most beautifully to all.

But it also appeared difficult how the human race could be redeemed saving divine equity. Yet in this difficulty the wisdom of God found the altogether best manner, redeeming man in such a form that both justice and mercy were satisfied: for sin does not remain unpunished, nor is the sinner left to perish. And in this the equity of God was also shown — that he does not make another overcome the tyrant, but as man was overcome by the devil, so the devil was overcome by man. Hence not by force but by equity he rescues man from the power of the devil.

Moreover the supreme mercy of God is shown, in that he did not spurn the infirmity of his own creature but assumed it in order to help it. Furthermore, in Christ's Incarnation the omnipotence of God shines forth, in that he so joined human nature — so imperfect and created last — to the divine nature, infinitely most high, and wrought such ineffably marvellous things in the Incarnation.

Therefore the Incarnation and likewise the Passion of the Saviour is the most illustrious book and the most sublime and most delightful object of all contemplation: in which the aforesaid properties and perfections of God are most evidently read and more profoundly considered than in the production, subsistence, form and aspect of the whole fabric of the world — if the Holy Spirit deign most gloriously to illumine the faithful and pious mind in the contemplation of these things, as he taught the soul of St Augustine, who could not be satisfied in the contemplation of the most holy counsel of the most high Trinity for the liberation of the human race.

Therefore let us contemplate these things frequently and (so far as possible) stably with a joyful, grateful and fervent mind, most devoutly rendering unceasing thanks to the most high God from the most inward sanctuary of the heart with the pure affection of divine charity, and humbly and fervently admiring, adoring and praising the goodness, love, condescension and munificence of the whole ever-living, most happy and most loveable Trinity toward us.

### Article XLVIII

*On the marvels around Christ's Incarnation; and why the Incarnation was most fitting for the Son*

*Without any doubt, great is the mystery of godliness which was manifested in the flesh.* (I Tim. iii, 16)

In Christ's Incarnation the sober contemplator finds many things to be wondered at, greatly transcending the capacity of natural reason and possible to the divine and immense power alone: in the contemplation of which the immoderation of human scrutiny is to be avoided, and in the divine marvels the magnificence of the Almighty is to be extolled with magnificent praises.

The first marvel was that infinitely and without proportion distant things — namely the uncreated and uncircumscribed Word of God and the humanity — came together into one person. The second, that from two natures complete in their quidditative act one person subsists. The third, that the human nature in Christ had its own actions and passions, though it subsisted not in its own created hypostatic being. The fourth, that an eternal, unchangeable and perfect person is truly and temporally generated without any change on his own part or acquisition of a new being in his own nature. The fifth, that a virgin conceived and gave birth without injury to her integrity — which was not without the penetration of dimensions. Also, that the formation and animation of Christ's body were instantaneous, and in the same instant the soul of Christ began to see God by vision; and many other things were completed in the same instant, as has already been shown.

Furthermore, through the Incarnation of the Word, faith is especially strengthened, since we do not doubt that he who speaks and testifies is God. Hope too is raised up and charity is kindled, when we recognise that God himself out of his exceeding charity has become incarnate for us. Perfect examples of humility, holiness, patience and constancy are also given to us through the Incarnation, the life and the Passion of the Word.

Why did the Incarnation befit most especially the Son? Because it is by the Son that the Father utters himself and all things; the Son is the Word, the image, the art of the Father; and it was fitting that through the same Word by which all things were made and known and ordered, the fallen world should be remade and restored. The Son too is wisdom, and it was by wisdom — by the ordering of the divine plan in the Incarnation — that man was healed. And the Son alone proceeds by way of the intellect, by way of nature and likeness, which most fittingly corresponds to the work of assuming a nature and communicating the divine image renewed to the creature.


### Article XLIX

*On the life, virtues and examples of Christ, as a most fruitful subject of contemplation*

*Jesus of Nazareth, a man approved of God among you, by miracles and wonders and signs.* (Acts ii, 22)

That the soul may be more fully nourished in the contemplation of Christ's humanity, it must dwell not only on the mystery of the Incarnation and the theology of the hypostatic union, but on the whole course of the life of Christ from the manger to the Cross. For the life of Christ is the perfect exemplar of all virtue, and the most delightful and profitable subject for the contemplating soul.

Let the soul contemplate first the most holy poverty and humility of the Incarnate Word, who in his birth chose a manger for his bed, poor shepherds for his first adorers, and fugitive exile for the opening of his life. Let it then behold the hidden life at Nazareth — thirty years of obedience, labour, silence and poverty — by which the Son of God has sanctified all the humble and hidden occupations of human life. Let it ponder the fast in the desert, the temptations, the poverty of the public life, the tireless charity of the miracles.

Above all, let it contemplate the humility of the Passion: how he who is the King of kings and Lord of lords allowed himself to be betrayed, seized, bound, mocked, scourged and crucified — bearing all these things not through compulsion but through most ardent love, for the very enemies who were torturing him. And in all these things, the soul of the contemplator receives the most vivid instruction in every virtue: humility from his abasement, patience from his suffering, charity from his forgiving, obedience from his acceptance of the Father's will, fortitude from his endurance.

### Articles L–LV

*On remaining aspects of the third species — Christ as teacher, intercessor and head of the Church; and on the contemplation of Mary and the saints*

[Articles L–LV complete the third species of contemplation by considering: Christ as teacher of saving truth; Christ as high priest and perpetual intercessor; Christ as head of the mystical body, giving life and grace to all his members; the contemplation of the Blessed Virgin Mary as the most perfect created image of the Incarnate Word and the model of the contemplative soul; and the contemplation of the saints in glory, who mirror most perfectly the perfections of Christ and of the Deity, and whose intercession the contemplator is to seek continually.]

### Articles LVI–LIX

*On the fourth species of contemplation — the goods of glory and of the heavenly homeland*

*One thing I have asked of the Lord, this will I seek after: that I may dwell in the house of the Lord all the days of my life.* (Ps. xxvi, 4)

The fourth species of contemplation is the intuition of the goods of glory — that is, of the blessed life in the heavenly homeland. This contemplation is most sweetly enkindling of divine love and most powerfully detaching the soul from love of the world.

Let the contemplator consider what it is to see God face to face — to see him not through a mirror and in an enigma, but directly and immediately, as he is in himself. Let it consider that this vision is not a finite comprehension (for God cannot be comprehended by any created intellect) but an infinite delight: for the more clearly the soul sees God, the more it loves him, and the more it loves, the more it is beatified, and this beatitude grows forever and is never exhausted, since God's infinity can never be exhausted by any finite intelligence, however perfectly united to him.

Let it also consider the resurrection of the body: how the very body that laboured and suffered and denied itself for God in this life will be raised in glory, become impassible, luminous, agile and subtle. Let it contemplate the joy of the company of the saints and angels, the perfect peace and harmony of the heavenly city, the beauty of the empyrean, the unceasing praise of the Holy Trinity by all the blessed.

And from all these contemplations, let the soul be filled with a burning desire for the homeland, a contempt for this valley of tears and exile, a joy in all present suffering endured for love of God. For one day in the courts of the Lord is better than a thousand elsewhere (Ps. lxxxiii, 11).

### Article LX

*Contemplation of the goods of sanctifying grace*

*The munificent goodwill and most kindly love of our God, who as he created man for supernatural beatitude, so gave him supernatural means for attaining this happiness — namely the most loving and most powerful helps of divine power: supernatural created habits, immediately infused by the Holy Spirit.*

Blessedness is obtained by the rational creature through acts pleasing to God. But before it can produce such acts — namely supernatural, spiritual, divine acts, acceptable to God and meritorious of eternal life — it is necessary that it be perfected in its essence, assimilated to God, placed in a deiform being fit for meritorious action, and made acceptable and pleasing to God. For if it itself were not pleasing, neither would its works be meritorious. Hence in Genesis it is written that God looked upon Abel and upon his gifts (Gen. iv, 4): first by approval and acceptance he looked upon the worker, then upon the work and offering.

The habit therefore so perfecting the essence of the rational soul is called sanctifying grace, which makes the soul supernaturally similar to God and renders it fit for meriting eternal life: it makes the soul so like and loveable to God that it is called his friend, spouse and daughter, heir, throne and bridal chamber; and by every act, affection and thought proceeding from this grace it merits the highest beatitude.

Then it is necessary that the soul be supernaturally perfected in its powers for acting: and so the habits supernaturally perfecting the soul's powers concerning God are the theological virtues — faith, hope and charity — by which God is primarily, immediately, directly and most powerfully honoured and worshipped, he who is the object of these virtues. Yet, since the theological virtues are most excellent for producing their acts readily and promptly, man still needs the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit — which are also supernatural habits infused by God through creation, immediately perfecting the virtues themselves, especially the theological ones — through which the soul is made governable, movable and teachable by the Holy Spirit and fit for producing the acts of virtue readily; just as through the moral virtues it is made readily and efficaciously movable by reason.

These gracious supernatural goods place the soul in a deiform and supernatural being. They are deiform rays and simple goodnesses, resplendent lights and deifying gifts, clear rivulets and most bright lamps, flowing immediately from the eternal font, from the fontal light, from the sublime God, and leading the soul back to him: by whose splendour, beauty and vigour the human mind is so suffused, exalted and beautified that nothing in the natural chain of beings, nothing in the elegance of the celestial spheres, nothing in the ornaments, perfection and light of the planets and stars can equal it.

Therefore let us contemplate the ineffable goodness, interminable piety, full love, immense condescension and most copious munificence of the most high God over us: who first at the font of baptism infused into us such most excellent, deifying and supernatural goods of grace — grace itself, the theological and infused virtues, and the gifts of the Holy Spirit — and as often as we have lost them by our perversities and ingratitude, as soon as we have grieved from the heart, has restored them with most ineffable mercy: nay, whenever we groan truly from the soul over mortal vices, he is ready at once to give them back.

### Article LXI

*How through sanctifying grace, the theological and infused virtues and gifts, the mind is supernaturally assimilated to God*

*We all, beholding the glory of the Lord with open face, are transformed into the same image from glory to glory, as by the Spirit of the Lord.* (II Cor. iii, 18)

Just as through the rational soul man is constituted in his substantial, natural and specific being, by which he is naturally assimilated to divine being as one produced to the image and likeness of his Creator, and through the powers naturally implanted in him — intellect and will — is capable of God, knowing and loving him even by his natural powers alone, as far as from creatures he can ascend by natural reason to the knowledge and love of the Creator: so through sanctifying grace the soul itself is constituted in an accidental, supernatural and super-rational, nay super-intellectual being, by which the rational specific being is elevated and perfected to meritorious, super-rational and supernatural acts through the infused gifts and virtues — acts toward God which it cannot produce or have by its natural powers; just as also through the light of glory it is placed in a higher and more excellent deiform, supernatural, super-intellectual and super-mental being, more sublime than any intellectual, natural, created being: by which it is made directly fit for the acts of beatitude.

Furthermore, the theological virtues supernaturally perfect the soul's powers for acting, for producing those acts concerning God that they cannot produce or have by natural power: hence the intellect through faith knows higher and loftier things of God than through natural investigation; the will also through charity — which is infused love — loves God more perfectly, more purely and more fervently than through natural love. Similarly through the infused moral virtues the passions are restrained and the works of virtues are exercised in a more eminent manner than through acquired moral virtues.

Similarly the gifts of the Holy Spirit make the soul much more ready and effective in holy works and more teachable and movable by the Holy Spirit in all things than any natural human sciences or virtues.

Therefore let us incessantly pray to the munificence of divine goodness for the increase and perfection of these goods of grace, so that we may obey God with filial affection in all things, devoting and fixing ourselves to him.

### Article LXII

*Contemplation of the foundations of the Catholic faith*

*Faith is the substance of things to be hoped for, the evidence of things that appear not.* (Heb. xi, 1)

In contemplative, anointed and perfect persons, faith exists with the reasons for believing and the understanding of the purified mind. For it would be levity to give assent to things that belong to faith and transcend all reason and capacity, unless faith had efficacious arguments showing it to be truly given by God and consequently good, holy and saving. These arguments do not prove faith in such a way as to make what belongs to faith comprehensible in this life, but they evidently demonstrate that the author, confirmer and witness of this faith is God.

Furthermore, through the gift of understanding the Holy Spirit infuses into the soul knowledge of those things by which the truth of faith shines forth: so that the more excellently any one is clarified and the more fully perfected by this gift, the more certainly and clearly he penetrates by a certain intuition what belongs to faith, understanding all or some of the reasons for believing — so that nothing seems to him more rational than what the Christian faith embraces. For all things accord with one another so fittingly, connected and ordered, that each appears more rational and credible from the other. And all things collated together obtain the most beautiful and supernatural consonance.

For, as God all-powerful created man for supernatural beatitude, so at the beginning he created the first parents in certain supernatural gifts, namely original justice, and placed them in the earthly paradise, and endowed the man with great knowledge and divine revelation; then, because of their transgression, they were expelled from paradise, deprived of original justice, and both they and their posterity became subject to damnation; and the human race could not be redeemed through any pure creature. Hence the Son of God predicted, promised, prefigured and foreshadowed from the beginning of the world through the Patriarchs and Prophets, and especially in the old law (in which he chose, gathered and dedicated to his worship one people from whose seed he would be born), that he would become incarnate; and finally, through the mystery of the Incarnation, descended, appeared, lived among men and fulfilled what had been foretold of him.

### Articles LXIII–LXVIII

*On the fifth species of contemplation — the goods of nature and the consideration of the created universe*

[Articles LXIII–LXVIII develop the fifth species of contemplation, consisting in the consideration of the gifts of nature: first the soul's own natural powers and their ordering; then the natural dignity of the human person as made in God's image; then the variety and order of the visible creation — the elements, the living creatures, the heavens and their order — all of which are contemplated not for their own sake but as vestiges and mirrors of the divine wisdom, goodness and power. Denis follows Augustine, Gregory and the tradition in reading the book of nature as a ladder to the Creator, and shows how each natural perfection glimpsed in the creature is a dim reflection of the perfection that is in God without limit, without composition, and without shadow.]

### Article LXIX

*Contemplation of the most high God from the disposition of the world, according to the Scriptures*

*Lift up your eyes on high, and see who hath created these things.* (Is. xl, 26)

The sensible world is a simulacrum, leading the mind by many ways of subtle considerations to the speculation of the Creator. And in this place by the name of world I take the totality of corporeal creatures; for the sensible world is not to be thought of as absolutely one simple thing, but only aggregatively.

Some philosophers, especially the Platonists, thought the sensible world to be one absolutely and specifically, namely a certain animal. Plotinus wrote a book on three substances — the highest God, the *nous*, and the world soul. And Augustine recounts in the thirteenth book on the City of God how Plato said the world soul extends and spreads from the very centre of the earth to the highest and outermost limits of the heaven by musical numbers, so that this world is a greatest, most blessed and sempiternal animal, whose soul holds both the perfect happiness of wisdom and does not desert its own body. Yet this opinion is insane and consonant neither with reason nor with faith, as Thomas diligently shows in the Summa against the Gentiles. None the less the world itself exists so magnificently and wonderfully instituted, distinguished, placed, ruled and ordered that Augustine rightly says in the City of God that every miracle done in the world has a greater miracle than itself — the world.

Hence from the original construction of the world, the disposition of the elements, the various distinction of mixed things and animals, the provident governance, the manifold property and fitting station of things, sacred Scripture praises the excellence of the Creator.

Hence the Lord speaks to holy Job: Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth? Who hath laid the measures thereof? or who stretched the line upon it? Upon what are its bases grounded? Who shut up the sea with doors, when it broke forth as issuing out of the womb, when I made a cloud the garment thereof, and wrapped it in a mist as in swaddling bands? Knowest thou the order of heaven, and canst thou set down the reason thereof on the earth? (Job xxxviii, 4–5, 8–9, 33). And again: Who stretcheth out the north over the empty space, and hangeth the earth upon nothing; who bindeth up the waters in his clouds; in his power the seas are suddenly gathered together, and his spirit hath adorned the heavens (Job xxvi, 7–8, 12–13). And Solomon says: The Lord by wisdom hath founded the earth, hath established the heavens by prudence; by his wisdom the depths have broken out, and the clouds grow thick with dew (Prov. iii, 19–20).

The holy Psalmist too has sung of these things: By the word of the Lord the heavens were established, and all the power of them by the spirit of his mouth; gathering together the waters of the sea as in a vessel, putting the depths in storehouses (Ps. xxxii, 6–7). He that spreadeth out the heavens like a curtain: who covereth the higher rooms thereof with water; who makest the clouds thy chariot; who walkest upon the wings of the winds. He hath founded the earth upon its own bases: it shall not be moved for ever and ever (Ps. ciii, 2–3, 5). And again: Whatsoever the Lord pleased, he hath done, in heaven, in earth, in the sea, and in all the deeps; bringing forth clouds from the ends of the earth; he hath made lightnings for the rain; who bringeth forth winds out of his treasuries (Ps. cxxxiv, 6–7).

And from all these things Augustine says of God's governance in the third book on the Trinity: God presiding as it were in a high and holy and secret seat in angelic spirits joined to one another in the highest peace and fullest friendship, and fused into one will as by some spiritual fire of charity — as in his own house and temple — moves himself thence to all creatures through certain most orderly motions, spiritual first, then carnal and corporeal, and uses all things according to the unchangeable counsel of his own judgement. And again: Nothing comes to be visibly and sensibly that is not ordered or permitted from the interior, invisible and intelligible court of the highest Emperor, according to the ineffable justice of rewards and punishments, of graces and retributions, in this widest and most immense commonwealth of the whole creation.

Therefore as the Devout Doctor says: one who is not illumined by such great splendours of created things is blind; who does not awaken at such great cries is deaf. All things proclaim: He hath made us, and not we ourselves (Ps. xcix, 3). He who from all these effects does not praise God is dumb; he who from such great signs does not advert to the first principle is foolish. Open therefore your spiritual eyes, give ear, loose your lips and apply your heart, that in all creatures you may see, hear, praise, love, worship, magnify and honour your God; so that all the earth does not rise up against you, but you may truly say with the Psalmist: Thou hast given me, O Lord, a delight in thy doings, and in the works of thy hands I shall rejoice (Ps. xci, 5).

### Article LXX

*Contemplation of the most high God from his natural image in man*

*Let us make man to our image and likeness.* (Gen. i, 26)

As Augustine says in the tenth book on the City of God, of every miracle that is done through man, man himself is a greater miracle. From this it is most certainly known that man is a preeminent and wonderfully instituted creature, and difficult fully to know, so that the perfection of the Creator shines forth excellently and is clearly known in him.

Therefore some affirm that in no creature, not even in any angelic spirit, is the Creator so clearly represented, reflected and recognisable as in the rational soul. Yet this saying seems ambiguous: for how can anyone know this unless he knows the properties of the angelic spirits as perfectly as those of the rational soul? And indeed as the angelic nature is of a more perfect intellectuality than our soul, so in it the image of God shines more sublimely and more clearly than in the soul aforesaid.

None the less, with respect to those things in which this image is secondarily situated, it is placed more in man than in the angel, at least as to some things: namely that as in the divine there is God from God, so in the human there is man from man; and as one God is the principle of all things, so one man is the ancestor of all men; and as God is whole in the whole universe and whole in any part of it, governing and containing it, so the soul is whole in the whole body and in any part of it, preserving and governing it. Again, as God contains the perfections of all beings in himself super-eminently and ideally, so man communicates in some perfection with all things, both higher and lower: for which reason he is called every creature, the ligature, horizon and girdle of beings, because through him the lower and upper orders are joined and connected to the constitution of one world.

Let us therefore in the aforesaid properties contemplate God the Trinity in man his image. Yet let us rather above all speculate on the most blessed Trinity in the rational mind, as to those things in which the nature of the image is principally situated, with respect to the intellective part of the soul. For as the Son knows himself through his own essence, and God similarly: so the angel knows himself through his own essence, and similarly God.

As God the Father, understanding himself, utters and begets within himself a certain knowledge of himself — a concept or Word — and with this Word and through it, produces in the affective power or will a love of the same object, namely God: so the soul contemplating God utters, forms and begets within itself from God himself as object an inner word or mental concept, and with this concept or through it, produces in the affective power or will love of the same object, namely God. So therefore in the mind and its knowledge and love is considered the image of the most blessed Trinity.

Furthermore, in man himself the divine power, wisdom and also kindness are magnificently commended — in that he joined things so diverse, namely an immortal, intellectual, immaterial, deiform and simple form, and a corruptible, dull, gross, earthly and most composite body, in the unity of a specific nature and person, most wisely, not only for the good of the body but also for the soul's advancement. Similarly in the interior and exterior formation of the human body, the distinction of members and senses, the multiplicity and order of the soul's powers, and finally the soul's various, artistic and subtle operation through the body — who can sufficiently or worthily wonder at the omnipotent and uncircumscribed Creator's most efficacious and immense wisdom?

Moreover, one who understands the nature, simplicity and indivisibility of his own soul, who recognises the immateriality, separability and indestructibility of his own intellect, has obtained no small path for contemplating the properties of the divine essence and the dispositions of the eternal mind. For as philosophical acuity proves the rational soul to be a certain incorporeal, non-quantified, impartible and simple essence; and the intellect to consist in an inorganic, separated, immortal and unmixed power: does not one thereby most easily of all perceive that the adorable and blessed God is an altogether immaterial, impassible, separated and simple being?

Therefore let us daily reform, cleanse and perfect the image of the most blessed Trinity in us; and let us strive to beget continually within ourselves through contemplation an inner word about God and to produce love — that our heart may utter a good word about God (Ps. xliv, 2), rightly understanding him to be the first, infinite and perfect being.

### Article LXXI

*Contemplation of God from the natural consideration of the angelic spirits*

*Who maketh his angels spirits, and his ministers a burning fire.* (Ps. ciii, 4)

Although the better philosophers said that certain intellectual substances more worthy than man — which we call angels — mediate between the human mind and that which they call understanding, positing animated celestial bodies, yet it seems safer to hold the opposite — though with Augustine in his commentary on Genesis, the Enchiridion and the City of God, one may leave the matter sub dubio.

Raising now the gaze of the mind to the natural consideration of the angelic minds, it is certain that God the exalted, who created all things so that his perfection might shine forth, be manifest and be known in them, made his perfection shine forth more brightly in the angelic spirits (whom he created near himself and first, not in time but in nature and dignity) so that they are most like the highest Creator in every noble perfection. Hence they have less admixed of potentiality, ignorance, distinction of powers, plurality of intelligible species, diversity of actions, variability of motions, multiplicity of virtues and habits: because what is done in lower things by many is done in higher things by fewer.

Angels are therefore — called by the philosophers separated substances and intelligences and movers of the spheres, in so far as they serve the first principle in the works of creation and nature; and by theologians they are called angels, in so far as they minister to the all-powerful Lord in the works of re-creation and grace — they are simple, immaterial, self-subsistent forms, deiform essences, adorned from the beginning of their creation with all natural wisdom and knowledge, filled with intelligible species, pre-eminent in natural power and freedom; in whom the image of God shines forth excellently as though by a seal, because they remain unceasingly in actual contemplation of the Creator and in love. In them the intellective power is one, not divided into agent and possible intellect; the will also in them is vigorous and most swift. They do not obtain other powers distinct from their essence, according to some; but the more sublime any angelic spirit is and the nearer to God, the fewer species it understands by — and yet more perfectly than those below by more. None the less an angel knows himself through his own essence, and similarly God.

Furthermore, the vigour, causality and power of these supernal minds is easy to observe from the fact that they carry the celestial spheres in such swift motion, agitate the elements, influence the lower things, and lead forth innumerable substantial and also living forms from the potency of matter through the motion of the heavens; that by the approach of the sun in the oblique circle they renew the face of the earth, make it green and germinating, render trees and herbs fruitful; that they recognise the order of the universe, and govern this lower world so wisely and uniformly according to the law divinely prefixed to them, that the being of corruptible things is perpetuated through the succession of generations.

From the contemplation of these things we are elevated more sublimely to the knowledge of the divine nature: that the divine mind is entirely free from all material admixture; that the divine intellect is most causal, most wise, all-powerful, so simple that through its own essence as through one intelligible form it knows all and each thing most distinctly, possessing fontally in itself the science and power of governing, prescribing it to others.

Finally, from the consideration of the beauty, excellence and perfection of these supernal virtues, we are sublimely exalted to admiration of the uncreated beauty, majesty and fullness of God. But since our spirit's eye has the same relation to the knowledge of those substances as the eye of the owl to the solar brightness, our ignorance closes our speech; yet the more excellent the nature in them, the more splendid also the beauty, the greater and more joyful the happiness.

If however the eternal God has impressed on corporeal things — herbs, trees, gems, birds, the body and face of man — so much beauty that we look upon them most eagerly and joyfully when we behold such things: what and how great the incomparable fontal and exemplary beauty of God must be — let us diligently ponder, let us ardently desire his vision, and through purity of heart let us continually prepare ourselves for his most blessed vision: who is above all things God sublime and blessed. Amen.

*Here ends Book One of the Treatise on Contemplation.*
