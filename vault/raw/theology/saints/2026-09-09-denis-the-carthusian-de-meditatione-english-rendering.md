# On Meditation

*Denis the Carthusian*

## Proemium

*My thoughts follow one upon another, and my mind is carried away in different directions.* (Job xx, 2)

The understanding and the will are bound to one another, and each perfects the other in its working. For the act of the understanding, that is, knowing, meditating or considering, is without affection formless, bare and ineffectual. The will, for its part, is held to be blind of itself and in need of the understanding's direction, which is as it were the eye of the will. That is why the object of the will is the good as understood, that is, apprehended or known by the understanding. Hence the good, virtuous and holy affections of the will arise from sincere, useful and wholesome meditations, and depend on them and are kept by them. For every advance, then, and for the salvation of our souls, we must attend to the guarding of the understanding, resist the wanderings of the mind, and by this means keep watch over virtuous affections. Therefore Solomon said: *With all watchfulness keep thy heart, because life issueth out from it* (Prov. iv, 23). And Christ, the fountain of life and our judge, declared: *Blessed are the clean of heart* (Matth. v, 8).

Hence, dearest brother, some years ago you asked me to write out my meditations for you. At the request, I confess, a blush overcame me, and I answered at once that I could sooner write out for you my distractions and my empty fancies, if that would serve to edify. Since you persisted in asking, I resolved to comply as far as I could, touching not so much on the meditations I have as on those I ought by rights to have.

### Article I

*Of the harm of evil thoughts*

Every day we confess that we have sinned in thought; and yet who can shut and bridle his heart against thought? Indeed we sin more by affection than by thought; and yet by thought it happens that we sin in many ways and gravely. First, by dwelling on it too long. Hence through Jeremias God rebukes the man who thinks foolishly, saying: *How long shall hurtful thoughts abide in thee?* (Jer. iv, 14). Second, by a disordered and evil affection joined to the thought or born of it, as when someone, hearing, reading or meditating on stories of carnal things and doings, is touched by lust and unlawfully stirred, even at times in the divine Office and in holy places; which is wholly to be avoided. Third, on the side of time and of what does not belong there: as those who, at the time of divine things, and while they are free for better and more spiritual things and acts, are intent on temporal and outward things, for which they could lawfully be free at a fitting time. Which, alas, at the time of prayer and psalmody and celebration frequently befalls us. Fourth, on the side of the disorder and evil of the thoughts in themselves: as one who turns over and holds within himself vain imaginings and useless thoughts, which are not set under the order of divine love nor of use to it, such as melancholy apprehensions, bitter thoughts, rash judgements, unjust suspicions, immoderate searchings into high and incomprehensible things, fancies about things not to be thought of, and magnifyings of oneself. Of these and the like, and of the foolish boasting in them, the Lord speaks in the Prophets: *Take away the evil of your devices from my eyes* (Is. i, 16); and again: *Woe to you that devise that which is unprofitable* (Mich. ii, 1); and again: *The Holy Spirit will withdraw himself from thoughts that are without understanding* (Wisd. i, 5); *perverse thoughts separate from God* (Wisd. i, 3). In these things time is spent without fruit, and often lost in vice, and the mind itself is torn in itself, embittered and afflicted, according to the saying: A disordered mind is a punishment to itself. All such thoughts, therefore, are wholly to be avoided, quickly cast off, vehemently despised and hated.

### Article II

*Of the things which are wholesomely and continually to be meditated and considered*

Of the wise and virtuous man, already blessed in its beginning, it is said at the opening of the Psalms: *His will is in the law of the Lord, and on his law he shall meditate day and night* (Ps. i, 2). Hence elsewhere it is written: *The things that God hath commanded thee, think on them always, and in many of his works be not curious* (Ecclus. iii, 22, 24). This is what he did who sang and confesses: *O how have I loved thy law, O Lord! it is my meditation all the day* (Ps. cxviii, 97). Did not God also command this in Deuteronomy, saying: *Thou shalt meditate on the book of this law, sitting in thy house, and walking on thy journey, sleeping and rising?* (Deut. vi, 7). Elsewhere too the Scripture warns: *Remember thy last end, and thou shalt never sin* (Ecclus. vii, 40).

Further, we ought unceasingly and most diligently to attend to Christ fastened to the cross for us and as it were hanging on the cross, in every manner in which he hung there; and to his whole way of living in this world, his teachings and his exhortations, and to the way of living of the Saints in this life; but also we ought without ceasing to weigh the presence of the divine majesty, and how we may be able to please him, to answer him and to make satisfaction to him, and so in every virtue fittingly obtain an advance.

### Article III

*A rebuke of the sloth, negligence and irreverence of our wretched and unhappy souls before the Most High*

Often God through the Prophets has complained against the one who sins shamelessly; now saying: *Thou hast cast me behind thy back* (III Kings xiv, 9); and elsewhere, of those who sin without shame: *They have turned their back to me, and not their face, and have walked contrary to me* (Jer. ii, 27; Lev. xxvi). More at length also through Solomon: *Because I called, and you refused: I stretched out my hand, and there was none that regarded. You have despised all my counsel, and have neglected my reprehensions. I also will laugh in your destruction, and will mock when that shall come to you which you feared* (Prov. i, 24-26). A like sentence the heavenly Judge uttered through Solomon's father, saying: *He that dwelleth in heaven shall laugh at them: and the Lord shall deride them. Then shall he speak to them in his anger, and trouble them in his rage* (Ps. ii, 4, 5). What more terrible than this can be said or thought? Wherefore the Psalmist prayed: *O Lord, rebuke me not in thy indignation, nor chastise me in thy wrath* (Ps. vi, 2); and Jeremias, sanctified in the womb (of whom holy Jerome, in his commentary on him, declared: Truly I think no one holier than Jeremias): *Correct me, O Lord, but yet with judgement* (no doubt, of discretion), *and not in thy fury, lest thou bring me to nothing* (Jer. x, 24). Truly, as the Apostle says, *it is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God* (Hebr. x, 31); so fearful that holy Job said, and prayed most affectionately: *Who will grant me this, that thou mayst protect me in hell, and hide me till thy wrath pass, and appoint me a time when thou wilt remember me?* (Job xiv, 13).

What do you say to this, O my wretched soul? Why do you dissemble? Why do you grow numb and wither in sloth? Why are you distracted by many things, and scattered in vain and frivolous thoughts? Nay, why do you go whoring with the demons in foul and base imaginings, when you have so many and such abundant matters for wholesome meditation, such a store and so many objects of thoughts needful for salvation and advance, so many causes, incitements and splendid, consoling and joyful occasions of noble and heavenly considerations and contemplations? *How long wilt thou be drunk* with wine (I Kings i, 14), insane and out of your mind? Digest a little, nay wholly vomit out *the wine of the grapes of Sodom and of the suburbs of Gomorrha* (Deut. xxxii, 32) with which you are soaked, so that, purged from the whirlpools of vicious and infernal rivers, you may draw near to the fountain of life, and begin to drink of, to be flooded with, to be filled with *the torrent of* deifying *pleasure* (Ps. xxxv, 9). Woe to you, who honour the presence of men more than the presence of God; who in the presence and sight of the most high God do not shudder to think, to turn over, to speak, to admit, to do what you would be ashamed to speak or do before notable and reputed persons or men, and so hold yourself so basely before your holy angel, your guardian, nay before the host of all the Saints. Has not *the forehead of a harlot* become yours (Jer. iii, 3), you who at such enormous shamelessness, irreverence and perversity of yours do not blush most vehemently nor tremble before God, the unceasing inspector and judge of hearts, to whom holy Job speaks: *Doth not he consider my ways, and number all my steps?* (Job xxxi, 4).

Further, I here take you to task, O blinded and wretched one, who often say that you wonder greatly that God, who is by nature kind and merciful, avenges sins so dreadfully as to inflict for one mortal sin a punishment both in intensity and in extent unbearable and unspeakably grave, being eternal, and this most of all when human frailty is so great, and the proneness of men to every evil. Surely this happens because you do not weigh the enormity of sin on every side on which it ought to be deeply and rightly weighed. And yet, my brother, I marvel no less at the unspeakable mercy, long-suffering and patience of God towards the wicked than at the strictness of divine justice just touched on.

THE BROTHER. On this I ask how Jerome can say, I think no one holier than Jeremias, when the Saviour says that among those born of women there has not risen a greater than John the Baptist (Matth. xi, 11).

THE AUTHOR. Jerome, who in the place just cited said the words we have touched on, elsewhere speaks thus: To all of them, that is, the Prophets, I confidently prefer the Apostles. So the Saints, according to different considerations, sometimes speak in different ways of the same thing, now preferring this one, now that. For of every confessor too the Church sings: *There was not found the like to him, who kept the law of the Most High* (Ecclus. xliv, 20). But who is simply the holier is for God to judge, according to what Solomon declares, saying: *The Lord is the weigher of spirits* (Prov. xvi, 2).

### Article IV

*That an order is to be kept in meditations*

As in love an order is to be kept, so in meditation: so that, as every affection towards other things is to be ordered to the love of God (on which, as far as is possible, we ought to insist, to dwell and to cleave unceasingly and in act), so it is fitting that we refer all meditation, thought and consideration about created things to the contemplation, beholding and attention of the Deity, and untiringly attend and give ourselves to the most sincere beholding and contemplation of the incomparable goodness, going, ranging and flying through each of the attributes. And as certain birds and winged creatures move one after another from flower to flower, from fruit to fruit, from branch to branch, from one tree to another, resting a little while on each, and taking some refreshment, or carrying off some coarser matter for the building of the nest; so let us too fly from the contemplation of one divine attribute to the beholding of another, and in the sincere consideration of each let us rest a little, settle and be still, and so be refreshed by holy delight and rejoicing in his excellence and infinity, and likewise from his works build ourselves a dwelling in this our pilgrimage.

First of all, then, let us behold in meditation how truly and incomprehensibly infinite the goodness of God is, which fills each thing according to its capacity, disposition or dignity, *opening his hand and filling with blessing every living creature* (Ps. cxliv, 16), and at the same time every soul with goodness, leaving nothing in the whole universe without a share in his goodness; nay, moving and stirring those things which share more abundantly in the gifts of the highest goodness to communicate and distribute these to others, especially to the lower and less perfect.

And all this he works purely freely, liberally and unceasingly. Nor is he held back by the ingratitude, the baseness, the turning away of things; rather he renews, multiplies, increases his influences, and provides for all, pouring in being, life, sense, the things needed for preservation and life, distributing the power of bringing forth, and continually maintaining the kinds of things, impressing on the generative principles, immovably and most tenaciously, a love for what they have brought forth, so that they do not cease from the guarding, feeding and defence of their young, according to the law brought in for them from above. And this he works in many forms in the gifts of nature, more sublimely in the acts of grace, and most gloriously in the effects of glory.

For he makes his sun to rise every day upon all the inhabitants of the earth: which indeed is plain to all as regards that greatest light; but to the more learned and those who weigh things more keenly it is clear of the other heavenly bodies too, the planets and the stars. For the influences and effects of all these meet and mingle in the beings of the universe, as the philosophers and astronomers of this world have searched and written much about. Nor yet have the holy Scriptures been altogether silent on this; rather they have spoken far more effectively, more heartily and more devoutly, especially in the book of Job, in which almighty God is magnificently commended from his natural effects, beautifully praised and devoutly proclaimed, as the almighty founder of all things, the wisest governor, the irreproachable Judge; so that wherever we turn, on every side there are set and presented to us objects, incitements and abundant remembrances of the excellences, the wonders and the mercies of the Lord our God.

In the same way, from tiny and most minute seeds great trees, great bodies and tall shrubs are born, in which, wonderfully, whole wholes are virtually contained in the smallest things, and innumerable substantial forms seem to be begun, enclosed or rooted in little portions of matter, so much so that almost anything may be thought to lie hidden in anything, and the manner of the hiding, the beginning, the coming forth and the production can scarcely be expressed and made clear with certainty and plainness by those who philosophise or theologise; which is shown by the variety and contrariety of their opinions.

He who at the beginning of the sensible world brought forth the kinds and classes of things over the intervals of six days (as Scripture seems to intimate), co-causing and planting in them primordial reasons, causal powers, productive principles, manifold fertilities: in all of which nature works hiddenly, through the turns of the seasons, at the Creator's command, breeding like from like. Which Scripture marks: *Let the earth*, it says, *bring forth the green herb, and such as may seed, and the fruit tree yielding fruit after its kind, which may have seed in itself upon the earth. And it was so done* (Gen. i, 11, 12). Then of the making fruitful of the other elements and the production of irrational animals in them it is added in the same place: *God also said: Let the waters bring forth the creeping creature having life, and the fowl that may fly over the earth. And God created the great whales, and every living and moving creature, which the waters brought forth, according to their kinds, and every winged fowl according to its kind* (Gen. i, 20, 21). *And God said: Let the earth bring forth the living creature in its kind, cattle and creeping things, and beasts of the earth, according to their kinds. And it was so done* (Gen. i, 24). Hence in the Scriptures the works of vegetative and sensitive nature are attributed to God; likewise the acts of the generative and nutritive powers, as blessed Job says: *Thy hands have made me, and fashioned me wholly round about. Remember, I beseech thee, that thou hast made me as the clay. Hast thou not milked me as milk, and curdled me like cheese? Thou hast clothed me with skin and flesh: thou hast put me together with bones and sinews* (Job x, 8-11).

To which in some way may belong what Ecclesiastes says: *All things are hard: man cannot explain them by word* (Eccles. i, 8). And again he says: *He hath delivered the world to their consideration, so that man cannot find out the work which God hath made from the beginning to the end* (Eccles. iii, 11). And again after some things he declares: *There are some that day and night take no sleep with their eyes. And I understood that man can find no reason of all those works of God that are done under the sun: and the more he shall labour to seek, so much the less shall he find* (Eccles. viii, 16, 17). Hence once more that wise man said: *As thou knowest not what is the way of the spirit, nor how the bones are joined together in the womb of her that is with child: so thou knowest not the works of God, who is the maker of all* (Eccles. xi, 5).

It is more wholesome, then, to study the virtues, which are both more easily and more wholesomely learned and grasped, and are more principally required of man from above. Hence another wise man too has declared: *Seek not the things that are too high for thee, and search not into things above thy ability: but the things that God hath commanded thee, think on them always, and in many of his works be not curious* (Ecclus. iii, 22). Therefore, finally, Solomon closed his book, in which after the manner of a preacher he uttered in the persons of different men various and as it were mutually opposed sentences, with one most solid doctrine: *Let us all hear together*, he says, *the conclusion of the discourse. Fear God, and keep his commandments: for this is all man* (Eccles. xii, 13).

### Article V

*Of man's advance in saving knowledge and in virtues and in divine charity*

Most rightly the Scripture says: *All men are vain, in whom there is not the knowledge of God* (Wisd. xiii, 1). For God made all things that he might be feared, loved, duly honoured, worshipped and continually praised (Eccles. iii, 14). For the cause shines out in its effect, the Creator in creatures. Therefore Isaias warned: *Lift up your eyes on high, and see who hath created these things* (Is. xl, 26). And through Jeremias the Lord reproves our thoughtlessness and ignorance: *Hear*, he says, *O foolish people, and without understanding: will not you then fear me, who have set the sand a bound for the sea, an everlasting ordinance, which it shall not pass over?* (Jer. v, 21, 22).

So the Most High has declared the immensity of his omnipotence and wisdom, not only in the creation of things, in the incomprehensible and most masterful production of such great effects of his out of nothing, but also in their wonderful placing, as he beautifully discoursed on this in another Prophet: *Where wast thou*, he says, *when I laid the foundations of the earth? Who hath laid the measures thereof, or who hath stretched the line upon it? Upon what are its bases grounded? or who laid the corner stone thereof?* (Job xxxviii, 4-6). Of the situation and wonderful disposition of the Ocean too it is added there: *Who shut up the sea with doors, when it broke forth as issuing out of the womb: when I made a cloud the garment thereof, and wrapped it in a mist as in swaddling bands? I set my bounds around it, and made it bars and doors: and I said: Hitherto thou shalt come, and shalt go no further, and here thou shalt break thy swelling waves* (Job xxxviii, 8-11). For since lighter things are placed higher than heavier, it would seem that the water ought to cover the narrow earth, unless by the disposing and command of the Most High, for the preservation and habitation of men and animals, a part of it were uncovered from the waters. Which the uncreated Wisdom too reveals that she did, in the Proverbs: *When he prepared the heavens*, she says, *I was present: when with a certain law and compass he enclosed the depths: when he established the sky above, and poised the fountains of waters: when he compassed the sea with its bounds, and set a law to the waters that they should not pass their limits: when he balanced the foundations of the earth; I was with him forming all things* (Prov. viii, 27-30). Touching this too the Psalmist: *Gathering together the waters of the sea, as in a vessel*, he says, *laying up the depths in storehouses* (Ps. xxxii, 7).

Further, Isaias, philosophising on these things: *Who hath measured the waters in the hollow of his hand*, he says, *and weighed the heavens with his palm? who hath poised with three fingers the bulk of the earth, and weighed the mountains in scales, and the hills in a balance? Who hath forwarded the spirit of the Lord? or who hath been his counsellor, and hath taught him? With whom hath he consulted, and who hath instructed him, and taught him the path of justice, and taught him knowledge, and shewed him the way of understanding?* (Is. xl, 12-14).

Therefore the Apostle says: *Who hath first given to him, and recompense shall be made him? For of him, and by him, and in him, are all things* (Rom. xi, 35, 36). From him assuredly is the whole order of the universe, the course of things, and he has set a law for all things: at whose bidding they were made, at whose command they were created (Ps. xxxii, 9; cxlviii, 5); in whose power are all things; *who made the sun to rule the day, the moon and the stars to rule the night* (Ps. cxxxv, 8, 9). From him all natural things, minerals and the things of the air have their origins, productions, natures, powers, processes, motions, quicknesses and swift courses. Which Scripture marks, especially in the book of Job: *He lifteth up the drops of rain*, it says, *and poureth out showers like floods, which flow from the clouds that cover all above. If he will spread out clouds as his tent, and lighten with his light from above, he shall cover also the ends of the sea* (Job xxxvi, 27-30). *He commandeth the snow to go down upon the earth, and the winter rain* (Job xxxvii, 6). *Who gave a course to violent showers, or a way for noisy thunder: that it should rain on the earth without man in the wilderness, where no mortal dwelleth: that it should fill the desert and desolate land, and should bring forth green grass?* (Job xxxviii, 25-27). Besides, of the natures of certain beasts and birds many things are touched on in the same place (Job xxxviii, 36 and following). How much more could be said of the natures of the heavenly bodies and their influences, of their motions too and their effects, as things incomparably greater, more wonderful and more glorious? And yet all would be very little compared with the things that would have to be said of supercelestial things, of the angelic spirits, of the supramundane hierarchies.

Behold, in all these things we can, as far as our strength allows, contemplate the majesty of the incomparable Creator, who not only in himself but also in his effects, nay in lower and lowest things, is so admirable and incomprehensible to us.

Of these things more and most abundant matter could be brought from the books on the Properties of Things, on the Natures of Animals, on the Virtues of Herbs and of Gems, and the preciousness of Metals, which are so wonderful that something of a fifth essence is said to be enclosed, impressed and imbibed in them. Hence it is rightly written in Ecclesiasticus: *The work of the Lord is full of his glory. God alone shall be justified, and he remaineth an invincible king for ever. Who shall be able to declare his works? For who shall search out his glorious acts? And who shall shew forth the power of his majesty? or who shall be able to declare his mercy? Nothing may be taken away, nor added, neither is it possible to find out the glorious works of God* (Ecclus. xlii, 16, 17; xviii, 1-5). Did not God make his Saints to declare all his wonderful works, *which the Lord Almighty hath firmly settled to be established for his glory?* (Ecclus. xlii, 17). *Lo, these things are said in part of his ways: and seeing we have heard scarce a little drop of his word, who shall be able to behold the thunder of his greatness?* (Job xxvi, 14). For *we are wrapped up in darkness*, and worthily *we cannot find him: he is great in strength, and in judgement, and in justice, and he is ineffable. Therefore men shall fear him, and all that seem to themselves to be wise, shall not dare to behold him* (Job xxxvii, 19, 23, 24). *For who among men is he that can know the counsel of God? For the thoughts of mortal men are fearful, and our counsels uncertain. And hardly do we guess aright at things that are upon earth: and with labour do we find the things that are before us. But the things that are in heaven, who shall search out?* (Wisd. ix, 13-16).

### Article VI

*Of the shortest and best manner and means of obtaining saving wisdom and truly wholesome knowledge*

As that great Philosopher asserted, all men by nature desire to know. For knowledge is the perfection of the understanding. Indeed the highest Truth has placed our beatitude in knowledge (John xvii, 3): not, plainly, in any knowledge whatever, but in the knowledge of him who is substantially eternal truth and the first goodness, pure, simply perfect and infinite, that is, the truth of God. Of which there is a manifold knowledge: one of the way, another of the homeland; one by faith, another by sight. The former is *through a glass in a dark manner*, the latter *face to face* (I Cor. xiii, 12). Towards the latter we tend by desiring, by striving for purity, by insisting on the other exercises of the virtues, above all by growing in divine and holy love, in which the more we advance, the more clearly and joyfully we shall behold for ever, without any interruption, that fountain of infinite cleanness, the most blessed, most beautiful and most lovable God.

Behold how great a happiness is promised us: the clear, certain, face-to-face, fruitive, contenting and wholly quieting sight of God. When he is so seen, nothing further can be desired: rather, as one who lay naked in the midst of a burning furnace closed in on every side, certain that he would never come out, would be so full within and without of pains and torments that he could scarcely contain himself, wholly swallowed up in sufferings; so those most blessed ones are so full and overfilled with every sweetness and joy, every peace, every security, delight and tranquillity, that they can scarcely contain themselves. Behold, this is our fountain, to which we ought to journey untiringly and indefatigably, contemplating, praising and with all our strength honouring the Lord our God in this exile assiduously, if not continuously, by inward and outward acts of worship.

Yet on these things Scripture instructs us more plainly, more wholesomely and more fully: namely, that by fervent prayers, by heartfelt aspirations, by radiant inspirations, by burning contemplations, by inward addresses, by loving embraces we should draw near to him who is within us, more inward to us even than we are to ourselves, and as often as we fix the mind's sight on him, so often be set on fire with his holy and sweet love and be melted into him. But you will say: Who can fulfil this? Hear counsel and teaching. What is easier than to look on him who is everywhere and always present, shining in all things and presenting himself in every place, and through all things and each exhorting us to turn and persevere in him? Look up to heaven, *for it is the throne of God* (Matth. v, 34). And you yourself, if you will, are the throne of God, who is the indweller of the just. Attend to yourself, who made you. Who created your soul? Who formed your body? Who jointed and distinguished your members, and prepared the organs of your inward and outward senses, and bestowed on them the sensitive powers, so that in a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, in the tiny pupil of the eye you perceive heavenly and earthly bodies, and grasp the images, colours, figures, multitude, differences and agreement of such different things? Who devised, instituted and preserved this fellowship, proportion and influence of sensible things and the senses, of objects and powers, so that sensible things impress themselves on the senses through their species and likenesses, and those species have such a being in the media and in the senses themselves? How does sensible light multiply and spread itself all round? Behold, these lowly things are done so wonderfully and so subtly that they can neither be fully expressed nor sufficiently wondered at; and unless it were established by experience that they happen so, we would say it was impossible for them to happen so. How then do we count it hard to behold him in all things and to feel him present, whom through most evident and admirable effects, wholly innumerable, we know and feel to be present, working and pouring in?

Since these things are so, why do you, wretched and miserable man, nay foolish and negligent, let yourself be distracted by many things, wander, and, like a ship tossing in the middle of the sea, be thrown this way and that? Fix yourself immovably in God; nowhere and never turn away from him. Follow him who says: *My eyes are ever towards the Lord* (Ps. xxiv, 15); and: *I set the Lord always in my sight* (Ps. xv, 8). For how shall we be able to look on so beautiful and so delightful a good without being set on fire for it, and without a felt tasting of it, unless it is that we are still too little and childish, and there is little light in us, and the bosom of our most wretched heart is not opened in him nor spread out luminously in him? What then remains, but that we draw nearer to him, not in place, for in place we are not distant from him, but in likeness, by casting off and unceasingly avoiding sins, which alone are not from him, because they are not, that is, positively and formally they are nothing, and they make the created mind unlike its Creator, displeasing, contrary and rebellious to him. These are vanity, the wicked love of self, distance and turning away from him who alone is essentially true, good and happy, and the salvation of us all. In him alone let all our affection be, all our intention, all our striving. Let us lay hold of him and not let him go *unless he bless us* (Gen. xxxii, 26), that is, unless by increases of grace, by the continuance of his mercies, he lead and carry us over into the kingdom of the vessels of his mercy, where in him we may remain for ever stable, immovable and unchangeable, growing no further, but most happily reposing and resting in the eternal embrace of eternal love.

For *where his treasure is, there is his heart also* (Matth. vi, 21), and where love is, there is the eye. What indeed draws and attracts so strongly, joins and binds so closely and intimately, so inseparably unites and connects, as heartfelt and inward love?

THE SOUL. And behold, O Lord, who art essentially charity (I John iv, 8, 16), how graciously and sweetly these things sound! But how shall I, wretched, weighed down by the heavy, muddy and foul mass of the flesh, reach this height, I who am hindered, called back and pulled apart by so many and such great necessities, occupations and comings and goings?

### Article VII

*Of the unceasing fixing of the mind on God*

THE BRIDEGROOM. Nothing is loved unless it is known: and the better, more perfect and more excellent a thing is in itself and of its own nature, and the more connaturally it is known, the more strongly it stirs and draws the beholder to love of it, unless from elsewhere the opposite or some defect intervenes, such as indisposition or want of exercise in the knower. Now the mind devoted to its God, made like to him through the gifts of grace and inward exercises, seriously intent on divine things, already cleansed from vices and the immoderations of the passions, vehemently detesting all torpor, sloth and negligence, strenuously pursuing and manfully trampling them, has through the supernatural theological virtues been carried over into the fellowship of the divine nature, taken up as the bride of its Creator and Saviour, who says to it: *I have loved thee with an everlasting love, therefore have I drawn thee, taking pity on thee* (Jer. xxxi, 3). Therefore it has been fitted and raised to continual burnings in its beloved, through the gift of wisdom and enlightened faith and diligent frequency. Hence it is connatural to it and delightful to rest, dwell and abide in God.

THE BRIDEGROOM. Now, now take heed, O worst of souls, not to say trickster and harlot, who continually go whoring with wood and stone, who give entry to every fancy that comes, and for the easiest occasions and the lightest causes, nay so base that they are not to be called causes but most foolish figments, leave me and attend to your distractions and follies. Do you not know how the lovers, men and women, of this world and of the foul flesh love one another? how they run about even in the hours of night? to what great dangers they are exposed in this, and meanwhile to wounds, and for the madness of love can scarcely sleep or eat? Do they not dry up, wither, and at times languish dreadfully with love? And what have you found in me contrary to loveableness and acceptability, that you cannot be as intensely affected towards me as they are towards one another, and towards perishable and vain things, silver, gold and the like that pass away? Why are you neither corrected by reason nor restrained by precepts? Why do you rather choose to be the adulteress of demons than the friend and bride of your God?

THE SOUL. Far be that madness from me, Lord. I love you above all things, and I desire always to love you. But how I may be able to ascend to the most sublime and intimate union and highest degree of your divine love just touched on, I greatly desire to be taught.

THE BRIDEGROOM. One who desires above all to please another, and to be heard by him in all things, what is incumbent on him? what is necessary for him?

THE SOUL. That he offend him in nothing, and always do what is pleasing to him.

THE BRIDEGROOM. What do you conclude from this?

THE SOUL. I see myself caught: for the defect is on my side, and I see that it is to be imputed to my negligences and vices that I do not love you sincerely and purely, nor cleave to you with my whole heart, nor endure in the flame of your love, because before you I do not persist in the due watchfulness. Nay, every day I am guilty innumerable times, and admit things contrary to your love or lessening it, in commissions and omissions, in thoughts and wanderings of the mind, in words and deeds, in movements of the affections and passions, in the unbridling of the inward and outward senses, in the taking of what the corruptible flesh needs; and surely, as Isaias declared, *all my justices are as the rag of a menstruous woman* (Is. lxiv, 6). But if my justices are so foul, what have I to confess and say of my injustices themselves?

THE BRIDEGROOM. Now you draw near to truth and light.

THE SOUL. Help me, O Lord my God; *O my God, enlighten my darkness* (Ps. xvii, 29).

THE BRIDEGROOM. How do you understand what Solomon said: *He that feareth God neglecteth nothing?* (Eccles. vii, 19).

THE SOUL. As far as belongs to the plain understanding, I well believe that one who fears God with a filial and actual fear neglects nothing of what is needful for salvation. But as there are different degrees of love, of humility and of the other virtues, so also of fear: therefore the more anyone has grown in true and holy fear, or even in initial fear, or at least in servile fear, the less negligent will he be, and the more diligent in guarding against sins, as far as concerns his fear.

THE BRIDEGROOM. Do you understand from this in what sense Job spoke: *I have always feared God as waves swelling over me?* (Job xxxi, 23).

THE SOUL. He who said this also says: *Neither doth my heart reprehend me in all my life* (Job xxvii, 6). If he so unceasingly considered and attended to the presence of almighty God and the sight of the supreme Judge, he could (unless I am mistaken) say such things for the honour of God and the edification of others, while still weighing the common frailty and weakness of men, by which no one at all is asserted to be free of fault.

THE BRIDEGROOM. How do these stand together, since it is written: *In many things we all offend?* (James iii, 2).

THE SOUL. I ask to be instructed by you, Lord.

THE BRIDEGROOM. Virtuous, holy and God-fearing souls, standing continually upon their watch (Habac. ii, 1), and spending no particle of time uselessly, exercise the acts of very many virtues one after another and in order: now praying for the forgiveness of daily faults and the increase of grace and virtues, now for the common good of the Church, now for the living and the dead and very many other pious causes; and at times they are intent on a most diligent and fearful examination of their conscience, and on a most powerful inward contrition: in which most secret acts they often feel inward consolations poured in and mingled, of the hope of pardon, and of obtaining a more abundant assistance of the Holy Spirit. And so, although they sin daily in those things without which the present life is not lived, yet on the other side they grow and advance more through the acts of virtue just touched on, and through continual and fervent aspirations, elevations and burnings towards God, and through the other good things which they do continually, by which they offer themselves to God in holocaust. In adversities too they keep patience, and give thanks to God.

### Article VIII

*A continuation of this matter*

From what has been set out you must gather, O my soul, that in order to advance in the stability of holy love it is necessary for you to strive for this: that you unceasingly do the things that please God, and avoid the things that offend and dishonour him; then that you always weigh his loveableness, honourableness and excellence, and so, as far as you can, be worthily and continually affected towards it. In these things is contained for the greatest part what in the Scriptures is taught, commanded, counselled and scattered here and there. O how blessed is he who is unceasingly careful before God not to offend him in anything, and continually fearful lest he displease him, busy and diligent lest he be parted from him!

Continually, then, behold the excellence, preciousness and graciousness of your God, so infinite, so incomparable, so supremely lovable; let nothing draw you back from beholding, tasting and venerating him; place yourself everywhere in his presence and sight, nay, consider that you are placed there; never turn the eye of the mind away from him, by whom you are unceasingly and most clearly seen, and, he granting it, may you behold him. For he is that most high God, of whose incomparable, incomprehensible, superessential, wholly infinite and uncircumscribable nature and majesty the more excellent of men have discoursed many and great things: that he is being itself, most pure, most actual, in every way and simply perfect, in whom all nobility of being, and whatever belongs to eminence, splendour and nobility of being, is eternally possessed. And what more can be said? He is pure goodness, immense beauty, unbounded sweetness, in comparison with whom all other things together are as a little dust and the finest sand (Is. xl, 15). And he is infinite power, omnipotent virtue, separated holiness, wisdom unbounded in every form: whose riches have no end, whose delights are intensively immense; and from his condign praise and full honouring all things that are about him fall short utterly and infinitely. Before him, as Porphyry, one of the philosophers, confessed, earth, sea and sky tremble, and the very deities shudder.

*And in all these things the heart of man is senseless* (Ecclus. xvi, 20). And you, O my most wretched soul, remain deaf and darkened, and are not set on fire for God. And if perhaps for a brief moment you are kindled, you quickly grow cold, and do not remain held in wonder at so infinite a beauty. What could you find more delightful than it, or indeed what so delightful, from whose sweetness all other things fall infinitely short? One who preferred mud and dung to gold and gems, what would he be judged?

Further, for the doing of this it especially avails that you consider most diligently, as far as you can, with grace helping, who, of what kind, and how great God is, nay, the whole of it; and hold and account his presence and sight so great that you attend to other things only as he has taught or commanded. Love him, therefore, incomparably and most fervently, whom you have present everywhere and always, nay, unceasingly ready to be addressed in the prayers and praises offered him, and in every reasonable address, in which he never repels or puts off the one who draws near. Note that every reason for honourable love consists and is found in him with infinite excess. That is, all goodness, beauty, wisdom, sweetness, holiness, liberality, nobility, wealth, charity, kindness, sincerity, excellence, and altogether eternal perfection, beneficence, and the whole reason of good and virtuous friendship and love is in God with infinite transcendence and excess.

If each thing is lovable in so far as it is good, how lovable is he who is pure and uncircumscribable goodness, whose nature is goodness pure, perfect and infinite! What madness it is to prefer anything to him or to love it equally, or to seek anything outside or besides him, as if it were not had in him! If a man came to you whom you knew to be of such goodness as holy Paul, Antony or Martin was, with what reverence would you rise and receive him! with what eagerness and diligence would you look at him! how affectionately and gladly would you stay with him! how ill would you bear it if someone took him from you! What then is this perversity, this madness, that you do not aspire with your whole heart to the gracious presence of God, to his consoling coming, nay to his most saving indwelling, that you are not vehemently affected towards his secret addresses, that you do not long with the highest affection to stay and persevere with him?

Hear this, outward and miserable man, especially you who wear the appearance of a religious but have forsaken its virtue; whom it delights more to chatter with men and take in empty rumours than to mingle conversation with the Lord your God, than to speak to the Most High in hymns, psalms and prayers, and to pour out your heart before him (Lam. ii, 19). See where you lie prostrate, how abject before all the Saints and how basely cast down. Reading this, be pierced, beat your breast, not without shame and enormous grief. Look to heaven, call on God, and from the deepest humility, with very great contrition, with groaning and sobbing and flowing tears cry: *O God, be merciful to me*, a most unhappy *sinner* (Luke xviii, 13). Alas! what shall I do? where shall I turn, who till now have been so negligent, and have so senselessly lost the time granted me for repenting and advancing, nay, have so viciously consumed it? Eternal God, *who knowest hidden things* (Dan. xiii, 42), I am confounded and *ashamed to lift up my face to thee* (I Esdras ix, 6), for the innumerable multitude and gravity of my faults.

You see, besides, how greatly the most beautiful youths, in whom there is no blemish, are loved, delightfully gazed on, eagerly embraced: how incomparably, then, and how vehemently is that almighty Lord to be loved, desired and embraced, who is essentially beautiful and infinitely fair, in comparison with whom every created thing that is beautiful, luminous and comely is of no moment, even if it were a hundred times, a thousand times, inexpressibly more beautiful, more pleasant and more acceptable than it is? How are you, most dull mind, so depressed and sunk that these things have no savour for you, nor kindle you, nor lift you above yourself, not to say ravish you? Of the beauty of Absalom, of Paris and of Adonis much is read, and how many hastened to look on them: and behold how slight and little to be reckoned was their comeliness that showed on the surface, which under such a skin teemed with so many foulnesses, stenches and dungs, so many uncleannesses and superfluities! But their souls were defiled with the filthiest vices, with foul ambitions, lusts, obscenities and the gravest crimes. Besides, contemplate how supremely comely that Maker is in himself, who in so small a portion of flesh has placed such beauty that those who look on it are scarcely sated or wearied. How then do you, my detestable soul, not delight in the consideration and contemplation of your most beautiful God, whose surpassing loveliness all created things proclaim to you, especially heavenly and supercelestial things, the order of the heavens, the whole company of angels, the heavenly hierarchies, the choirs above?

So weigh his other properties and eminences. You know that all the earth desired to see the face of Solomon for the wisdom given him from above (III Kings x, 24). And what is that wisdom in comparison with uncreated wisdom but folly and darkness? To the fountain of infinite wisdom, then, unite yourself with your whole heart, and rejoice in all his excellence, and congratulate him with your whole affection. Say to him: Blessed and more than blessed be you, O Lord my God, of whose wisdom there is no end, who alone are eternally, independently and incomprehensibly wise, and are yourself superessential wisdom, for whom to be wise and to be understanding is the same as to be. Further, you alone are truly almighty, who preserve all things, without whose upholding not even the world itself could subsist or endure for a moment. Nay, whatever can be thought of as lovable, noble, estimable, sweet, precious or worthy is wholly in you, and you yourself are this with infinite perfection and excellence. Now, then, be yourself sweet and savoury to me above all things; ravish and hold my whole affection in you. Whatever hinders, withdraws or distances me from you, let me loathe, avoid, abhor; everything by which I can please you, draw near to you or honour you, let it be pleasant and light to me. *Set* your *eye*, O my soul, *upon* your *ways* (Agg. i, 5, 7), that there be nothing in them to displease the Almighty, all of whose *ways are beautiful* ways (Prov. iii, 17).

### Article IX

*Of the continual and earnest examination of oneself*

With what face, O my soul, do you ask daily and every hour to be forgiven, you who do not desist from your past and accustomed negligences, torpors and sloths, nay, continually heap evils on evils, so much so that you cannot count them, nor in sacramental confession bring out all and each distinctly, though you confess daily? Take heed, poor little one, and vehemently lament your innumerable calamities, defects and faults.

Alas, alas, alas for me, O Lord my God, how infinite is my misery! I confess daily that, to my grief, in my Hours, in my psalmody, in prayers and celebrations and in all the divine Office I am distracted in heart, bearing myself irreverently and without watchfulness: and behold, in every psalm, or rather in every verse, I am distracted in mind, nor do I weigh the sense with due diligence, and at times I scarcely pronounce the very words whole. I am impatient, angry, indignant, if others do not chant, do not read, do not sing according to my will; and I am not rather indignant with myself, that I bear myself so irreverently, so defectively and so badly before you in all your service. Alas, how unprepared I come to celebrate! how unworthily I bear myself in celebrating! how ungrateful I am found! how quickly I return to idle talk, and am thrown headlong into my former evils!

What does it profit you, O wretched one, to confess daily, you who a little after, confession now made and celebration too performed, are as unfearing, as light and unguarded as before? Of what contrition is this a sign? nay, of how great a dissoluteness, ingratitude, forgetfulness and unseemliness is this a token and an effect, of how great a guilt and punishment a cause, especially since even lay people, on the day on which they communicate, are wont to be more serious, more devout and more exemplary than usual? Where, O senseless one, is the spiritual fattening from so frequent a taking of heavenly and divine foods? Lament yourself, then. For he who so superabundantly gives himself to you as food, his own body and blood as refreshment, is assuredly ready to confer on you a fuller grace and a most loving hearing, unless you yourself set a bar and offer daily obstacles. O would that you rightly recalled how, according to the description of the divine Dionysius (On the Ecclesiastical Hierarchy, ch. 3), in the primitive Church the priests went to celebrate with admirable devotion and most diligent preparation! Therefore they obtained most abundant fruit, and returned like lions breathing flame, terrible to the adverse powers, nourished in the manner of heaven.

Consider how sober you ought to be in the food and drink of the body, how free from culpable words, you who through your mouth receive the most sacred body of the Lord and his divine blood, and utter those heavenly words. And contemplate more diligently how gravely and in how many ways you are guilty in these things. Further, from the vices of the flesh, from lustful touch, from shameless looking, you must remove, withdraw and abstain yourself infinitely, and untiringly hasten to *better things, and nearer to salvation* (Hebr. vi, 9). What have you to do with base things, with unfruitful conversations, with sterile studies, you who are called to so supernatural and most divine a priesthood? Lest in you too there be damnably fulfilled what the divine word says: *Man when he was in honour did not understand* (Ps. xlviii, 13, 21). This is good magnanimity and true nobility of heart, to be unwilling to degenerate from the nobility of grace. Let your whole way of living be a continual disposition for celebrating: nay, in unceasing turns offer to God the Father his only Son, that is, all that he took on, did and bore for the salvation of men.

### Article X

*Of the things to be meditated concerning the incarnation of the only-begotten Son of God and his other mysteries*

Christ *is made unto us* by God the Father *wisdom, and justice, and sanctification, and redemption* (I Cor. i, 30). The glory and wisdom of the Christian is to remember Christ and his mysteries continually. Recall, then, O my soul, the only Son of the eternal Father sent into the world for your salvation, so that by his incarnation he might deify you, by his most bitter passion snatch you from eternal damnation, by his expiring on the cross quicken you for ever with the life of grace in the present and the life of glory in the future.

What is so delightful and pleasing to the true Christian as continually to think over these things: how the most pure and most innocent Virgin, most excellently adorned beforehand with all holiness, at the announcing of the holy Archangel, remaining most whole, ineffably conceived the most beautiful Son of God, made a little child of her, truly man, from the first moment of his incarnation consummated as to his soul in all holiness and perfection of virtues, nay, completed with beatific fruition, and then in his time coming forth from her most holy womb? How much the devout faithful ought to glory daily in these most divine mysteries is made known from this, that then the host of angels so vehemently gloried in them, gave thanks and sang: *Glory to God in the highest* (Luke ii, 14).

Weigh, poor little man, what and how great things the Most High then did for your salvation, who for your saving and building up took on such extreme and utmost poverty, humiliation and abasement, so that he was *wrapped in swaddling clothes, and laid in* the narrowness of *a manger* (Luke ii, 12), refreshed at his Mother's breasts; and so thereafter, from the beginning of his birth to his expiring, in all things chose whatever could belong to the fullness of poverty and the abundant suffering of adversities and tribulations. Who then are you, who wish to be called Christian from Christ, and not to follow Christ's footsteps in these things? What do you make yourself? Do you think yourself more excellent than the Son of God? Woe to you who desire to abound in riches, to be raised by honours, to be refreshed by delights, delicately nourished, conformed to this world. Look, wretched one, of what head you are a member, of what lord a servant, of what master a disciple. Behold in what rigour of discipline, in what depth of humility, in what patience and hardness and affliction of body the most chosen Christians from the beginning of the Church have imitated him, and the glorious martyrs too, nay, very many most tender virgins and most noble youths and young men who entered the cloister and the desert. And you, by their example, humble yourself to the utmost; *crucify your flesh with its vices and concupiscences* (Gal. v, 24); walk strenuously along the narrow way of salvation. *Be not weary in well doing* (II Thess. iii, 13): rather, daily adding fervour to fervour, grow towards the better, until you happily end in the best.

### Article XI

*Of meditating on Christ's way of living*

*He that saith he abideth in him, ought himself also to walk, even as he walked* (I John ii, 6). The whole occupation of true Christians, the whole intention of the faithful of Christ and all their affection, consists in the passion of the only-begotten Son of God. This mystery is great, nay of immense depth, transcending the philosophy of all the gentiles, exceeding the whole art of natural inquiry. O hidden and most profound object of divine contemplation, most radiant mirror, how by the death of life the human race was redeemed from eternal death! Those first parents, nay the destroyers of their own stock, by the demerit of their transgression infected their posterity, so that it was deprived of the gift of original justice, as if an earthly prince or king should withdraw from his soldier the feudal good which he had bestowed on him for himself and his offspring, so long as he persisted in faithful service. As therefore the whole body of descendants was by their forefather's demerit stripped of so great a gift of grace, so by the virtue and merit of their Creator, in the humanity taken on by him for them, it was repaired from so great a loss, nay, adorned with more eminent gifts of graces. For to all who believe in the incarnate Son of God *he gave power to be made the sons of God* (John i, 12) and to obtain eternal salvation. So by the merit of Christ's obedience, and of the things which he took on, did and bore for us, we are saved, inasmuch as the Son of God, out of the highest charity, the greatest kindness, the fullest obedience and the most vehement humility, honoured himself and the eternal Father, and laid low the enemies of human salvation. He also showed the most effective way of reaching the prize of the heavenly calling, in himself and by himself, by his examples and teachings, giving likewise most wholesome and most wise counsels, by the keeping of which one comes more shortly and more gloriously to that immense beatitude: which he most excellently fulfilled in himself, living here in every kind of poverty, in the highest humility, in the most tranquil patience under manifold persecution.

In these things, O my soul, it is good for us to remain, and to walk with the Lord Jesus as it were in his childhood. Let us go out with the most holy and most pure Virgin from Nazareth towards Bethlehem, her delivery now at hand; and let us say to Joseph: O good and innocent old man, where do you propose to go with this most tender and incomparably gentle Lady, her delivery now pressing? Where will you find for her a quiet and fitting lodging in such a throng of travellers? And you will hear him answering you in spirit: It is a mystery disposed by God; he will dispose all things on this journey according to the height and excellence of his providence. And you will say: I will follow you from afar, if perhaps I can serve you in anything, and be present at the wonders of God, to publish and bear witness of them. And so, midnight now drawing near, contemplate how the most blessed virgin Mary, most familiar with God, with most fervent mind, with fullest devotion, on bended knees praying most affectionately for the salvation of the world, and giving most heartfelt thanks to God the Father, in an instant bears God and man in one person; and soon you will rejoice in spirit that the oracles of the Prophets are fulfilled, and that the king, the Messias, is born, and that now there is in the world a work divinely done, greater, more wonderful and more glorious than the world itself: in which incomprehensible work that almighty craftsman, the author and maker of wholly infinite wisdom, declared the immensity of his wisdom, omnipotence and kindness more gloriously and more eminently than in the production of the whole world.

Besides, hear the holy angel declaring to the shepherds: *I bring you good tidings of great joy: for this day is born to you a Saviour, who is Christ the Lord, in the city of David* (Luke ii, 10, 11). At the hearing and announcing of these things do you too inwardly rejoice, give thanks to God with all your strength, and for so inestimable a kindness and grace shown and bestowed on the human race exult with your whole heart, in that God and their Creator has become their brother and saviour. Then note at once the army of the heavenly host, doubtless of innumerable angels, glorying vehemently also in the restoring of their own ruin, praising the most glorious God and saying: *Glory to God in the highest* (Luke ii, 13, 14).

Note too, in these things, of what kind and how great this most singular Virgin is, chosen from eternity by the supremely incomparable Trinity to conceive and bear the only-begotten Son of the eternal Father, to handle and rear him, to minister to him and dwell with him in most familiar company. Since she was made co-parent with the eternal Father, and the truest mother of his Only-begotten, true God, and the mother too of him from whom the Holy Spirit truly and eternally proceeds, it is plain how near and how dear she was made to the whole most worthy Trinity. Since she is the glorious Mother of the true God, she is assuredly of a dignity in some way infinite, and after God is to be supremely loved and venerated by us all.

Besides, you must most devoutly and joyfully contemplate, devout worshipper of Christ, how the three Magi, led by the star, came to Bethlehem to adore the little child, and offered him mystical gifts (Matth. ii), and how after these things the child Jesus was carried into Egypt, and brought back; how at last he was found in the temple by the Virgin Mother (Luke ii, 46), then in humility kept silence until about his thirtieth year, when he deigned to be baptised by his most holy minister; a little after was tempted by Satan, and then began openly to preach the gospel, to teach, to shine with miracles, and to found the evangelical law.

Contemplate Jesus, the sermons and teachings of the heavenly kingdom completed, most fervently longing to redeem the human race, to lead the souls of the fathers out of limbo, to restore the ruin of the heavenly Jerusalem, to overthrow the powers of the air, to convert the world through the holy Apostles, foretelling to them that his passion was close at hand (Matth. xxvi, 2), and celebrating with them the last supper (Matth. xxvi, 20 and following), in which he instituted the supernatural sacrament of the evangelical law. Above all, he showed, commended and taught his charity and humility, first by deeds, then by words (John xiii, 4 and following). Then he went out with the glorious Apostles, setting out to them the mystery of his passion, of his resurrection and of the sending of the Holy Spirit (John xvi, 4 and following). Then before three of his familiar disciples he began to fear, as much and in what manner he willed (Mark xiv, 33), for most reasonable causes; and went undaunted to meet his enemies, whom, as he willed, he suddenly laid low. By them he was taken, held and killed by a most cruel and most shameful death; and on the third day, as he had foretold, rose again most victoriously. His resurrection too being sufficiently made plain and proved, he ascended into heaven before his disciples, the holy angels standing by and bearing witness that he would return at the end of the ages to judge the living and the dead (Acts i, 9-11).

### Article XII

*Of Christ's passion*

*With Christ I am nailed to the cross* (Gal. ii, 19). The whole way of living of true Christians, all their intention, affection and devotion, turns about the passion of Christ, in which the perfections of all the virtues shine most fully. In this most saving passion of Christ, then, consider, and with all your strength imitate, the highest and most perfect charity of Christ, by which he so laid down his life for his little sheep (John x, 15); his most complete obedience, by which he became obedient to the Father even unto such a death (Philipp. ii, 8); his patience, by which he interceded even for those who crucified him (Luke xxiii, 34); his humility, silence, meekness, and every virtue which he taught in his passion. All of which you must fittingly weigh, in order to suffer with him, to imitate, to give thanks, to be set on fire, to wonder, to contemplate, to fight: not cursorily, but maturely, continually, with a contrite and most grateful mind, so as to prevail most swiftly in every adversity, persecution and temptation: for every tribulation of the present life is borne with an even mind, if the bitterness of the Lord's passion is rightly recalled.

But in this most intent remembrance of all that your God bore for you, you must place the sum of your study. In the affectionate and inwardly compassionate remembrance of these things set up the fullness of your wisdom; and impress the Lord and Saviour, in that disposition, form and array and painfulness, and in every manner in which he hung on the cross for you, most deeply and indelibly on your heart, and set him unceasingly before the eyes of your heart, and grow accustomed, in every distraction and dispersion, to recall, bring back and fix the mind's gaze untiringly on him so hanging. Which will profit you, and everyone who does this, unspeakably for salvation, for abundant advance, for bridling all dissoluteness, lightness and inertia of mind.

Think and think again on all those things as if you saw them present. Who, of what kind and how great he is who suffered them, weigh with affection, and why, for what fruit, to what end he bore all the affliction of his most tender body in his most violent stretching, in his most painful scourging, in the piercing of his most holy and most tender feet and hands, in the thorny crowning of his most divine head, and in the most grievous suffering of his most loving heart, which indeed then exceeded the pain of the bodily passion. In these let your occupation be frequent, continual, persevering. Recalling which, pray with tears and continually for the salvation of the whole world, for the living and the dead, for all your kin, those entrusted to you and your benefactors, for grace and a worthy advance, for abstinence from sins, weighing what dishonesty, ingratitude and perversity it is to offend such and so great a benefactor.

### Article XIII

*Of the excellence and greatness of the grace distributed by Christ to the faithful*

The time of the evangelical law is not undeservedly called the time of grace: although from the beginning of the ages the Almighty has bestowed great and various gifts of graces on men. But from the day on which true God became man, it was especially fitting that the things of God should be imparted to men more abundantly.

Hence after Christ's passion and resurrection the Holy Spirit was at once visibly sent and given to his disciples and to the primitive Church: who were most admirably filled with the supernatural gifts of the Holy Spirit, both the gifts of sanctifying grace and the gifts of grace freely given; and they began to speak in the tongues of all nations, and most clearly understood and most readily cited the scriptures of the old Testament, and shone most abundantly with miracles, as the effect most fully and most certainly showed. For notwithstanding all the rage of innumerable Jews, especially of the leaders, that is, the chief priests and the other rulers and elders, who implacably raged against the very few disciples of Christ, they none the less daily grew, advanced and prevailed over all their adversaries, so much so that within a short time they converted so many thousands of Jews, both in their chief city Jerusalem and in all Judea and Galilee and Samaria and the neighbouring places, that more seem to have believed in Christ than not, although the faithless Jews tried in every way possible to them to wipe out all those believers.

Then the disciples of Christ themselves, especially the holy and glorious Apostles, leaving the unbelievers in Judea, were scattered and went forth through the whole world, until by themselves and through their disciples they converted the greatest part of the human race to Christ. Which was in truth the highest wonder, namely that those few, by divine virtue, without human power and magic art, converted so many peoples, nations and races to so most arduous a law and so incomprehensible a faith, with Emperors, kings, princes and tyrants resisting on every side, who inflicted the cruellest deaths on believers; with the magicians too and philosophers resisting, and all their cunning. And so at last the Christian religion grew and prevailed in the whole world, above all amid the tyrannies of the wicked, so that there were so many and such great most holy fathers, anchorites and hermits leading a most spiritual and most arduous life in deserts, caves and solitudes: a life indeed so angelic and divine, so superhuman and most virtuous, full of so many and such great miracles, that it could not be fully described, nay, in truth has scarcely been described in particular for a small part. For as of these few writings have come down to the very many and wholly innumerable Christians dwelling in the most remote parts of India, Armenia, Persia, Media and so on; so of the deeds and wonderful prodigies and magnificences of the martyrs, fathers, prelates and most divine men, nay of heavenly persons of both sexes, scarcely a hundredth or a thousandth part has reached us in the west. Yet these very things, comparatively speaking, have been so many and such, so glorious and innumerable, that they transcend every manner and faculty of telling, praising and wondering, so that what happened of old under the law of Moses seems to bear no comparison with them.

Hence the truth of the Christian faith is most certain, and all the heavenly doctrine of the Church, although now, alas, through the ruin, carnality, perversity, ingratitude and most enormous crimes of Christians, *the gold is changed* and turned *into dross* (Is. i, 22), and *the finest colour is changed* (Lam. iv, 1) into a deadly pallor. Alas! where now is the most lovely beauty of the holy religious orders, the most splendid brightness of innumerable most devout cloisters, of venerable colleges? O who would not weary of the present life, if he read, noted and thought over what has been written of these things in the authentic books of the most blessed and most divine Dionysius, and in other histories, chronicles, legends, historical mirrors and most trustworthy books, and at the same time saw, weighed and pondered one by one how, alas, alas, alas! now all things are changed, deformed, discoloured, overturned!

### Article XIV

*A lament over the evils of the Church*

*Through the voice of my groaning, my bone hath cleaved to my flesh* (Ps. ci, 6). The more sincerely and intensely we love the Lord our God, the more heartily we desire his veneration, praise and glory, and the more vehemently we glory in his glorification, and the more insistently and untiringly we labour for this very thing, by living exemplarily, by praying unceasingly and fervently, by exhorting our neighbours, by correcting offenders, and by labouring in every way possible to us for the salvation of souls, above all by labouring for the common good. In the same way, the more closely we embrace our neighbours and the whole human race with the affection of charity, the more amply we burn with zeal for their salvation. In like manner, the more perfectly and spiritually we are zealous for the Lord and Saviour, God and man, the more affectionately we desire that his passion, the price of his blood, and all that he did and bore for our salvation, should bear fruit. Besides, the more deeply we look on the last things of those whom we are bound to love as ourselves, the more fearfully we shudder at that unbearable damnation of theirs, and the more inwardly we long for their most desirable salvation.

From this it is made known how unspeakably the true lover of God and the spiritual man is continually afflicted, saddened and grieved at the ruin and deformation of the Church and the daily perdition of innumerable souls, above all since the monstrousness of vices is so great, and the boasting of the most cruel demons in all evils. Since, then, we see unceasingly so many crimes committed, so many souls perishing, must we not most vehemently groan, be saddened and grieve, and never give place to vanity, dissoluteness, jests and games? Do we not see that faithful and honest servants, and above all good sons, cannot bear with an even mind the injuries, insults and scorn done to their lords and fathers?

Therefore, if there are in you the bowels of Christian piety and the affection of true charity, in the consideration of these things groan inwardly, weep continually, pray most fervently; resist the devil's contriving, labour for the honouring of God. Weep with drops of blood over the mutilation of the Church in all its borders, and intercede most affectionately that the almighty and most merciful Lord may deign to provide his Church with true and fit pastors and prelates, and to confer amendment in every state, degree and order. Let this be your daily exercise and occupation. *Pour out thy heart like water before the face of the Lord* your God (Lam. ii, 19) for the souls of the little ones, lest they perish scandalised.

*Remember*, O my soul, *thy last end* (Ecclus. vii, 40), and that of all who are pilgrims with you in this life. Exhort all as far as you can. Examine your life frequently, especially at some fitting hour. Attend to how you spend the single hours of the day; how many, what kind and how great wounds you incur in them, how you may avoid them daily, and diligently and more reverently discharge and fulfil every work of God. Contemplate faithfully the eternal happiness and glory of those who in this life were subject to their Creator with due diligence; see what inestimable happiness they have obtained, what evils and dangers they have escaped, what unlosable security they have won, and imitate them strenuously. Unceasingly turn over and turn over again the things of God, and meditate wholesome things. Direct the eyes of your mind everywhere and always to the Lord; continually weigh, fear and never cease to venerate his holy and most venerable presence. Walk through all the beatitude of the elect, how gloriously and most delightfully they enjoy the most happy Trinity, and eternally and unchangeably feel and taste the mutual beholding, enjoyment, love, embrace and peace of it, and love one another in God, and God above all things, without end, with inestimable tranquillity and sweetness.

This little work of my meditations, which I do not so much have as ought by rights to have, receive gladly, dearest brothers, and pray for me continually, who for the rest intend to carry myself over to the harbour of safe silence, and to prepare for a safe departure, as the Lord shall grant, especially since the strength of the body too is greatly failing. And this little work I finish in the sixty-seventh year of my life, in the year of the Lord one thousand four hundred and sixty-nine, to the praise and glory of the Almighty, who is over all things God, sublime and blessed. Amen.

*The end of the little work On Meditation.*
