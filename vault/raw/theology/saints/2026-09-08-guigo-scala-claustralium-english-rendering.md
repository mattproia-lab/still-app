# The Ladder of Monks

## or, A Treatise on the Manner of Praying

[REVIEW NEEDED] Migne's headnote: This treatise is found among the works of St Augustine (in volume VI) under the title "The Ladder of Paradise", with this censure of the Doctors of Louvain: "This little work is also carried among the works of St Bernard, though it seems to be neither's." In a manuscript of the Charterhouse of Cologne it is ascribed to Guigo (the fifth prior of the Grande Chartreuse?). There is another small work on the same subject in Chifflet's Manual of Solitaries, "On the fourfold exercise of the cell", under the name of a different Guigo, to the prior and Carthusians of Witham, where four exercises of the cell are set out, the first three the same as here, reading, meditation and prayer, but the fourth action in place of the contemplation preferred in this treatise, whose heading in the Cologne manuscript reads:

## The letter of Dom Guigo the Carthusian

*To brother Gervase, on the contemplative life*

To his beloved brother Gervase, brother Guigo: joy in the Lord. I am bound to love you by a debt, for you began to love me first; and I am compelled to write back to you, for your letters invited me to write before I had thought of it. So I have resolved to send you certain things I had been thinking over about the spiritual exercise of those who live in the cloister, so that you, who have learned such things better by living them than I by treating of them, may be the judge and the corrector of my thoughts. And it is right that I offer these first fruits of our labour to you before anyone, so that you gather the first fruits of a new planting: for it was you who, stealing yourself away from the slavery of Pharaoh by a praiseworthy theft, set yourself in the ordered ranks of the camp, and, having skilfully cut a branch from the wild olive, prudently grafted it into the good olive.

### Chapter I

*A description of the four rungs of the spiritual exercises*

1. One day, busy with the bodily work of my hands, I began to think about the exercise of the spiritual man, and four spiritual rungs suddenly presented themselves to my thinking mind: reading, meditation, prayer and contemplation. This is the ladder of those who live in the cloister, by which they are lifted from earth to heaven; a ladder of few rungs, yet of immense and unbelievable height, whose lower end rests on the earth while its upper part pierces the clouds and searches the secrets of the heavens. These rungs differ in their names and number, and just so in their order and their office. If anyone looks carefully at what each one's properties and offices do in us, how they differ from one another and how each rises above the last, he will count whatever labour or study he spends on them short and easy against the greatness of their usefulness and sweetness. Reading is the careful looking into the Scriptures with an attentive mind. Meditation is the studious action of the mind, searching out the knowledge of a hidden truth under the guidance of its own reason. Prayer is the devout turning of the heart to God, that evils may be taken away and good things obtained. Contemplation is the lifting up of the mind held fast in God, tasting the joys of everlasting sweetness.

### Chapter II

*A description of the offices of the four rungs*

2. Now that the four rungs have been described, it remains for us to see their offices. Reading seeks the sweetness of the blessed life, meditation finds it, prayer asks for it, contemplation tastes it. So the Lord himself says: *Seek, and you shall find; knock, and it shall be opened to you* (Matthew 7:7). Seek by reading, and you will find by meditating; knock by praying, and it will be opened to you by contemplating. Reading, as it were, puts solid food to the mouth; meditation chews it and breaks it; prayer draws out its savour; contemplation is the sweetness itself, which gladdens and refreshes. Reading is in the husk, meditation in the marrow, prayer in the asking of desire, contemplation in the delight of the sweetness obtained. So that this may be seen more plainly, I will set down one example out of many. In reading I hear: *Blessed are the clean of heart, for they shall see God* (Matthew 5:8). Here is a short saying, but full of sweet and manifold meaning. It is served to the soul, as it were a grape for its food; and when the soul has looked at it carefully, it says within itself: There may be something good here. I will return to my heart and try whether I can understand and find this cleanness. For this is a precious and desirable thing, whose possessors are called blessed, to whom the vision of God, which is eternal life, is promised, and which is praised by so many testimonies of holy Scripture. Wanting, then, to have it explained more fully, the soul begins to chew this grape and break it, and sets it, as it were, in the winepress; then it stirs up the reason to inquire what this cleanness is, and how so precious and desirable a cleanness can be had.

### Chapter III

*The office of meditation*

3. Careful meditation comes near, then, and does not stay outside, does not cling to the surface, but plants its foot further in. It enters the inward parts, searches each thing; it notes attentively that he did not say, Blessed are the clean of *body*, but of *heart*: because it is not enough to keep the hands innocent of evil deeds unless we are cleansed in the mind of wicked thoughts. This the authority of the prophet confirms, who says: *Who shall ascend into the mountain of the Lord, or who shall stand in his holy place? The innocent in hands and the clean of heart* (Psalm 23:3-4). Again it considers how much the same prophet longed for this cleanness of heart, who said in prayer, *Create a clean heart in me, O God* (Psalm 50:12); and again, *If I have looked at iniquity in my heart, the Lord will not hear me* (Psalm 65:18). It thinks how anxious the blessed Job was in this guard, who said: *I made a covenant with my eyes, that I would not so much as think upon a virgin* (Job 31:1). See how strictly the holy man bound himself, who shut his eyes lest he see vanity, lest perhaps he should look unguardedly on what afterwards he would desire against his will. After it has gone through these and similar things about cleanness of heart, it begins to think of its reward: how glorious and delightful it is to see the longed-for face of the Lord, beautiful above the sons of men; no longer cast down and mean, no longer wearing the appearance his mother the Synagogue clothed him in, but robed in the stole of immortality and crowned with the diadem with which his Father crowned him on the day of the resurrection and of glory, the day which the Lord has made. It thinks that in that vision will be the fullness of which the prophet says: *I shall be satisfied when thy glory shall appear* (Psalm 16:15). Do you see how much juice has flowed from the smallest grape; how great a fire has risen from this spark; how far this little lump, *Blessed are the clean of heart, for they shall see God* (Matthew 5:8), has been drawn out on the anvil of meditation? And how much further still it could be drawn, if someone came to it who had lived such things? For I feel that the well is deep; but I am still a raw recruit, and have hardly found what little I have drawn here.

4. Set alight by these torches, stirred by these desires, the soul, the alabaster broken, begins to sense the sweetness of the ointment, not yet by taste but as if by the scent in the nostrils. From this it gathers how sweet it would be to feel the experience of this cleanness, whose mere meditation it has found so pleasant. But what will it do? It burns with the desire to possess, but does not find within itself how it may possess; and the more it seeks, the more it thirsts. As it applies meditation, it applies sorrow too, because it thirsts for the sweetness which meditation shows to be in cleanness of heart, but does not yet taste it. For it is not in the power of the one who reads and meditates to feel this sweetness, unless it is given from above. To read and to meditate is common to the good and the bad alike. Even the philosophers of the nations found, under the guidance of reason, where the highest true good consists; but because, when they had known God, they did not glorify him as God (Romans 1:21), and, presuming on their own powers, said, *We will magnify our tongue; our lips are our own: who is Lord over us?* (Psalm 11:5), they did not deserve to perceive what they were able to see. They became vain in their thoughts (Romans 1:21), and all their wisdom was swallowed up (Psalm 106:27), the wisdom that the study of human learning had brought them, not the Spirit of wisdom, who alone gives true wisdom: that is, savoured knowledge, which gladdens and refreshes with an inestimable savour the soul it has cleaved to. Of that wisdom it is said: *Wisdom will not enter into a malicious soul* (Wisdom 1:4). And this comes from God alone. The Lord granted the office of baptising to many; but the power and authority of forgiving sins in Baptism he kept to himself alone. So John said of him, by way of distinction and as his own name: *He it is that baptiseth* (John 1:33). In the same way we can say of him: He it is that gives the savour of wisdom and makes the soul savoured. Speech is given to many, but wisdom to few; and the Lord distributes it to whom he will, and as he will.

### Chapter IV

*The office of prayer*

5. The soul, then, seeing that of itself it cannot reach the longed-for sweetness of knowledge and experience, and that the more it draws near to the deep heart the more God is exalted (Psalm 63:7-8), humbles itself and takes refuge in prayer, saying: Lord, who are not seen except by the clean of heart, I have searched by reading, I have sought by meditating, how true cleanness of heart may be had, so that by its means I might know you, at least in some small part. I sought thy face, O Lord, thy face I sought after (Psalm 26:8). Long have I meditated in my heart, and in my meditation a fire burned up, and the desire of knowing you more fully (Psalm 38:4). While you break the bread of holy Scripture for me, in the breaking of the bread there is great knowledge (Luke 24:30-31); and the more I know you, the more I desire to know you, no longer in the husk of the letter but in the sense of experience. And I do not ask this, Lord, for my merits, but for your mercy. For I confess that I am unworthy and a sinner; but even the little dogs eat of the crumbs that fall from the table of their masters (Matthew 15:27). Give me, Lord, the pledge of the inheritance to come, at least one drop of the heavenly rain, with which I may cool my thirst; for I burn with love.

### Chapter V

*The office of contemplation*

6. With these and similar burning words the soul kindles its desire; so it shows its affection. With these incantations it calls the Bridegroom. And the Lord, whose eyes are upon the just and whose ears are open not only to their prayers (Psalm 33:16), does not wait for their prayers to end until they have finished speaking, but breaking into the middle of the prayer's course he hastens in, and runs quickly to meet the longing soul, drenched in the dew of heavenly sweetness, anointed with the best ointments: he restores the weary soul, feeds the hungry one, fattens the dry one; he makes it forget earthly things, wonderfully strengthening it by the memory of himself, giving it life, inebriating it, and making it sober again. And as in certain carnal offices the soul is so overcome by carnal desire that it loses all use of reason and the man becomes, as it were, wholly carnal, so rightly in this heavenly contemplation the movements of the flesh are so consumed and swallowed up by the soul that in nothing does the flesh contradict the spirit, and the man becomes, as it were, wholly spiritual.

### Chapter VI

*The signs of the Holy Spirit's coming to the soul*

7. But, O Lord, how shall we know when you do these things, and what is the sign of your coming? Are sighs and tears the witnesses and messengers of this consolation and joy? If so, this is a new figure of speech, and an unaccustomed meaning. For what agreement has consolation with sighs, or joy with tears? If indeed these are to be called tears at all, and not rather the overflowing abundance of the inward dew poured in from above, and the outer man's cleansing as the sign of an inner washing: so that, as in the Baptism of infants the outward washing signifies and figures the inward washing of the soul, so here, the other way about, an inward cleansing goes before the outward washing. O happy tears, by which inward stains are purged, by which the fires of sins are put out! Blessed are you who mourn in this way, for you shall laugh (Matthew 5:5). In these tears, O soul, recognise your Bridegroom; embrace the one you longed for. Now drink yourself full at the torrent of pleasure; suck milk and honey from the breast of his consolation. These are the wonderful little gifts and the comforts your Bridegroom has given you: groans and tears. He brings you drink in these tears, in measure. These tears are bread to you day and night; bread indeed that strengthens the heart of man, and sweeter than honey and the honeycomb. O Lord Jesus, if these tears which the memory and desire of you stir up are so sweet, how sweet will be the joy that will be taken from the open vision of you! If it is so sweet to weep for you, how sweet it will be to rejoice in you! But why do we bring such secret conversations into the open? Why do we try to express with common words affections that cannot be uttered or told? Those who have not experienced such things do not understand them, unless they read them more plainly in the book of experience, where the anointing itself teaches them. Otherwise the outer letter profits the reader nothing. There is little savour in the reading of the outer letter, unless it takes its gloss and its inner sense from the heart.

### Chapter VII

*On the hiding of grace*

8. O soul, we have drawn out our talk a long while. For it was good for us to be here, contemplating with Peter and John the glory of the Bridegroom, and to remain long with him, if he willed that there be made here not two, not three tabernacles (Matthew 17:1-4), but one, in which we might be together and together take delight. But now the Bridegroom says: Let me go, for the dawn is already rising; you have already received the light of grace and the visitation you desired. So, having given his blessing, having deadened the sinew of the thigh and changed the name Jacob into Israel (Genesis 32:25-31), the Bridegroom, long desired, quickly slipping away, withdraws for a little. He withdraws himself both from the vision spoken of and from the sweetness of contemplation; yet he remains present as far as governing goes.

### Chapter VIII

*That the hiding of grace for a time works together for our good*

9. But do not be afraid, O bride; do not despair, do not think yourself despised, if the Bridegroom withdraws his face from you for a little while. All these things work together for your good, and you gain both from his coming and from his going. He comes for you, and for you he also withdraws. He comes for consolation; he withdraws as a precaution, lest the greatness of the consolation puff you up; lest, if the Bridegroom were always with you, you should begin to despise your companions and attribute this continual visitation no longer to grace but to nature. But this grace the Bridegroom gives to whom he will and when he will; it is not possessed as if by right of inheritance. It is a common proverb that too much familiarity breeds contempt. So he withdraws, lest perhaps by being too constant he be despised, and so that being absent he may be more desired, being desired he may be sought more eagerly, and being long sought he may at last be found with more thanks. Besides, if this consolation, which compared with the glory to come that will be revealed in us is dark and partial, were never lacking here, we might perhaps think we had here a lasting city and seek the one to come the less. So let us not take exile for our homeland, nor the pledge for the full price. The Bridegroom comes, and in turn withdraws; now bringing consolation, now turning our whole bed into weakness. For a little while he lets us taste how sweet he is, and before we feel it fully he withdraws himself: and so, hovering over us as if with wings spread, he provokes us to fly. As if he said: See, you have tasted a little how sweet and pleasant I am; but if you want to be filled full of this sweetness, run after me in the fragrance of my ointments, with your hearts lifted up, where I am at the right hand of God the Father. There you shall see me, not through a glass in a dark manner, but face to face; and your heart shall rejoice fully, and your joy no one shall take from you.

### Chapter IX

*How carefully the soul must bear itself after the visitation of grace*

10. But take care, O bride: when the Bridegroom absents himself, he will not go far; and though you do not see him, he always sees you, full of eyes before and behind. You can never be hidden from him. He also has his messengers about you, spirits like the most sagacious of scouts, to see how you behave while the Bridegroom is away, and to accuse you before him if they catch in you any signs of wantonness or buffoonery. This Bridegroom is jealous. If perhaps you receive another lover, if you strive to please others more, he will at once leave you and cling to other maidens. This Bridegroom is delicate; he is noble and rich, beautiful in form above the sons of men; and so he deigns to have none but a beautiful bride. If he sees a stain or a wrinkle in you, he at once turns his eyes away. He can bear no uncleanness. Be chaste, then; be modest and humble, so that you may deserve to be visited often by your Bridegroom. I fear this talk has held us too long; but a subject at once fertile and sweet drove me to it, and I did not draw it out of my own will, but was drawn on unwilling by some sweetness of it, I know not what.

### Chapter X

*A recapitulation of what has been said*

11. So that what has been said at some length may be better seen joined together, let us gather up the sum of it in a recapitulation. As was noted in the examples above, it can be seen how these rungs hold together, and how each goes before the next both in time and as a cause. Reading comes first, as it were the foundation, and, giving the matter, sends us on to meditation. Meditation inquires more carefully what is to be desired, and, digging as it were for treasure, finds it and shows it. But since it cannot obtain it by itself, it sends us on to prayer. Prayer, lifting itself with all its strength to the Lord, obtains the desirable treasure, the sweetness of contemplation. And this, when it comes, rewards the labour of the three before it, when it inebriates the thirsting soul with the dew of heavenly sweetness. Reading, then, is in the outward exercise; meditation in the inward understanding; prayer in desire; contemplation above every sense. The first rung belongs to beginners, the second to those making progress, the third to the devout, the fourth to the blessed.

### Chapter XI

*Neither reading without meditation nor meditation without prayer profits*

12. Now these rungs are so linked together, and serve one another with mutual help, that the earlier without the later profit little or nothing, while the later without the earlier can rarely or never be had. For what does it profit to spend time in continual reading, to run through the deeds and writings of the saints in reading, unless we also draw out their juice by chewing and ruminating on them, and by swallowing send them down to the depths of the heart, so that from them we may consider our own state carefully and set ourselves to do the works of those whose deeds we love to read? But how shall we think on these things, or how shall we be able to keep from passing the limits set by the holy fathers, by meditating on things that are false or empty, unless we have first been instructed about such matters by reading or by hearing? For hearing in a certain way belongs to reading. So we are accustomed to say that we have read not only the books we have read to ourselves or to others, but also those we have heard from our masters. Again, what does it profit a man to see by meditation what is to be done, unless by the help of prayer and the grace of God he grows strong to obtain it? For every best gift and every perfect gift is from above, coming down from the Father of lights (James 1:17), without whom we can do nothing; and he does the works in us, yet not altogether without us. *For we are God's coadjutors*, as the Apostle says (1 Corinthians 3:9). God wants us to help him, and to open to him, when he comes and stands waiting at the door, the bosom of our will, and to consent to him. This consent he demanded of the Samaritan woman, when he said: *Call thy husband.* As if he said: I want to pour grace into you; you, apply your free will. He demanded prayer of her, when he said: *If thou didst know the gift of God, and who he is that saith to thee, Give me to drink; thou perhaps wouldst have asked of him, and he would have given thee living water.* Hearing this, the woman, instructed as if by reading, meditated in her heart that it would be good and useful for her to have this water. Kindled, then, with the desire of having it, she turned to prayer, saying: *Sir, give me this water, that I may not thirst, nor come hither to draw* (John 4:16, 10, 15). See how the hearing of the Lord's word, and the meditation that followed on it, stirred her to pray. For how would she have been concerned to ask, unless meditation had first kindled her? Or what would the meditation that went before have brought her, unless the prayer that followed had obtained what it showed was to be desired? For meditation to be fruitful, then, the devotion of prayer must follow it, and the sweetness of contemplation is, as it were, the effect of that.

### Chapter XII

*How the rungs already named are linked one to another*

13. From this we can gather that reading without meditation is dry; meditation without reading, erroneous; prayer without meditation is lukewarm; meditation without prayer, unfruitful; prayer with devotion obtains contemplation; the attaining of contemplation without prayer is either rare or a miracle. God, whose power has no number or limit, and whose mercy is over all his works, sometimes raises up children of Abraham from stones, when he compels the hard and unwilling to consent and to will; and so, like a spendthrift, as the common saying goes, he drags the ox by the horn, when unbidden he pours himself in. And although we read that this has sometimes happened to some, as to Paul and certain others, we ought not for that reason to presume on divine things as if tempting God, but to do what belongs to us: to read, that is, and to meditate on the law of God; to pray to him that he help our weakness and look on our imperfection; which he himself teaches us to do, saying, *Ask, and you shall receive; seek, and you shall find; knock, and it shall be opened to you* (Matthew 7:7). For now the kingdom of heaven suffers violence, and the violent bear it away (Matthew 11:12). See, from the distinctions marked out above, the properties of these rungs can be discerned, how they hold together, and what each one does in us.

14. Blessed is the man whose mind, empty of other business, desires always to move among these four rungs; who, having sold all that he had, buys that field in which the desirable treasure lies hidden, that is, to be at leisure and to see how sweet the Lord is; who, exercised on the first rung, circumspect on the second, devout on the third, lifted above himself on the fourth, by these ascents which he has disposed in his heart goes up from strength to strength, until he sees the God of gods in Sion. Blessed is he to whom it is granted to remain on this highest rung even for a little time, who can truly say: See, I feel the grace of the Lord; see, with Peter and John I contemplate his glory on the mountain; see, with Jacob I delight often in the embraces of Rachel. But let such a one take care lest after this contemplation, in which he was lifted up to the heavens, he fall by a disordered fall down to the depths, and lest after the vision of God he turn back to the wanton acts of the world and the enticements of the flesh. When the weak eye of the human mind can no longer bear the illumination of the true light, let it come down lightly and in order to one of the three rungs by which it climbed; and let it stay now on one, now on another, in turn, according to the movement of free will and the demands of place and time, nearer to God by so much as it is further from the first rung. But alas, how frail and wretched is the human condition! See, under the guidance of reason and by the testimonies of the Scriptures we plainly see that the perfection of the good life is contained in these four rungs, and that the exercise of the spiritual man ought to move among them. But who is there who holds to this path of living? Who is he, and we will praise him? To will is near to many, but to perform, to few. And would that we were among those few.

### Chapter XIII

*Four causes that draw us back from these four rungs*

15. There are four causes that for the most part draw us back from these rungs: unavoidable necessity, the usefulness of some honest action, human weakness, and the vanity of the world. The first is excusable; the second, tolerable; the third, pitiable; the fourth, blameworthy. For those whom this last cause draws back from their holy purpose, it would have been better not to have known the glory of God than to turn back after knowing it. What excuse indeed will such a one have for his sin? Cannot the Lord justly say to him, *What is there that I ought to do more for thee, that I have not done?* (Isaiah 5:4). You were not, and I created you; you sinned and had made yourself the devil's slave, and I redeemed you; you were running with the wicked in the circuit of the world, and I chose you. I had given you grace in my sight, and I wanted to make my dwelling with you; but you despised me, and cast behind you not only my words but myself, and walked after your own desires. But, O good God, gentle and mild, sweet friend, prudent counsellor, strong helper, how inhuman, how rash is he who casts you off, who drives so humble, so meek a guest from his heart! O what an unhappy and ruinous exchange, to cast off one's Creator and take in wicked and harmful thoughts! To hand over that secret chamber of the Holy Spirit, the secret place of the heart, which a little before was intent on heavenly joys, so suddenly to be trampled by unclean thoughts and sins! The Bridegroom's footprints are still warm in the heart, and already adulterous desires are let in? It is ill-fitting and unseemly that ears which have just heard words that it is not lawful for a man to speak should so quickly be bent to hearing tales and detractions; that eyes just baptised in holy tears should suddenly be turned to look on vanities; that a tongue which has just sung the sweet wedding song, which with its burning and persuasive words had reconciled the bride to the Bridegroom and led her into the wine cellar, should be turned again to empty talk, to buffoonery, to the framing of deceits, to detractions. Far be it from us, Lord! But if perhaps through human weakness we slip into such things, let us not despair for that, but run again to the merciful Physician, who raises up the needy from the earth and lifts the poor out of the dunghill (Psalm 112:7), and who does not want the death of the sinner: he will again cure and heal us.

16. Now it is time to put an end to this letter. Let us pray to God, then, that the hindrances which draw us back from contemplating him he may soften for us in the present and take away from us altogether in the future; and that by the rungs described he may lead us from strength to strength, until we see the God of gods in Sion, where the elect will receive the sweetness of divine contemplation not drop by drop, not at intervals, but, filled without ceasing from the torrent of pleasure, will have the joy that no one shall take from them, and peace unchangeable, peace in the selfsame. You, then, my brother Gervase, if ever it is given you from above to climb to the height of these rungs, remember me, and pray for me when it goes well with you, so that curtain may draw curtain, and he who hears may say: Come.
