# On Contemplation, Book Three

*De Contemplatione Dei per Sapientiam Unitivam Theologiamve Mysticam*

*On the Contemplation of God through Unitive Wisdom and Mystical Theology*

## Proemium

*Behold, God is great, exceeding our knowledge; the number of his years is inestimable. Therefore men shall fear him, and all the wise of heart shall not dare to behold him.* — Job 36:26; 37:23-24

## Article I. That in this Third Book we are to treat of the Contemplation of God through Negation, as pertaining to Unitive Wisdom and Mystical Theology

Denis recalls that in the first part of this work he showed how the divine Dionysius treats the triple theology — symbolic, proper, and mystical — distributing their determination into three books (cf. Book I, art. 26). He now briefly notes the distinction of these theologies.

According to the teaching of the philosophers, each thing has as much intelligibility and knowability as it has being, actuality, and perfection. Since the most blessed God is pure subsistent Being itself, full, perfect, and unlimited, and is in some manner uncircumscribed pure act, in whom all nobility and perfection is contained superessentially, most happily, simply, and — it must be said — ineffably and wholly incomprehensibly, it is clear that God is of altogether infinite intelligibility, fully knowable to himself alone, surpassing the capacity of our understanding infinitely and beyond all comparison, insofar as his essence exceeds our nature. With the reverence of filial fear, therefore, one must open the eye of the mind toward his contemplation; and no one should presume to behold him as though capable of comprehending or penetrating him.

The contemplation of God treated in Books One and Two pertains chiefly to the theology called *proper* — that which treats of God by affirmative and intelligible and perfective predicates: that God is the highest goodness, the first being, most actual life, simplest intellect, infinite power, omnipotent governor, most wise ordainer, and the like. These attributes are (speaking absolutely) more fittingly held to be present in God than absent, and the great Architect of theology, Dionysius, treats them in the books on the Divine Names. These pertain to the second species of contemplation as assigned in Book One.

The contemplation of God now to be treated concerns the theology that Dionysius described in the *Mystical Theology* — how he who is incomprehensibly known surpasses all, neither sharing genus nor species with created things. Although in the first book God's contemplation was divided into two species — supernatural contemplation of the highest Trinity, and natural contemplation of the simple Deity — this division has no place here: in the contemplation by which we behold God through negation, whether as regards what pertains to the unity of essence or to the trinity of Persons, there does not seem to be a specific diversity. Both species of the contemplation of God already mentioned can be divided into contemplation by affirmation and contemplation by negation. The contemplation by negation pertains to the theology of the *Mystica Theologia* of Dionysius. For although in the contemplation of God by negation the mind does not rest in mere or pure negation — as God himself will make clear — the principal and formal element in this kind of contemplation is negation or removal, in the sense to be touched on below.

## Article II. What Unitive Wisdom and Mystical Theology Are

This contemplation of God is held to be so supernatural that it is utterly hidden from the philosophers of this world, nor is it known by experience or interior taste by the faithful themselves, save by the heroic and perfect, most ardently burning with divine love, whose soul has been purified — unless perhaps the almighty God, by a most merciful dispensation, reveals it by taste to some who are advancing or even beginning, through a singular grace. For even the Apostle Paul, not yet long purified, God showed himself in a higher manner than is possible for this life through ordinary contemplation.

Dionysius defines unitive wisdom — which has another name, mystical theology — in chapter seven of the *Divine Names*: "Wisdom is the most divine knowledge of God, known through unknowing, according to the union which is above the mind, while the mind withdraws from all things, and even forsakes itself, united to the superbrilliant rays, illumined by the unfathomable depth of wisdom."

The interpretation of this definition: "Wisdom" — this unitive wisdom, taken rather for the act than for the habitual sapiential state — "is the most divine knowledge of God," that is, most supernatural, deifying, and most worthy, of those knowledges of God possible in the present life; "known through unknowing" — that is, by which God is known through unknowing, namely through the negation or removal of all things from him. By this contemplation the mind is joined to God as to the incomprehensible and unknown, as to an infinite sea and a darkness utterly unsearchable and hidden to the mind, though in his own nature he is the most superbrilliant light. Further, this knowledge is held "according to the union which is above the mind" — that is, through a supramented and superintellectual union, because it transcends the natural power of the mind, the inquiry of reason, and proper natural capacity; and because it is not consummated in the intellective power, nor is it principally obtained through the clarity of intelligence, but is poured in by the burning fervor of holy and deified charity, and is consummated and terminated in the ardor of divine love, so that in it fervent love wholly dominates and is, as it were, alone sensed — because even the knowledge itself is said to pass over into love and to be in a certain manner transformed into it.

"While the mind withdraws from all things" — that is, ceasing and desisting from the operation, consideration, love, and occupation of sensible and intelligible things — "and even forsakes itself," because it also rests from attention to itself and all its own operations, and turns around nothing created — "it is united to the superbrilliant rays," that is, to the supernatural and most beautiful radiances, theoriae, droplets, and flowings of the Holy Spirit, "illumined by the unfathomable depth of wisdom" — taught by the incomprehensible, supremely high, most pure source of all wisdom.

To this most radiant contemplation, no one ascends unless he be actually touched and aroused by the Holy Spirit; and for this excitation he is made ready by the gift of wisdom in its perfect state, and by ardent, sincere, and overflowing love.

Hence some, defining this wisdom or mystical theology, say it is an anagogical or upward-leading movement of the mind to God through pure and fervent love; or again, that it is an experiential knowledge of God through the embrace of unitive love; or yet again, that it is a sapiential knowledge of God, when the supreme apex of the affection is united to him through love.

## Article III. On the Differences between Speculative and Mystical Theology

Some who distinguish speculative or scholastic theology from mystical assign the following differences.

The first difference: the subject of speculative theology is the intellect; the subject of mystical theology is the affective power.

The second difference: the object of speculative theology is the true, or God under the aspect of the true; the object of mystical theology is the good.

The third difference: speculative theology uses ratiocination, as do the physical disciplines — for which it is also called scholastic and literary; but mystical theology is learned and acquired through the penitent affection and the exercise of the moral virtues that are dispositive to the purity of mind. Hence speculative theology is obtained in the school of the intellect, mystical theology in the school of affection or religion.

The fourth difference: mystical theology, although it is the highest and most worthy, can nevertheless be possessed by the faithful, however unlettered, simple, and dull, without subtle genius and without great, especially acquired, knowledge — when, namely, they burn with love of God and devotion, and with a purified heart know God to be good, sweet, loveable above all, and recognize his benefits. Speculative theology is otherwise.

The fifth: no one rightly understands the mystical theology of the Saints unless he has absorbed their spirit.

The sixth: as the blessed Dionysius often teaches and admonishes his disciple Timothy (in the *Celestial Hierarchy*, *Ecclesiastical Hierarchy*, and elsewhere), the most mystical and most divine wisdom of God is not to be set before the unlearned — that is, the carnal, the puffed up, the perverse, those weighed down in sensible things — but before the devout, the meek, the teachable. Hence the Savior says: "Give not that which is holy to dogs" (Matt. 7:6). Speculative theology is not thus prohibited from being proposed to whomsoever.

The seventh difference: mystical theology does not puff up, does not serve vices, and is not acquired for a perverse end — as sometimes happens with speculative theology when abused, since "knowledge puffeth up" (1 Cor. 8:1) — not that it is in itself evil, but it accidentally causes swelling because it pertains to a certain excellence. The eighth: through mystical theology we are strengthened in God; speculative theology, if alone and without grace, disturbs the soul rather than quieting it.

Finally, as we see in ourselves and in animals, sensitive apprehension is sometimes without affection toward the sensible object, and sometimes from such apprehension so great an impulse of sensual affection is generated that the sensuality almost leaps out of itself and pours toward the thing apprehended and desired. Correspondingly in the interior life: the intellective power is sometimes little or not at all affected toward the good it perceives, while at other times it is vehemently kindled toward it and wholly transferred there by an impatient affection. And as water placed in a vessel over fire, when not yet heated, remains contained, but when heat is applied, soon begins to rise and overflow; so the mind, not yet enkindled by love, contains itself within; but once aflame with the fire of holy love, goes beyond itself, burning and tending toward God whom it loves.

## Article IV. On the Fourfold Way of Ascending to the Contemplation of God from Creatures, and the Double Contemplation

As gathered from various places in the books of the blessed Dionysius, we ascend from creatures to the knowledge of the Creator by a fourfold way.

The first is the way of position or affirmation, when we assert that whatever perfection consists in creatures belongs, first and above all, to the creative principle. Hence in the first chapter of the *Mystical Theology* Dionysius says: "It is necessary to posit and to say in him who is the cause of all, the positions or positive perfections of all beings." Praising and contemplating God in this way, one should begin from the most excellent and common attributes, then in order descend to what is lower — as Dionysius teaches in the second chapter of the *Mystical Theology* and acts upon in chapters four and five of the same.

The second is the way of removal or negation, when all that is found in creatures is denied of God, who superexcels all and shares neither genus nor species with created things, but excels all incomparably, so that nothing of what we know belongs to him in the way we are able to comprehend it. Praising God in this way, one should begin from the lower, denying of God first the viler, sensible, and earthly things, then the higher and intelligible, as Dionysius teaches in the second chapter of the *Mystical Theology* and treats in chapters four and five.

The third is the way of causality, when by weighing the nature and properties of divine effects we raise ourselves to the contemplation of the nature and property of the first Maker. This way reduces to the first.

The fourth is the way of eminence, when we ascribe to God the nobilities and perfections of creatures, not in the manner in which they belong to creatures, but in a more sublime and excellent manner. For created things fall infinitely short of full assimilation to the Creator; therefore whatever imperfection adheres to created things must be removed when they are attributed to God, while what is perfect in them must be ascribed to God in a mode infinitely more exalted than we can comprehend. This way reduces to the second.

Thus all contemplation of God is either by affirmation — treated in Book One — or by negation — now the matter of this third book.

## Article V. That Contemplation of God through Negation is more Perfect, and stands midway between the Beatific Vision and Affirmative Contemplation

It has been shown that God exceeds the capacity of every created mind absolutely and infinitely, so that he and created things share nothing univocally — neither genus nor species. Therefore we cannot grasp what God is by any species in this life; nor can any created intellect by natural power know the divine essence in itself and as it is, through any species — since it is altogether certain that every created form and species falls infinitely short of full representation of the divine essence.

Hence, according to the most holy Dionysius and doctors commonly, the contemplation of God by negation is more perfect than by affirmation, so that it is more apt and profound to say: God is not goodness, God is not essence, God is not life, wisdom, intellect, etc. — than: God is goodness, essence, life. When I say "God is goodness, essence, life," I seem to designate what God is, and that he has something common and similar with created things — whom it is certain to be incomprehensible, unknown, inscrutable, and ineffable, separated from every effect by immense and wholly incomparable distance and excellence. But when I say "God is not goodness, etc.," I indicate that he is in no way goodness, essence, life, mind, or anything in the manner in which created things are, and in the way we are able to estimate and comprehend, but I suggest that these belong to him with supersubstantial excess and incomprehensible sublimity. Hence Dionysius in the second chapter of the *Celestial Hierarchy*: "Negations in divine things are true," that is, properly and truly said; "but affirmations are non-compact" — that is, unfitting, incoherent, and improper.

Moreover, this contemplation of mystical theory is the most certain and the clearest. For it is wrought through the most pure and most fervent ardor of divine love, which enters and penetrates where knowledge remains outside; and having entered deeply and most acutely, it itself also introduces knowledge, and most closely and intimately (as far as is possible in this life) connects the mind to God. The closer the mind is fixed, impressed, and united to that divine and infinite light, the more fully it is illumined, the more certainly it gazes, the more limpidly it contemplates, and is almost wholly dissolved into the fire of love. And when it beholds that the uncreated light is wholly and infinitely incomprehensible, and that it falls infinitely short of the clear vision of that light and its full comprehension, this is precisely to see and contemplate it — to see that light is altogether incomprehensible; and the more clearly it sees this, the more splendid and perfect is its contemplation. Hence this contemplation is said to stand midway between the beatific vision of the homeland and the ordinary speculation of God by affirmation — though the contemplation of the homeland far outshines it, as mystical contemplation surpasses ordinary affirmative contemplation. According to Dionysius, the state of the evangelical law is placed midway between the Old Testament and the homeland — higher than the former, lower than the latter.

## Article VI. Whether the Contemplation of Unitive Mystic Wisdom is Lasting or Momentary

This question about the duration and brevity of contemplation draws its origin from various testimonies of the Saints. Sometimes they affirm it lasts only a moment, at other times that it endures long.

In Bernard's words cited above it was shown that the Spouse's presence in this contemplation is brief, passing as in the act of something piercing. Gregory testifies the same repeatedly in the *Moralia*, saying among other things that first the cloud of vices is cleared away by the burning, penitential grief of the mind's edge, and then the mind is illumined by the rapidly flashing coruscation of uncircumscribed light. And again: when the mind is raised up to contemplate, it soon sinks back under the weight of the flesh. In general, the higher a perfection is and the more it transcends natural powers, the more difficult and brief its continuation seems to be. The contemplative gaze of mystical theology is the highest perfection of the present life.

On the other hand, certain saints are read to have actually persisted in highest contemplation for long periods: sure signs of which were the rigidity and stupor of the body, the wonderful abstraction from the senses, the insensibility of the lower nature, and the miraculous and prolonged elevation and suspension of the whole person in the air — which will be shown more particularly below.

The answer is: this entire dark, mystical, and most luminous contemplation depends on a supernatural cause — the Holy Spirit — as regards its beginning, continuation, and consummation. Although the Holy Spirit is the principal cause, the proximate, formal, and intrinsic causes are the actual and most fervent love of God, the abstraction of the mind from all operation concerning created things, the gift of wisdom, and the mind's endeavor to extend and fix itself in God. The more persevering, stronger, more supernaturally assisted, and uninterrupted these causes are, the longer will the contemplation and resulting alienation or ecstasy be. The whole depends principally on the omnipotent piety and most merciful liberality of the Holy Spirit. Indeed, for as long as it pleases him to preserve the mind in so supernatural an elevation, so long will it remain suspended. For the more supernatural this contemplation is, the stronger, more continuous, and more abundant the divine assistance it requires.

Ruysbroeck, in the book *On the Sacrament of the Altar*, writes of this: when we have been transformed in the divine clarity — that is, in the eternal and uncreated light — we have forgotten ourselves and are one with it; and thus we live in it, and it in us; we ever remain, however, divided in substance. The clarity of God that we see in us has no beginning, end, time, place, way, path, form, species, or color. It has totally surrounded us, comprehended, penetrated us, and opened our simple and most luminous eye perpetually, and we cannot or are able to close it. Denis suggests this is because that most excellent contemplation causes in the mind a most vehement desire to persist in it, or to return to it incessantly, so that we can scarcely withdraw the mind from its gaze or affection. Hence Wisdom says: "They that eat me, shall yet hunger; and they that drink me, shall yet thirst" (Eccl. 24:29).

When the Saints assert that the aforesaid contemplation is very brief, they speak with respect to the weakness of the human mind, to what happens more frequently and regularly, or to the duration and eminence of beatific contemplation in the homeland, compared to which this is considered quite small, obscure, and imperfect — however continuous, sublime, and splendid it may be in itself and by comparison with the state of the present life.

## Article VII. How Certain Eminent Philosophers Approached Mystical Theology

The glorious standard-bearers of Christian wisdom — doctors and contemplatives — glorify God with grateful and humble mind, knowing that singular and most exalted wisdom, namely mystical theology, has come to them from above and supernaturally.

It is said that mystic theology consists preeminently in the contemplation of God by negation, and is wrought through ardent love and a most swift coruscation of the mind to and from God. Since therefore the better philosophers — both Platonists and Peripatetics — testify that God is more truly and subtly known through the negation of all things from him than through affirmation, that he is to be loved above all things, that the human intellect is sometimes drawn near to him in a deiform manner, and is illumined by the most rapid, utterly brief, coruscating radiance of pure contemplation — they too seem to have known, tasted, and grasped mystical theology.

Alpharabius says that more is known of God concerning what he is not than what he is, indeed that what he is, is altogether unknown. Avicenna and Algazel likewise say that the first being is beyond every genus, and shares nothing univocally with things; that all things are better denied of him than any being affirmed of him.

Proclus the Platonist in his *Theological Elements* says most subtly that the divine being is ineffable and unknowable to all secondary beings together: hence the first being is wholly unknown, as an unmixed *amethekton* (unparticipated) being. Again: "The divine being is not opiniable, nor meditatable, nor intelligible; there is no opinion, no science, no intelligence of it; but from what depends on it, its properties are inferred." The author of the *Book of Causes* similarly testifies in the fifth proposition: the first cause is above all narration, since it is above every cause, and is only narrated through secondary causes illumined by the light of the first cause; wherefore it falls under no sense, meditation, thought, intelligence, or speech. And again: "The first cause is above every name by which it is named, since to it belongs neither diminution nor complement alone."

Moreover, according to Augustine in the ninth book of *The City of God*, Plato is praised by Apuleius the Platonist in these words: God alone, who cannot even moderately be comprehended by the poverty of human speech, is scarcely contemplated by wise men when they have withdrawn from the body as much as possible by the vigor of the mind; and even that wisdom sometimes, like the most rapid flash of lightning in deepest darkness, intermingles with its light.

And Aristotle says most acutely that our life at times is such as the life of the first principle always is — namely, contemplative and deiform.

Some brilliant philosophers, adorned with natural virtues, who wholly extended themselves to philosophical considerations and were fitted through abstraction from sensibles for eminent speculation of metaphysical essences, were sometimes led to a certain excellent and sweet contemplation of the highest God, as far as could be done through acquired sciences, natural light, natural love of God, and naturally acquirable virtues. But this their contemplation and wisdom falls as far short of the mystical theology of Christians as cloud from light and shadow from truth; as reason, natural wisdom, and natural love fall short and fail beside faith and supernatural wisdom (which is a gift) and gratuitous and infused charity of God. Nor did the wisdom of the philosophers have such savors, delights, ardors, excesses, raptures, ecstasies, and prolongations as Christian mystical theology, through which the apex of the intelligence contemplates God with a clarity and the summit of the affective embraces him with a fervor that natural wisdom and love cannot effect.

## Article VIII. On the Abstraction and Simplification of Mind Necessary for this Contemplation; and What the Divine Darkness Is

The more excellently, purely, and simply this most excellent contemplation of mystical wisdom occupies itself supernaturally around the superessential and supremely sublime God, the more necessary it is for intellect and affection and the whole spirit to withdraw from all created things — lest the object of that contemplation be anything created, nor any inordinate creaturely affection retard the mind from the superloving of God and from the clean, preeminent fervor of his holy love — so that the whole gaze of the mind and the whole appetite of the volitional power, without anything drawing them back or hindering, may integrally and sincerely be carried, fixed, and established in God.

Hence Dionysius in the first chapter of the *Mystical Theology* says to Timothy: "Thou, about mystical visions, by vigorous friction" — that is, by that vigorous and fitting exercise by which you dispose and fit yourself for the sapiential contemplations of mystical theory — "and leave the senses and the intellectual operations, and all sensibles and intelligibles, and all non-existing and existing things (that is, operations, powers, objects, yourself, and all created and creatable things), and thus strive to be restored to unity with him who is above all substance and knowledge — that is, incomparably above every creature and all knowledge — incognitely, that is, through the non-consideration of all the aforesaid, or 'incognitely' through the removal of all things from God and the incomprehension of God himself."

The divine darkness is the inaccessibility and incomprehensibility of the divine light, or divine light as incomprehensible and unknown. For the holy Dionysius says in the letter to Dorotheus: "The darkness in which God is said to dwell is inaccessible light." And again: "God is invisible because of the exceeding clarity of his own light." Although divine light is in itself supremely radiant and supremely clear, yet by comparison with the wayfaring mind, which cannot behold it in itself because of its super-infinite splendor and beauty, it is called darkness — as Dionysius establishes in the letter to Caius: "The divine darkness covers itself over every light and is hidden from all knowledge by the glory of its own incomparable clarity." Sometimes, however, the darkness which the mind is said to enter means the actual unknowing of all things in the mind's transcendence above all things.

In the darkness taken in the first sense, God is held to dwell — who makes darkness his covering (cf. Ps. 17:12); to whom Isaiah speaks: "Verily thou art a hidden God" (Is. 45:15). In the darkness in the second sense God also dwells, inasmuch as this is the proximate disposition by which he inhabits and illumines the contemplating mind.

To enter the divine darkness, then, is to extend the eye of the mind and the apex of the affection toward God through this mystical theory — a going-out from all that the inner sermon can do, from the running of images, through the transcendence and negation of all, the mind resting from every act of its apprehensive and appetitive powers concerning created things, and entering the divine darkness itself — that is, extending and immersing itself in the uncircumscribed and inaccessible eternal light: which, the more perfectly it is gazed upon, the more clearly it is contemplated as incomprehensible, inaccessible, and boundless.

## Article IX. How the Interior Word is Progressively Abbreviated as the Soul Ascends, until it Reaches Total Deprivation of Speech

As has been shown, in mystical theology God is praised and beheld through the removal of all things from him; and the ordered way of beginning is from sensibles and lower things: for it is more apt to say God is not a body or a stone or an animal, than that he is not life, intellect, or essence. And in this ascent there is a proportionate abbreviation of all interior and exterior speech — for the more we ascend to what is higher and intelligible, denying those things of God (namely that he is not intellect or life), the more interior and exterior speech about God is shortened, until, reaching the summit and the first and most universal things — that God is not power, not substance, not essence — and entering the darkness declared in the preceding article, all speech will cease. Not brevity of speech, but the pure privation of speech, incomprehensibility, and unintelligibility will be found; and we shall be united to God as ineffable, incomprehensible, and unknown.

Indeed, as has also been previously shown, the closer, clearer, and more intimately the mind is fixed and united to the abyssal, uncreated, and uncircumscribed light, the more limpidly it perceives how ineffable, invisible, and unknown that light is.

This is what Dionysius teaches in the third chapter of the *Mystical Theology*. In the fourth chapter he explains how all sensibles are denied of God: "Let us say that he is neither body, nor figure, nor species; he is not in a place, and is neither seen nor sensed; he does not need, and is not subject to passion or disturbance," etc. And in the fifth and final chapter he shows how even the more worthy and intelligible things are denied of him: "Ascending further, let us say: he is neither soul, nor intellect; he has neither reason nor intelligence; he is neither spoken of nor understood; he is neither magnitude nor smallness; he is neither power, nor does he have power; he is neither darkness nor light; he neither lives nor is life; he is neither substance, nor truth, nor wisdom; he is neither one, nor unity, nor deity, nor goodness; he is neither spirit, nor filiation, nor paternity; nor is he anything of things existing or not existing; nor do existing things know him according to what he himself is."

## Article X. Whether Mystical Theology is Really the Same as the Gift of Wisdom; and How this Mystic Contemplation is given more to the Simple than to the Learned

The concordant opinion of the doctors is that among all infused supernatural habits of the soul, the theological virtues take precedence; after which the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit hold the next place in dignity; and among these, no one doubts that the preeminent gift is wisdom.

If mystical theology is taken for the habitual intellectual perfection — namely, as unitive wisdom — it will be really the same as the gift of wisdom. For it is not a theological virtue. And if it were the same as the gift of wisdom in some other sense, there would be some wisdom above the gift that is mystical theology — which does not accord with the words of the holy Dionysius, who declares mystical theology to be the highest. Furthermore, the gift of wisdom is sapiential knowledge, judging rightly of divine things with an interior taste, whose nature it is to be formed divine knowledge; hence it is inseparably annexed to charity. But all this clearly belongs also to mystical theology.

If someone objects: if mystical theology is identified with the gift of wisdom, then whoever is in the state of salvation has mystical theology — which is held to belong only to the perfect — the answer is: this does not follow. For mystical theology is the gift of wisdom according to its highest grade. There are three grades of this wisdom. Therefore whoever possesses the gift of wisdom according to its perfect grade, mystical theology also shines in him. If mystical theology is taken for the act, it will be the operation of the gift of unitive wisdom possessed in its supreme grade.

From all this it is clear that for the attainment of mystical theology, what is required is not subtle genius, scholastic exercise, or bare speculative science, but a penitent affection, a pure heart, fervent love, simplification, elevation, and recollection of the mind in God, and the illumination of the Holy Spirit. Since all these are frequently more abundant in the unlettered and simple — even in women and gentle, humble, and purified girls, especially those who in all things cast away their own will — it is clear that this most worthy contemplation of mystic wisdom is many times poured more copiously into them than into the ingenious and learned who have not rightly and worthily fitted themselves through the purgative and illuminative way for this unitive way. Indeed, those who are great in their own eyes and count others as nothing are driven from this mystical theory as most unfit and most unworthy.

But the more someone grows in divine charity, the more he advances in the gift of wisdom. Since therefore the simple often grow much more in the fervor of charity through humility and patience than the ingenious and learned, it is clear that the simple shine more in mystical theology. Therefore, speaking absolutely, they know God more excellently and clearly than the latter — and so much more sublimely as supernatural, unitive, and mystical wisdom excels natural and scholastic wisdom, or even infused wisdom in its inferior grades.

Whoever desires to attain true, most profound, and most divine wisdom must first effectively sweat at the reformation of all passions, the eradication of vices, and the full contempt of worldly things; then continually ask to be illumined by God, and tirelessly strive to be inflamed in the most sincere love of the Divinity — and so endeavor to simplify and fix his heart in God.

## Article XI. Whether the Contemplative Anagogic Ascent toward God is More Laborious or Sweet

Some who write about contemplation and the burning of divine love place the whole perfection of the contemplative life in three things: fervor, sweetness, and song — by fervor meaning the intense affect of divine love; by sweetness, spiritual delight or sweet consolation in God; by song, a secret and interior jubilation, the quiet of the soul arising from fervor and the sweetness already described.

Others on the contrary affirm that, although through unitive and mystical wisdom the mind tends upward, yet in the actual exercise, as regards its anagogic movements, it consists without any soothing unction or delight; but rather, they say, there is there in a wondrous way a bodily affliction, and the only joy there is that in this actual upward tendency the mind rises directly and without any deflection toward God most blessed as toward the place corresponding to its dignity — as a heavy body tends toward its center. Hence, as they say, many are deceived who are less expert in this philosophy, thinking that in the anagogic movements the ascending mind is multiply bedewed with celestial sweetness, whereas on the contrary the mind is driven by the most laborious upward-action, and from the extension of the spirit there is a certain enervation of the body and a spiritual separation, and a stretching of the bodily members from the impetuosity of the anagogic movements, so that the body could not long bear the impulse of the anagogic movements without great affliction — were it not that the joy of the spirit from its direct tendency tempers this.

Denis observes that both positions could be supported by argument. For the first, every virtue has its proper joy annexed to its action, and the delight of a higher virtue is greater. Since therefore the aforesaid contemplative ascent is the act of the most excellent habit and virtue of the soul — namely unitive, theoretical, supernatural, deiform, and mystical wisdom — it appears that by its nature it is most sweet, anointing, and delightful. And again, an action the more fitting and perfective, the more delightful it is. And since joy is the diffusion of the soul in the concept of the good, and arises from the union of the power with the proportionally coupled object, and in the anagogic ascent there is the most noble union of the mind with the highest and most supremely fitting object, it seems that preeminent joy consists in it. Ruysbroeck testifies that the grace of God overflows into the lower powers of the soul and inherits the person's mind — whence springs a heartfelt and perceptible love and delight toward God, passing also through the flesh and blood and all corporeal nature, so that the person does not know how to conduct himself, being made like a drunkard. From this arise manifold and rare behaviors that some cannot restrain: raising the head skyward with impatient longing, now rejoicing, now weeping, now singing, now dancing, now sorrowful, now well, often both at once.

As for the resolution of this question — the exercise of mystical theology is wholly supernatural, and is the more easily and joyfully present, the more the mind is preceded, guided, and helped by the more abundant charismata of the Holy Spirit. Although the disposition to that most secret theoretic, intuitive, and most ardent union with God can be laborious and afflictive because of the mind's striving in the transcendence of all things, fervent prayer, proper recollection, and the alteration of the body; it is nonetheless at times very easy by the special gift and help of the Holy Spirit. Indeed, sometimes the mind without its own preparation is suddenly, as it were, struck, irradiated, kindled, rapt, and transformed by the Holy Spirit; and thus it enters the divine darkness with voluptuous ease. But it is certain that there are diverse grades and modes of mystic visions and theoretical contemplations — wherefore no general rule can be assigned for the solution of this question.

## Article XII. The Seven Steps by which One Arrives at the Tranquil Entry into the Divine Darkness

As the end of the mortification of the exterior person is that the members of the body be not offered as instruments of iniquity to sin, but fully subjected to the command of reason (cf. Rom. 6:13), so the end of the mortification of the interior person is that the mind adhere to God as inseparably and without distraction as possible.

In the interior person — the soul — there are three things to be mortified from the world so that they may live wholly for God: memory, from all occupation with and remembrance of passing things that hinder union with God; intellect, from all depicting and intuition of vain likenesses; and will, from all inordinate affection and base delight in perishable things. A person dies thus to this age when his mind, free from all exterior and interior tumults, as if buried in the bosom of divine love, sweetly reposes in the secret of the face of his God.

Toward this stability of most tranquil repose and most blessed slumber in God — which is nothing other than that most ardent and most secret contemplation of mystical wisdom, or the entry into the darkness and intimate union with the Most High — the devout soul strives and advances by six steps, and in the seventh, supported by divine help, arrives at the desired harbor of repose.

**The first step** is the laying aside of all earthly anxiety (on which much has already been said). For unless the mind becomes as if insensible to exterior things, it does not reach the taste of inner sweetness.

**The second step** is the perfect stripping of the mind from every created species. The surface of the mind that wishes to receive the Creator's impression and image must be stripped of every impression and image of any created thing. The Creator himself says in the Canticle: "Put me as a seal upon thy heart" (Cant. 8:6). Of this also much was said above.

**The third step** is the total recollection of the self within one's inmost self. For the division of the heart is the soul's death, as Osee testifies: "Their heart is divided: now they shall perish" (Osee 10:2). For when the mind is plucked from the supreme One and dispersed into many things, it is reduced to almost nothing by its own diminution. Since therefore our heart is divided from God by vain and varied loves and imaginations of creatures, it must be totally recollected in him again — lest it wish or think anything outside him. For this recollection three things especially avail: solitude, quiet, silence — as Jeremias testifies: "He shall sit solitary and hold his peace: because he hath taken it up upon himself" (Lam. 3:28).

**The fourth step** is the total transformation of the will into God: that in every event the person commit himself to the good pleasure of the divine will — in all adversity giving thanks, most faithfully grounded in God, certain that "God hath care of you" (1 Pet. 5:7), and that without his providence nothing happens to us; and that to those who love God and truly suffer, "all things work together unto good" (Rom. 8:28). Such a person rejoices within himself in great peace and delight, as God grants.

**The fifth step** is the full self-abandonment: that the person offer himself wholly to God as a holocaust, reserving nothing of himself to himself, but rendering back to God through worship all that he has received from God.

**The sixth step** is the complete self-transcendence — when the mind, visited by some strong grace, follows the drawing grace to such a degree that, forgetful of all things and of itself, it passes at once, as it were, by God's gift into another state of transformation. This transcendence is also called the alienation of the mind. For whenever the mind treating of something divine senses what transcends human powers, it senses this not in itself but in God who draws it.

**The seventh step** is the spiritual introduction into the divine darkness — in which God truly is, dwells, and is beheld — in which the highest peace possible in this life is given to the mind, its repose and perfection.

These seven steps, of which the first six are ordered to and prepare for the seventh, are the seven days in which Moses disposed himself to approach, contemplate, and speak with the Lord during the six preceding days, and on the seventh, the Lord calling him, he entered into the midst of the cloud in which God was, and remained with him (cf. Exod. 24:15-18).

## Article XIII. Why Unitive Wisdom is called Irrational, Senseless, and Foolish

The divine Dionysius in chapter seven of the *Divine Names* calls the unitive wisdom aforesaid irrational, senseless, and foolish — not that it truly and absolutely is such, but it is so called by excess, because it transcends natural reason, intellect, power, and wisdom, just as he also calls God neither one, nor good, nor wise (cf. *Mystical Theology*, ch. 5). For it has been frequently shown that mystic theory is wholly supernatural and infused, and is acquired and experienced through the most ardent love of pure charity; and it is fully known only to one who has come to it through taste and experience — a taste and experience not obtainable by natural power.

Again, this wisdom is called irrational and senseless, that is, supramented and superrational and suprasapiential: for in it the sincere fervor of divine love wholly predominates, by whose heat the mind is elevated beyond and above itself, and is led in the most desirable manner to the most eminent union possible in this life with the supremely sublime God.

Again, this wisdom is called irrational, senseless, and foolish because it appears irrational, senseless, and foolish to the carnal, the unlearned, the proud, and the vain lovers of the age — who indeed ridicule it and disbelieve in its existence.

Again, it is called senseless and foolish like the law of Christ: because it leads its disciples to such a life and requires of them such a life as, to the wise and prudent of the age who do not have the sense and spirit of Christ, appears wholly irrational, without sense, and foolish. Hence the Apostle says: "We are fools for Christ's sake" (1 Cor. 4:10). And: "God was pleased by the foolishness of preaching to save them that believe" (1 Cor. 1:21). And: "If any man among you seem to be wise in this world, let him become a fool, that he may be wise" (1 Cor. 3:18).

## Article XIV. Whether Mystical Theology is Essentially Love or Knowledge; and on its Subject and Object

Among those who distinguish speculative theology from mystical, some place the subject of speculative theology in the intellect, and mystical theology in the affective power; and hold the object of speculative theology to be the true, and of mystical theology the good — from which it seems to follow that mystical theology is formally or essentially love.

Denis argues simply and plainly: mystical theology is not formally or essentially love or fervor, but knowledge; and its subject is the intellect, not the affective power.

First, as has been frequently shown, this theology is really the same as unitive wisdom — which is in the intellect as truly as its act or habit, which truly is wisdom. Second, as shown from the preceding, according to the expositors of Dionysius, mystical theology is the most secret locution of the mind with God. That this locution is an act of the intellect, and not of the will, cannot be doubted. Third, Dionysius in the first chapter of the *Mystical Theology* says to Timothy: "About mystical visions, leave the senses." He thus calls the act of mystical theology a vision — and without doubt he speaks of mental vision, namely theological and hidden, enclosed and strict contemplation. Fourth, mystical theology has been shown to be really the same as the gift of wisdom, which the doctors unanimously place in the intellect. Fifth, Dionysius through the whole text of the *Mystical Theology* treats this theology as the most eminent knowledge of God through which all things are removed from him.

Contemplative life consists in the purgative way dispositively, in the illuminative way formally, in the unitive or perfective way completively — as beatitude is essentially in the intellect (since it is the knowledge of God, God himself saying: "This is eternal life, that they may know thee, the only true God" [John 17:3]), yet completively in the will.

However, mystical theology, as regards its complement and formative, and as regards that by which it is moved and excited to its exercise, is in the apex of the affective — as regards the fervor of love. Hence contemplation and the contemplative life consists dispositively in the purgative way, formally in the illuminative, completively in the unitive way. And the ardor of love that is concomitant and arises from the mystic theoretical contemplation pertains more to mystical theology than the ardor that precedes and prepares for it: for the concomitant ardor is hotter than the prior, because the contemplative inspection of this unitive wisdom, this clear and sacred intuition, the more limpid and intimate it is, the more it kindles, rapts, suspends, transforms, and deifies.

Regarding whether the subject of mystical theology is the good: it appears not. First, in the *On the Triple Way*, God is apprehended in this wisdom not under the aspect of the sweet, odorable, beautiful, or melodious, nor under any aspect that is the object of any interior sense. Second, the mind in the vision of mystical theology is joined to God not according to any affirmative, or under any reason of positive or comprehensible entity or property, but as to the ineffable and unknown — according to Dionysius through the whole text of the *Mystical Theology*.

## Article XV. Whether the Affective Apex can tend toward God without any Prior or Concomitant Knowledge of the Intellect

This question is proposed at the end of the book *On the Triple Way*, and the author of that book strives to prove by reasons and authorities that in perfect men, enkindled by the fervor of divine love, the affective apex is immediately carried by the force of love into God without prior knowledge — since as a heavy body is moved downward by its own weight, so the apex of the affection by its own weight or fervent love is immediately, without any prior or concomitant knowledge, carried into God, because the affective power in its highest part has an immediate respect to the Holy Spirit, who is love, and is immediately unifiable to him, and is thus directly without prior knowledge inflamed, touched, and drawn upward by him.

Denis replies: God is never loved or tasted unless he is first somehow known — "Who loves what he does not know?" (Gregory). "Wisdom says, no one loves what he does not know" (Gregory). And Augustine: "Unseen things we can love, but not unknown things." Denis concludes that mystical theology is in the apex of the intellect and essentially consists in vision, as appears clearly from Dionysius's words throughout the *Mystical Theology*.

The ardor of love that disposes the anagogic ascent of mystic wisdom precedes it in the affective apex; but that ardor is merely motive and dispositive toward the act of mystical wisdom, which the most ardent affection of holy charity accompanies — not in order of time but of nature. And what is more: that ardor of love that disposes one for the sapiential act is itself preceded by some knowledge, at least in the order of nature.

This knowledge, however, is not obtained by natural cognition, but by the most gracious illumination of the Holy Spirit — by the infusion of a deiform and intelligible and simple clarity splendidly illumining, anointing, and readying the mind supernaturally for the most prompt, swift, and most certain contemplation of God. In this irradiation and contemplation, neither error nor uncertainty can intermingle.

## Article XVI. On the Divine Deifying Love and its Fiery Fervor; and the Signs of Ecstatic Love

Since the supreme, most proximate, and most worthy way to the use, act, or contemplation of unitive wisdom and mystical theology is the overflowing fervor of divine love, which opens, expands, and capacitates the mind for God, desired above all things and his gifts — Denis now speaks of this holy and pure love of the Divinity.

This divine love is not a natural affection toward God, but a gratuitous, supernatural, infused love: which is the soul's supreme vigor and life, the mistress, mover, and end of all virtues and acts. It dilates the heart of the lover, expands the appetite, stretches the bosom of the mind, amplifies the dwelling of our heart, that it may contain the immense and hold within the uncircumscribed dweller and guest. The soul it elevates above all earthly, perishable, vain, and material things, causes it to rise above all created things; it flies and arrives to the Lord of majesty, unites it to him, transforms it into him, indissolubly, immediately, and intimately binds the mind it perfects, inhabits, and informs; and finally effects such a conformity between uncreated and created will, that their willing and not-willing are the same — so that the loving spirit with God is said to be one. This love of God makes friends and sons. Since it is acute and penetrating, it strives to enter to the inmost of the beloved, desires to be immediately united to God, to embrace and press him within itself; it is that fire by which vices are consumed, demons repelled, passions overcome, and the mind dissolved into God.

To what excites, kindles, and draws us to this love: first, the very goodness of God, which is connnatural to God, pure, perfect, immense, to be desired and loved, embraced, retained above all and beyond all things, infinitely and most ardently — leaving nothing unregarded by which the love of so great a goodness is in any way impeded, lessened, or taken away.

Second, the fact that the most glorious, almighty God himself first and freely loved us with an eternal love, so liberal, pure, and complete, that he is ready to give himself to us (1 John 4:10, 19). He teaches us and commands that we seek and require him himself; grants us the confidence to pray; deigns to be called Father by us; testifies that he is ready to give us whatever we worthily ask (Matt. 6:9; John 14:13-14; 15:7; 16:23-24). Whatever he requires or commands of us, he turns to our salvation.

Third, the innumerable multitude, most munificent exhibition, and incomprehensible generosity of God's benefits — from whom we have being, life, sensation, understanding, motion, body and soul, powers and members, health and strength, peace and joy, and all the other gifts of nature, goods of fortune, revenues of the Church; who has preserved us in being until now; and who so many times poured such great charismata of graces, virtues, fruits and gifts of the Holy Spirit upon us, first at the font of Baptism, then however often we have repented after sin; and who has so often and so long spared those ungrateful, impious, and most gravely sinning — and has repaid good for evil, and did not cease to do good even while our malice continued. Moreover, above all, we should be strongly kindled to love by the fact that the only-begotten Son of God, the Father eternally willing and the Holy Spirit cooperating, has truly become our brother — that he assumed, did, and suffered so much for us, and daily gives himself in the Sacrament so most liberally.

Fourth, the ineffable beatitude and immense abundance of the goods of glory that he has prepared for us with himself in the homeland — where also he has arranged to give himself as the beatific and consummative object of perfect happiness.

### Signs of Ecstatic Love

Love that is spiritual and ecstatic pertains to and prepares for mystical theology. This ecstatic love is called such from *ecstasis* — because it does not allow the loving mind to think of anything other than what it loves, nor allows the lover to be his own but belongs to the beloved, into whose advancement, service, or use it wholly transfers itself.

The first sign of carnal ecstatic love is that lovers in absence conceive much but in presence speak little and defectively — for defective speech is proper to lovers when speaking of or with the beloved. Similarly, spiritual ecstatic love is brief and abrupt in words when it addresses God in secret or speaks of him. Thus the Spouse says in the Canticle: "My beloved to me, and I to him" (Cant. 1:16); and again: "I to my beloved, and his turning is towards me" (Cant. 7:10).

The second sign of carnal ecstatic love is the desiccation of the members: from the vehement occupation about the beloved the spirits are multiplied, from which lovers incur interior desiccation and drying of the members. Similarly the soul, from vehement love of God, dries from every moisture of carnal pleasure: by which, like dry wood, it is fit to be more vehemently kindled by divine love.

The third sign is the concavity of the eyes, which follow the spirits, withdrawing and converging toward the place of the estimative power. So the eyes of the loving soul are deepened in the heart, that it may see whether there is anything in the heart that displeases God, and may weigh the hidden approach and withdrawal of God.

The fourth sign is the dryness of the eyes and lack of tears. The loving soul is saddened by nothing except by the offense of its beloved — hence it does not lament over losses of possessions or harsh things.

The fifth sign is a disordered pulse. Every lover has a pulse either too slow and rare, or too fast and frequent, according to the diverse apprehensions conceived about the beloved. The loving soul's pulses are its affections — swift when it is confident of obtaining its beloved; slow when it fears and grows faint from the consideration of its own dangers.

The sixth sign is deep thought and anxiety turned to interior things, so that the lover notices nothing of what is said unless something about the beloved is uttered. Similarly the loving soul does not notice the rumors and vain, carnal things of the world; but if there be speech about Christ, it immediately awakens, rejoices, and smiles.

## Article XVII. On Rapture and Ecstasy, what they are and how they differ

From what has been shown, the mind is elevated and led to God most specially and wholly supernaturally by mystical theology; and it is transformed and abstracted from sensibles, alienated or abstracted — which pertains to rapture and ecstasy.

Rapture is defined by some thus: rapture is an elevation from what is according to nature into what is above nature, by the force of the intellectual or superior nature. Others describe it as: rapture is an elevation of the mind, from whose prevailing actuation the lower powers are weakened or cease in their operations. Or: rapture is a strong and vehement actuation of the superior power, on account of which the inferior powers in their acts cease, are weakened, or are bound, so that they by no means impede the superior power in its operation. Thus when one is abstracted from the apprehension of sensibles, he is said to be rapt.

According to Thomas (*Summa Theologiae* II-II, q. 175, a. 1), this kind of abstraction can proceed from three causes: first, from a natural cause, as in those who from illness incur alienation of mind; second, from demons, as in those who are possessed; third, from the divine power, as Ezechiel says: "The spirit elevated me between heaven and earth, and brought me in Jerusalem in the vision of God" (Ezech. 8:3).

Thomas distinguishes rapture from ecstasy (*Summa Theologiae* II-II, q. 175, a. 2, ad 1): ecstasy imports simply the excess from oneself, by which someone is placed outside his own ordering; rapture superadds a certain violence. According to him, one who is rapt must be rapt by something exterior into something diverse from that toward which one's proper inclination tends. This diversity can be considered in two ways: first, regarding the term of the inclination, as when something heavy is hurled upward; second, regarding the mode of tending, as when a stone is thrown downward more swiftly than its nature is. Correspondingly the soul is rapt in two ways into what is beyond nature: first, regarding the term of the rapture, as when it is drawn to torments; second, regarding the mode of tending, as when it is elevated to contemplate divine things in a higher mode through the light of grace than its nature allows.

Others say conversely that ecstasy adds to rapture and is a species of rapture — that ecstasy properly pertains to the superior part of the soul, the intellectual part, when the mind is so suspended in its act and intent on divine things that the acts of the lower powers completely cease, to such an extent that sometimes the natural nutritive and motive powers cannot perform their acts. Ecstasy is thus of greater efficacy than rapture in suspending the acts of the lower powers.

Since in the theoretical act of mystical theology the mind is most vehemently occupied, affected, and impressed about divine things, it is certain that in it rapture and ecstasy find their chief place.

## Article XVIII. In which Powers of the Soul Rapture and Ecstasy are found; and the Three Modes of Ecstatic Alienation

Since the holy Dionysius in chapter four of the *Divine Names* declares that divine love causes ecstasy, it is clear that ecstasy can pertain to the appetitive power. For ecstasy is named from *ex* and *stasis* — a placing of something outside itself; and love places the lover, by an affectionate tendency, outside itself toward the beloved. Hence ecstasis also consists in the intellective power. The Gloss on the Psalm verse "I said in my excess" (Ps. 115:11) says: here ecstasis is called when the mind is taken up not by terror but by the inspiration of revelation.

Others say rapture can properly only be in the appetitive power — since rapture is beyond one's proper inclination with a certain violence. But they say rapture can have its cause in the appetitive power, since from the fact that one is vehemently affected toward something, it can happen that from the violence of the affection one is alienated from all other things. But this does not seem of much force. When the intellect or will are more powerfully occupied by the help of grace in divine things than they naturally can be, this is not properly violence, since both powers are naturally ordered toward God; hence that rapture is not against nature nor violent, although there is a great supernatural impulse there, not corruptive of the mind but perfective.

Finally, love is compared to weight: as by weight a body is drawn to its proper place to rest there, so by love the mind is rapt to God as to the place where alone it truly rests, stands, and is content. Affection more than cognition effects rapture.

The ecstatic alienation of the mind occurs in three modes. First, by the greatness of devotion: when the mind, enkindled with such heavenly desire that the inner flame of love grows beyond human measure, is as if liquefied like wax and carried upward thinned like smoke into the highest; and the higher it rises, the more subtle it becomes, until emitted into the heights it fails altogether from itself — as the one who could say: "As the hart panteth after the fountains of water, so my soul panteth after thee, O God" (Ps. 41:2); and: "I desire to be dissolved and to be with Christ" (Phil. 1:23).

Second, by the greatness of admiration, when, having considered the works of God and suspended in the highest reasons and causes, the soul is struck with such stupor that it is utterly shaken from its proper state — as the Saint exclaims: "How great are thy works, O Lord!" (Ps. 103:24). This alienation can occur from the great admiration of the divine majesty, as is written: "O Lord, our Lord, how admirable is thy name in all the earth!" (Ps. 8:2). And Job says: "Considering him, I am afraid" (Job 23:15).

Third, by the greatness of exultation, when the mind, intimately inebriated with the abundance of inner sweetness, forgets what it is or was — as wine overturns the sense of the drunkard, so this joyfulness of divine love alienates the soul so that it forgets itself and all things, and rejoices to be in him alone whom it loves. This joy arises chiefly from pure love of the Divinity, from the recollection of his benefits, and from the hope of heavenly promises.

## Article XIX. On the Contemplation, Rapture, and Ecstasy of Certain Saints

The closer the Saints were to our Lord Jesus Christ, the only-begotten of God, the more like to him they also were. Since Christ himself from the beginning of his Incarnation was the perfect contemplator of the Divinity according to the assumed nature — not only through the most excellent species of contemplation possible in this life, namely mystical theology, but through the clear, immediate, and beatific vision — he bestowed upon his chosen ones so much the more excellent an ornament of contemplation in this life as they were more dear and more chosen by him.

The most blessed Virgin Mother received before all humans the most splendid excesses of contemplation. Then the Apostles, especially John, and Paul, who testifies in his own person (2 Cor. 12:2-4) that he was rapt to the third heaven and heard secret words which it is not granted to man to speak. The holy Fathers unanimously affirm that in that rapture he contemplated God through essence, so that that contemplation exceeded the mode and nature of every contemplation possible in this life.

Mary Magdalene was most sublime a contemplative, who in the desert was daily seven times elevated in the air by holy angels, hearing with bodily ears the symphony of heavenly chants — as her true history testifies. The great Dionysius himself was preeminent in the grace of contemplation, as all his sapiential and most exalted books demonstrate. Jerome testifies of himself: he had frequently been among the angelic choirs, sensing nothing bodily for weeks; and after the vision of the divine, after many days returned to his body weeping — "What felicity I had there, what ineffable delight I sensed — the Trinity I beheld is witness, my own conscience is witness."

Among the desert fathers: Anthony spent whole nights standing with mind, face, and hands raised to heaven, contemplating and praying God, so copiously flooded in mind with the light of divine clarity and sweetness that when the sun rose in the morning, as if intoxicated with divine savors and splendors, grieved and complaining that the perception of bodily light impeded the vision of the inner light, he said: "Why, O sun, do you impede me from the contemplation of the true light?" The great Arsenius appeared to a brother like all fire. Abbott Sisoi had this custom: as soon as he extended his hands in prayer he was rapt in God mentally. Abbott Bessarion stood fourteen days and nights with hands raised praying God; and forty nights he stood among thorns, sleeping nothing. The holy father Silvanus, returning to himself from his most radiant raptures of contemplation, covered his face when going outside: "What need is there to see the light of this world, in which nothing is useful?"

The blessed Francis of Assisi: his body and soul were rapt and elevated in the air so much the more sublimely the more heartily he despised all temporal, earthly, and carnal things. Sometimes elevated to the measure of a man's height, sometimes to the tops of trees, sometimes so high that he could hardly be seen. On Mount Alverna, Christ appeared to him in the form of a winged Seraph and impressed upon him the holy stigmata of his wounds. When he and his brothers sat with Saint Clare in church to eat, the whole church appeared to be burning and covered with fire; the terrified townspeople ran in, but entering found all those blessed ones abstracted from all senses and rapt. O how they burned within, whose mental fervor of pure love God so wondrously declared outwardly!

Thomas Aquinas: the frequent elevation of his body in the air, a star entering and standing over his head, and his customary rapture — not only in choir and cell but sometimes at table — demonstrate how great a summit of most profound contemplation the almighty and most merciful God poured into him.

The devout virgin Elisabeth of Spalbeec, a compatriot of Denis from near Rickel, was daily seven times rapt to God, always also upon receiving holy communion — so much so that she was deprived of all use of her senses. The most holy virgin Catherine of Siena was always upon receiving communion, and often otherwise, so eminently and forcefully rapt in mind to God that if her hands and feet had been cut off at the time she would not have felt it, but returning to herself would have experienced most grievous pains — which was indeed experienced around her on one occasion. The devout John Ruysbroeck: when sometimes found in the wood under a tree, the tree itself was seen to be burning — which manifested the splendor and ardor of his contemplation.

These things, few out of many — indeed out of innumerable — were inserted so that we may be enkindled to the love of contemplation and conceive confidence of advancing in it: for these Saints just mentioned were mortals like us, and yet through the grace of God did not attain such perfection without great labor.

## Article XX. A Disputation for the Catholic Faith, from the Experience of Mystical Theology, against Porphyry, Averroes, and the Other Scorners of Christian Wisdom

The divine Dionysius writes (*Ecclesiastical Hierarchy*, ch. 7) that especially the Greek philosophers, hearing the wisdom of the Christian law preached, laughed widely and derided it — among whom the most notable were Porphyry the Platonist and Averroes the Peripatetic.

Denis summarizes Porphyry's position from Augustine's nineteenth book of *The City of God*, chapter twenty-three: from the oracle of Apollo, Porphyry concluded that Christians are irremediably reprobate and that the Jews receive the supreme God more than Christians. He also wrote that the gods pronounced Christ most pious but Christians polluted. Augustine shows that these words of Porphyry's are self-contradictory: if Christ was most pious, he was not the deceiver of others, and his doctrine — which prohibits the worship of the gods — is not false.

Averroes, furthermore, disparaged every law other than the natural law — namely the laws of Moses, Christ, and Mohammed.

Denis responds from experience: from what appeared sensibly around the most divine Christians — of whom in the preceding article — it is shown by experience that the Christian law is from the true God, holy, and salvific. Such alienation, rapture, and ecstasy cannot be from a natural cause. Those who experience such raptures and ecstasies, illuminations, ferments, delights, elevations, and most admirable multiform changes within themselves are so confirmed in faith by these that their faith is more certain and clear than all natural science or even all sensation — so that even if all the world contradicted them, they would not doubt. If the wicked do not believe such things have occurred to Christians, they oppose themselves to experience — since even to the present day these things occur to certain most devout persons, of whose most profound humility, supreme patience, deific charity, most sincere truth, and singular innocence there is no doubt; and to whose holiness and happiness Almighty God bears witness through diverse miracles.

## Article XXI. On Prayer as it Pertains to the Theoretic Illuminations of Mystical Wisdom

Prayer and contemplation are the two most agile wings of the mind, by which the fervent and humble soul penetrates the heavens and is led to the throne of majesty. Since one ascends gradually, and contemplation itself is more sublime than prayer simply speaking, one must ascend to the apex of contemplation through most devout prayer; and the more the interior locution with God and contemplative vision of mystical theology transcend the natural faculty of the soul and consist on a higher level, the humbler and more fervent the prayer necessary for its attainment.

Since mystical theology belongs only to those who are perfect in divine love, whose soul is purified — those who, with God's help, have become such or worthily desire to become such, in their prayers must not turn back to themselves, but by the amorous affection of the purest charity turn directly and lineally toward God, to be fixed in and proceed toward him — asking for or seeking to obtain nothing by prayer except the Lord God himself, and whatever must be from his will in us; so as not even to seek his gifts, consolation, illumination, or enkindling except insofar as they are necessary to us for having and possessing him. Let us most affectionately beseech him that he apply himself, unite himself, and pour himself into us; that he bind us to himself, illuminate, enkindle; that he incessantly inhabit our mind, always possess it wholly, fill and govern it — that we may so honor him daily more perfectly, contemplate him more limpidly, love him more fervently, embrace him more strictly and intimately, and thus adhere to him ever more purely and stably, and so tend to mystical wisdom.

Before prayer let us prepare the soul, casting aside its dispersions. To do this worthily, we must consider both the properties of God whom we ask and our own. If we weigh God's goodness, charity, majesty, justice, and clemency, and also our own depravity, ingratitude, smallness, calamity, and poverty, we shall pray with love's fervor, intimate reverence, great trust, chaste fear, filial confidence — reverently, from weighing God's majesty and our smallness; fervently, contemplating his goodness and charity and our poverty; with fear, gazing on his justice and our perversity; with trust, gazing on his immense clemency and our misery.

A person who has so prayed will sometimes be led to that perfection of prayer in which, by excessive fixation, intuition, and transformation of the mind into God, one does not attend to one's own act and forgets oneself. As Anthony teaches and Cassianus writes: we recognize that prayer is not perfect unless in it the devout person forgets himself.

The habit of praying intently — not superficially and with noise — is most useful; and the more the fervor of love and the clarity of the gift of wisdom abound in the soul, the readier, more efficacious, more frequent, and more joyful the prayer will be. In every place, time, act, cause, and occupation — even at table and in bed — let us accustom ourselves, care, and strive to fix the mind in God by holy, fervent, and humble love, to impress it by interior contemplation and praise, to embrace him by actual conjunction as much as possible and to hold him inseparably — saying with the Spouse: "I held him, and I will not let him go" (Cant. 3:4).

## Article XXII. On Devotion and its Grades, especially on the Devotion pertaining to Unitive Wisdom

Devotion is most properly and formally defined as the readiness or alacrity of the will toward those things that are of God. Hence the subject of devotion is the will — the superior and intellective appetite. The virtue whose immediate and elicited act is devotion is called latria, by which worship is rendered to God. Charity is the cause of devotion — not only imperatively, in the way it is generally the cause of all virtuous acts, but also in a closer and special mode, because from the affection and fervor of charity itself the promptness of the soul toward divine things flows. Contemplation is also a cause of devotion, as the way toward it and its director.

There are three grades of devotion, corresponding to the three ways. The first belongs to beginners, the second to those progressing, the third to the perfect.

Devotion is called experiential or sensible when the readiness of will is so amorous and delicious that it perceptibly and joyfully overflows into the lower part of the soul, the sensitive; rational or intellectual, when it remains in the superior appetite without such overflow.

A tripartite division of devotion distinguishes desirous, voluptuous, and furious devotion. Desirous devotion is when the superior appetite alone, or together with the inferior, is carried toward the things of God with great avidity. Voluptuous devotion is when this is done with an overflowing and intimate sweetness in both appetites. Furious devotion (*furiosa*) is when the aforesaid appetites, especially the superior, are carried toward the veneration of God with a zeal so impetuous, importunate, impatient, and serious that they seem to have something of the properties of those who are beside themselves — which befits those who, transcending all God's gifts, desire God himself with the most pure and chaste love, and greatly affect his honor, loving, seeking, and desiring God in himself and for himself: which is properly the way of the sons of God.

The definition by which some describe devotion as the tenderness of heart by which one easily dissolves in tears belongs principally to the second grade of devotion. Another definition — that devotion is the fervor of a good will which the mind, unable to hold back, manifests by certain signs — is held to belong to the third grade. This furious devotion, which because of the inflammation of the affection is called furious, belongs to the professors of mystical theology: who, when they have spent all they are capable of in love and veneration of God, think they have done little or nothing; humbly grieving that they can do no more; and ascribing wholly to the grace of God even what they do.

## Article XXIII. That Mystical Theology belongs to the Hidden Sons of God; and the Distinction between Hirelings, Faithful Servants, Secret Friends, and Hidden Sons of God

The distinction of the threefold way — purgative, illuminative, perfective — is recalled (cf. Book I, art. 19-20). To the purgative way belongs the state of beginners, called the animal state; to the illuminative, the state of those advancing, called the rational; to the perfective, the state of the perfect, called the spiritual. Furthermore: to the purgative way belong faithful servants; to the illuminative, secret friends; to the perfective, hidden sons of God. Again: to the purgative belongs ethics; to the illuminative, theory; to the perfective, divine life. And: to the purgative belongs political or moral virtue; to the illuminative, purgatory intellectual virtue; to the perfective, virtue which theologians call divine, Platonists call virtue of the purified soul, Peripatetics call heroic virtue.

There is therefore a great distinction between hirelings, faithful servants, secret friends, and hidden sons. Hirelings are those who, in all they render God, turn back inordinately wholly to themselves — intending their own advantage, profit, or reward more than the honor and glory of God; indeed loving themselves more than God. These do not seek or love God for himself and for his proper goodness, but for themselves — to escape eternal punishment and obtain heavenly joys. They avoidingly divide themselves from God, nor dare to trust in him.

Faithful servants are those who with infused charity and sincere intention observe the divine commandments, diligently avoiding mortal sins, but not yet become interior and contemplative, nor having wholly renounced themselves through the profession and custody of the evangelical counsels.

Secret friends are those who cleave to God by amorous and intimate adhesion, extending themselves to spiritual taste and interior exercise. But they have not yet become naked, nor are they wholly dead to themselves, nor have they cast away every propriety — since they look with great affection upon gifts: namely interior consolation, spiritual enrichment, and mental delights.

Hidden sons of God die to all propriety and the gifts mentioned, nor in any way turn back to their own advantages, even spiritual ones; but carried by the most ardent affection of charity they tend purely and wholly toward God — for the pure, immense, and essential goodness of God — attending only to God himself, not to his gifts; and when they desire to adhere to him intimately, to press and embrace him within themselves and to enjoy him, they look only to God, since with every private love utterly plucked out, they blaze with the purest love of the Divinity. Their most purified minds are capable of mystical theology, and are worthy to enjoy God. For this enjoyment six things are required: true peace, interior silence, amorous adhesion, repose in the beloved, the slumber of the mind in him, and a certain intuition of the darkness — which essentially constitutes mystical theology.

Let us always aspire with our whole hearts to the perfection of these hidden sons of God, since to those who love God in this way, the most excellent charismata of graces are bestowed.

## Article XXIV. Whether the Contemplation of the Present Life can ascend to the Vision of God in his Essence

Although according to all that has been proved, the theoretic intuition, contemplative inspection, and interior locution with God of unitive and mystical wisdom is a very excellent, supernatural, and most perfect operation of the mind, it nonetheless stands at an almost incomparable distance from the immediate and clear vision of the divine essence. Since therefore for mystic visions so great an abstraction from sensibles and intelligibles, from sense and intellect and their acts, is required — as has been shown — it is clear that for the vision of the Deity through essence, a much more sublime abstraction of mind is required. Therefore no one living in this age, actually living the bodily and sensitive or human life, can attain to the contemplation of the divine essence in itself as it is. Hence Moses the lawgiver of the Hebrews, and Paul the teacher of the Gentiles, when in this life they saw God through essence, were entirely abstracted from every actual life of flesh and senses.

Moreover, as has been shown, in the contemplation of God by negation according to mystical theology, there sometimes occurs such a great alienation of the mind from all senses and all created things that one uses no bodily sense but falls into full ecstasy through the most vehement fixation, immersion, and absorption of the mind in God. Since therefore in the contemplation of God through essence the fixation and immersion of the mind in God is almost incomparably greater than in the mystical intuition just described, it is most certain that this contemplation is impossible in this life during the actual exercise of human and sensitive life.

Furthermore, as has often been shown elsewhere, God cannot be seen through essence unless the divine essence is united to the mind as an intelligible form. But such conjunction wholly transcends the condition of the present life (as regards its act). Hence, according to the divine Dionysius, in this life we are most perfectly united to God through negation, as to the incomprehensible and unknown. And to Moses the Lord says: "Man shall not see me and live" (Exod. 33:20). And in John: "No man hath seen God at any time" (John 1:18). And Paul: "Whom no man hath seen, nor can see" (1 Tim. 6:16) — in the present state, in the manner now said. Hence Dionysius in the letter to Caius, Gregory over Ezechiel, and Damascene in the first book concordantly affirm that if anyone in this life saw God intelligently, he contemplated not him in himself and in his proper clarity but something of those things that are his and under him.

However: it is not evident from canonical Scripture that any pure human person saw God through essence in the present life, besides Moses and Paul; nor is it certain whether it was granted only to these. Nor should it be thought that what is written as granted to servants was denied to the Virgin Mother of God. Some of the Saints seem to speak and think as though sometimes, in an ineffable, swift manner, certain most amorous souls are given even in this life a certain point of such contemplation. On this matter Denis, seeing dimly, does not presume to speak further. He adds only: "Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God" (Matt. 5:8).

## Article XXV. On the Divine, Deiform, Deifying, Deificatory, and Deified Life

The perfection of the created mind is to be ordered-ly assimilated to the uncreated mind; and the more perfectly it is assimilated to the latter, the more divine it also becomes. Since therefore the holy and glorious God is essentially wisdom, truth, charity, virtue, and holiness, piety, and justice, the human mind is deified the more sublimely the more it is adorned, filled, and occupied with more splendid wisdom, more sincere truth, more ardent charity, greater virtue, purer holiness, more intimate piety, and more excellent justice.

Most especially, through acts of wisdom and charity, the soul is conformed to God supernaturally. For since the life of the highest and unchangeable God in himself is nothing other than his eternal self-love, his immortal and unchanging contemplation — it is clear that the created mind is made so much the more deiform as, in the contemplation and love of the living, superexcelling, adorable God, it is more illumined, more ardent, and more stable. And in this brief word stands all our perfection. One who is more perfect in this is, speaking absolutely, a better person — whether religious or not. Therefore let us order all the exercises of virtues, all spiritual works, all that we do, suffer, all that we are or can do, always toward the pure contemplation and fervent love of God — of which the one cannot be had without the other — and toward stabilization in both. For if a cloistered person has not acted thus, and has not truly advanced brilliantly, ardently, and stably in the contemplation and love of almighty and blessed God, let him know that he has left the world without fruit, and is altogether unwise, sterile, and negligent.

Moreover, the eternal life of the infinite God is the internal utterance of the uncreated Word — which is called generation — and also the spiration of immense and uncreated proceeding Love, and the mutually, thoroughly, immensely intensive complacency of the three super-holy and super-honorable and uncircumscribed Persons, and also their mutually infinitely sweet and superlatively sweet fruition. Hence the heartfelt complacency by which God takes delight in the human mind, and the fruition by which it enjoys the most exalted God, greatly deify it and make it most dear and embraceable to God — to such an extent that it is found happily embraced by the most superdelicious, superessential, and supersimple Trinity in its mutual embrace.

Thus to love God above all, to adhere, to be united, conformed, fixed, and immersed in him — is to be deiformly simplified, most excellently sanctified, supernaturally stabilized, transferred into the most sincere affection of goodness, elevated above oneself, to fail oneself and flow from oneself, to be immersed in God, to expire and die in him, to be absorbed in him, to become one or the same with him, to have and to live the life that deifies as it lives, and the deified life through most gracious imitation.

All this is to be understood not through the consumption, cessation, or translation and permutation of created being into divine and ideal being, but through the amelioration of one's mode of being, through the improvement of quality, through excellent and preeminent imitation. For each thing is most conformed to that whose operations it most follows — as we call voracious those who are like wolves. And an intellectual thing is said most to become one with that to which it is bound by fuller charity. For charity transforms the lover into the beloved and makes the wills of both conform in a certain conformity of willing and not-willing. Hence a friend is called another self. And when anyone ceases to attend to his own connnatural and proper operations and insists more on the operations of a higher or at least different nature, he is said to fail himself, to turn into another, and no longer to remain what he was — as the Apostle says: "I live, now not I" (Gal. 2:20).

In this manner of speaking, John Ruysbroeck often says we become one with God, one life, one happiness — such that certain ones, attending more to the surface of his words than to the intention of his mind, write that he wished to renew the error of those who said that the rational creature, through its elevation to God, returns and turns into ideal and uncreated being — which was the most crude and most foolish error. Nor should it be thought that Ruysbroeck ever intended to affirm or renew this: indeed he writes most strenuously against the said error. But he does have a manner of speaking frequently quite extended, as do certain other Saints who speak from the excess of their devotion and the fervor of their charity.

Finally, since God naturally and superessentially and infinitely good distributes many goods by most liberal piety to all — both good and evil — and is always most ready to have mercy, to embrace the penitent, and to save the converted, and since with him there is no acceptance of persons but as the just one, he loves justice (cf. Ps. 10:8): it is clear that the created mind is eminently assimilated to God through true, interior, and generous piety, and stable and sincere justice — when it imparts to all, as far as its powers allow, the affection and effect of piety from true charity; and everywhere according to the censure of right reason holds inviolably the zeal for justice, with every private favor, human fear, carnal love, and hatred utterly uprooted; always having God before its eyes, and saying with holy Job: "Justice I put on as a garment, and I clothed myself with my judgment as with a robe and a diadem" (Job 29:14), and: "My justification, which I have begun to hold, I will not forsake; for my heart doth not reproach me in all my life" (Job 27:6) — choosing to die rather than to be turned away from the way of truth and equity.

*To the praise, honor, and glory of him from whom all virtue, piety, and justice has flowed — our Creator and Savior, who is above all, God blessed for ever. Amen.*
