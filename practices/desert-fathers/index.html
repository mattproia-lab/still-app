Egypt · Fourth century · The beginning of it all

Who were the Desert Fathers?

They went into the Egyptian desert to be alone with God, and accidentally invented every form of Christian monasticism that followed.

The beginning.

Around the year 270, a young Egyptian named Antony heard the Gospel read in church — go, sell what you have, and give to the poor — took it as addressed to him personally, disposed of his inheritance, and walked into the desert. He stayed for most of the next eighty years.

He was not the first Christian ascetic, but he became the famous one. Athanasius wrote his life shortly after his death and the book travelled across the empire. Within a generation the deserts of Egypt held thousands of men and women living in cells, singly or in loose colonies, at Nitria, Scetis, and Kellia.

The timing is not accidental. Constantine legalised Christianity in 313. Martyrdom, which had been the highest form of Christian witness, largely ended — and the desert became the place where people went to give their whole lives to God in a Church that was suddenly safe and respectable. The monastic life has been described ever since as a white martyrdom.

Some names.

Who they were

Antony the Great (c. 251–356) — the archetype. Athanasius's Life is the founding text of the genre.

Pachomius (c. 292–348) — organised the first communities under a common rule, making him the father of cenobitic monasticism as distinct from the hermits.

Macarius the Great — of Scetis, one of the most quoted in the sayings, known for a refusal to condemn anyone.

Poemen — the single most represented voice in the collected sayings, and consistently the gentlest.

Moses the Ethiopian — a former bandit who became one of the most revered abbas, and the source of the most famous line in the whole literature: go, sit in your cell, and your cell will teach you everything.

Arsenius — a Roman aristocrat and imperial tutor who fled the court, and whose sayings are dominated by silence.

Evagrius Ponticus (345–399) — the intellectual of the desert, who systematised the eight thoughts that assail a monk. That list, transmitted west by Cassian, becomes the seven deadly sins.

And the mothers

Popular collections routinely drop them, which distorts the picture. Amma Syncletica of Alexandria left a substantial body of teaching, much of it on the beginnings of the ascetic life. Amma Sarah lived sixty years beside a river without once looking at it, and told a group of monks that she was a woman in body but not in mind. Amma Theodora taught on discernment, and on the necessity of humility over asceticism.

What they taught.

Five ideas worth knowing

The cell

Not a room so much as a discipline. Stay put; do not go looking for a better place to become holy. The cell teaches by refusing to entertain you — everything you have been avoiding surfaces in it, which is precisely the point.

Hesychia

Stillness, or quiet — both exterior and interior. It is the aim of the whole enterprise, and it is what the Jesus Prayer tradition was later built to serve.

Logismoi

The thoughts. Not sins but the intrusive suggestions that precede them. The desert method is to notice a thought early, name it, and decline to entertain it — well before it becomes a decision. Anyone who has tried contemplative sitting will recognise the mechanics immediately.

Penthos

Compunction — a settled sorrow over one's own condition that the Fathers considered a gift rather than a burden, and quite distinct from self-loathing. It is warm rather than cold, and it produces tenderness toward others rather than contempt.

Discretion

The virtue they rate above all the spectacular ones. The desert literature is full of stories mocking excessive fasting and heroic sleeplessness. Antony compares the monk to a bow: string it too tight and it snaps. Moderation, from people living in caves, carries a certain authority.

Why everything after
comes from here.

The line of transmission

Around 400, a young monk named John Cassian spent years among the Egyptian monks, then travelled west and founded monasteries near Marseille. He wrote down what he had learned in the Institutes and the Conferences.

Benedict, writing his Rule in Italy a century later, instructs his monks to read Cassian. Through Benedict the desert reaches every Western monastery — Cluny, Cîteaux, the Trappists, the Benedictines of today. Through the Greek and Syriac lines it reaches Sinai, Athos, the Philokalia, and Russian hesychasm.

Practically everything in Christian contemplative practice — Lectio Divina, the Liturgy of the Hours, the Jesus Prayer, the examination of conscience, the rule of life — traces back through that chain to a few thousand people living in huts in Egypt.

This part is the practice.

How to read them

The sayings — the Apophthegmata Patrum — are a strange genre, and most people read them wrongly and get little out of them.

They are terse, often only two or three lines. They are frequently oblique to the point of seeming evasive. Many have a sting. They are not aphorisms designed to be agreed with, and reading fifty in a sitting produces a pleasant impression and no lasting effect whatsoever.

The native form is a request. A brother walks to an elder's cell and says: Abba, give me a word. He receives one sentence. He goes away and lives with it — sometimes for years.

That is how they are meant to be taken. One at a time. Without commentary, at first. Carried rather than understood.

Go, sit in your cell, and your cell will teach you everything. Abba Moses

Notice that the saying does not explain itself, does not flatter, and gives you nothing to do except the one thing you were probably trying to avoid. That is characteristic. If a saying makes you slightly uncomfortable and you cannot immediately dispose of it, that is the one to keep.

Where to start reading

Several major modern translations are still in copyright and worth buying. If you would rather read freely, the older public-domain editions are substantial: Cassian's Institutes and Conferences in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers series, Wallis Budge's Paradise of the Holy Fathers (1907), Palladius's Lausiac History, and Athanasius's Life of Antony. All are available online at no cost.

Still is built on this tradition rather than adapted from somewhere else — the cell, the thoughts, hesychia, the word given and carried.
