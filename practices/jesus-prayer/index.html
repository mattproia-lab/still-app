The Jesus Prayer

Twelve words that the Christian East has prayed without ceasing for more than a thousand years — and the cautions that have always travelled with them.

The words.

Lord Jesus Christ,
Son of God,
have mercy on me, a sinner.

That is the whole prayer. Shorter forms are used and are entirely legitimate — Lord Jesus Christ, have mercy on me, or in the simplest reduction just Jesus, mercy. Long practitioners often shorten it over the years rather than lengthening it.

Its content is two things fused: the confession of who Christ is, and the cry of the tax collector in Luke 18 and the blind man on the Jericho road. It is a creed and a plea in one breath.

Where it comes from.

A short history

The practice grows out of the Egyptian desert of the fourth and fifth centuries, where monks used short repeated phrases of Scripture — what Cassian calls a single verse held constantly in the mind — to keep attention fixed through the day's work.

By the sixth and seventh centuries the invocation of the name of Jesus has taken a settled form in Sinai and Palestine. Through the Byzantine period it becomes the central discipline of hesychasm, from hesychia: stillness, quiet, the state of inner rest in which God can be attended to. St. Gregory Palamas defended the hesychasts theologically in the fourteenth century.

Two texts carried it to the modern world. The Philokalia, compiled on Mount Athos in the eighteenth century, gathers the primary sources. The Way of a Pilgrim, a nineteenth-century Russian account of a wanderer learning to pray without ceasing, put it in the hands of ordinary readers — including, eventually, a great many Western Christians who had never heard of it.

The goal is acquisition of the Holy Spirit through stillness. St. Seraphim of Sarov

How to begin

The mechanics are almost embarrassingly simple, and the simplicity is not a beginner's version of something more complicated.

Position

Sit upright, somewhere quiet, in low light. The classical instruction has the head slightly inclined and the eyes closed or lowered. You are not trying to achieve a posture; you are trying to stop fidgeting.

The words

Say the prayer slowly, either aloud, under your breath, or interiorly. Slowly enough that you mean it rather than recite it. Speed is the enemy here — a fast Jesus Prayer becomes machinery within about two minutes.

The breath

Many practitioners let the first half fall on the in-breath and the second half on the out-breath. This is a help, not a requirement, and it should never involve holding, forcing, or lengthening the breath. Several of the older texts describe more elaborate breathing techniques; the standard modern counsel — including from Orthodox writers within the tradition — is to leave those alone unless you are under direction.

When you drift

You will drift constantly. Return to the words. The returning is the practice; there is no version of this where the mind stays put.

How long

Ten minutes a day, at a fixed time. Resist extending it for the first several months. A prayer rope — a knotted wool cord, usually 33, 50 or 100 knots — is useful if you want a way to keep count without watching a clock, though counting can quietly become the object of attention, so hold it loosely.

The question everyone asks.

Is this a mantra?

The mechanics look similar and the difference is not a technicality.

A mantra, in the traditions that use the term, is generally a sound or formula whose repetition works by its own operation. The Jesus Prayer is an address. You are speaking to a Person, by name, and asking him for something specific. The words are not a device that produces a state; they are what you are saying to someone who is listening.

This matters practically as well as doctrinally. If the prayer is an address, then a session in which you felt nothing but kept speaking to Christ was a good session. If it is a device, a session that produced no state was a failure. The first understanding will keep you praying for decades. The second will not survive the first dry month.

What the tradition warns about

Almost no other Christian practice comes with this density of caution attached, and the cautions come from inside the tradition rather than from its critics.

Do not chase experiences

The literature describes warmth, light, tears, and states of deep stillness. It also warns, insistently, against seeking them. The Greek term is plani, in Russian prelest — spiritual delusion, the state of a person who has mistaken their own imagination or vanity for God, and who is now unreachable because the mistake feels holy. The classical safeguard is straightforward: want mercy, not experiences.

Do not go deep alone

The tradition assumes a spiritual father or mother. Very few people reading this will have one, and that is a real gap rather than an optional extra. The practical compromise most modern guides settle on: pray the prayer, keep the sessions short and ordinary, and find a confessor, spiritual director, or priest before you attempt anything beyond that. If the practice begins producing intense states, that is the moment to talk to someone, not the moment to press further.

Do not use it to escape

Stillness can be a way of not dealing with things. If you find the prayer functioning as an anaesthetic — a way of avoiding a relationship, a decision, or a grief you are not facing — the fathers would say the prayer has been recruited into the thing it was meant to heal.

If you are carrying something heavy right now, this practice is not a substitute for a person. Speak to a priest, a director, or someone who loves you. And if you are in danger of harming yourself, please reach out now — in the US you can call or text 988, at any hour.

Where it goes.

From the lips to the heart

The tradition describes a movement over years: the prayer is first on the lips, then in the mind, then — the phrase used everywhere in the literature — it descends into the heart, and eventually continues on its own beneath conscious attention.

This is described as a gift rather than an achievement, and it is worth knowing about mainly so that you do not attempt to manufacture it. What is actually asked of you is ten minutes a day of saying twelve words slowly to Christ. That is the entire assignment, and it stays the entire assignment for a very long time.
