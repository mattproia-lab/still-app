Monastery guesthouses · Written from the guest side

What actually happens on a silent retreat

Nobody instructs you. Nobody checks on you. For most first-time retreatants that is the difficult part, and it is also the point.

Begin here.

A silent monastic retreat is not a workshop. There is usually no programme, no facilitator, and no group sharing. You are given a room in a guesthouse attached to a working monastery, the times of the Office, the times of meals, and then you are left alone — for two days, or three, or a week.

Almost everyone finds the first twenty-four hours harder than expected, and it is worth knowing in advance that this is universal rather than a sign you have made a mistake.

The shape of a day.

The rhythm

The monastery's own horarium runs whether guests attend or not. You are welcome at the Office, and attending it is what gives the day its structure — without it, the hours can become shapeless very quickly.

A typical Trappist or Benedictine day looks roughly like this. Times vary by house, sometimes considerably.

TimeWhat
3:15 – 4:00amVigils. Optional for guests, and the one most people are glad they went to at least once.
6:00 – 7:00amLauds, often followed by Mass.
MorningBreakfast, usually taken in silence. Then unstructured hours.
MiddayA little hour. Dinner — the main meal in many houses.
AfternoonUnstructured. Walking, reading, sleeping.
5:00 – 6:00pmVespers. Supper, often light.
7:30 – 8:00pmCompline, then the Grand Silence until morning.

Between those fixed points there is nothing scheduled at all. That is roughly eight to ten waking hours a day with no task in them.

The honest part.

What the first day is like

Restless. Frequently unpleasant.

What surfaces when the input stops is not usually peace. It is the backlog — the conversation you have been avoiding, the grief you have been outrunning, the low-grade anxiety that the noise had been covering. The monastic tradition has a name for the particular flavour of this: acedia, the noonday demon, the restlessness that convinces you the day is wasted and you should leave.

The counsel from the Desert Fathers on this has been the same for sixteen centuries: stay in the cell. Do not fill the time. Do not go home early. It passes, usually somewhere in the second day, and what is on the other side is what you came for.

Practical version: bring less to read than you think you need. The most common first-retreat mistake is arriving with four books and using them to avoid the silence entirely.

Cost, booking, rules.

The practical questions

What it costs

Most monastic guesthouses in the United States operate on a suggested donation or free-will offering rather than a fixed rate, and no one will turn you away for giving less. Check the individual monastery's site — some now publish a suggested amount per night. Give what you can; guesthouses are frequently a significant part of how these communities survive.

Booking

Book well ahead. Popular houses run months out, and Lent and Advent are the tightest. Most take requests through a form on their website or by phone with a guestmaster.

What to bring

  • Layers, and shoes you can walk in for a long time.
  • A Bible, and at most one other book.
  • A notebook. Many people write more on retreat than they have in years.
  • An alarm clock, if you intend to leave the phone off — and leaving it off is the single decision that most changes the retreat.

The rules

Silence in the guesthouse and church, always. Silence at meals in most houses. You are not required to attend anything, including Mass. Enclosure — the monastery proper — is not open to guests. If you want to talk to a monk, ask the guestmaster; many houses will arrange it, and many monks are glad to.

Do you have to be Catholic?

Generally no. Most Trappist and Benedictine guesthouses receive people of any faith or none, on the same terms. Communion is the exception, and the usual norms apply.

Three worth the drive.

Where to go

Abbey of Gethsemani — Trappist, Kentucky

Thomas Merton's monastery, and one of the most storied houses in America. Cistercian silence, rolling Kentucky knobs, a guesthouse that has been receiving retreatants for generations. If you have read Merton and want to see the place, this is the place. monks.org

Mepkin Abbey — Moncks Corner, South Carolina

A Trappist community on the Cooper River, set in former plantation gardens under live oaks and Spanish moss. The setting is extraordinary and the quiet is deep. mepkinabbey.org

Monastery of Christ in the Desert — Abiquiú, New Mexico

Benedictine, at the end of a long dirt road in a remote canyon in northern New Mexico. Among the most beautiful retreat settings in the country, and among the most genuinely cut off. christdesert.org

Verify current guesthouse policies and availability directly with each monastery — schedules, offerings, and openings change.

Afterwards.

Coming home

The re-entry is sharper than people expect. A grocery store on the drive home can be genuinely disorienting after three days of silence, and the clarity you leave with tends to dissolve within about a week unless something holds it.

What holds it, in practice, is a small daily practice you keep once you are back — one hour of the Office, or fifteen minutes of Lectio, or the Examen at night. Not an attempt to reproduce the retreat. Something small enough to survive an ordinary Tuesday.

That is what a rule of life is for, and it is worth writing yours on the drive home while the retreat is still legible.
