How to pray Lectio Divina

Four movements: reading, meditation, prayer, contemplation. It is not a Bible study. It is a way of letting a text read you.

Read this once.
Then close the tab and begin.

Lectio Divina is the oldest continuous method of Christian prayer still in daily use. Monks in the Egyptian desert were practising it in the fourth century. Benedict wrote it into his Rule in the sixth. In the twelfth, a Carthusian prior named Guigo II gave it the shape most people still learn — four rungs of a ladder, he called it, set on earth with its top in the clouds.

What follows is that ladder. You do not need a commentary, a study guide, or any prior training. You need a short passage of Scripture, somewhere to sit, and about twenty minutes.

Before the text is opened.

Settle first

Almost everyone skips this, and skipping it is why most attempts at Lectio feel like reading with extra steps.

Even before we open the text we allow ourselves to come under the influence of what we are about to read. Michael Casey, O.C.S.O.

Sit down. Let the noise of the day settle for two or three minutes before you read a single word. You are not reading to finish something or to learn something. You are reading to make contact with God, and contact requires that you stop moving first.

If it helps, pray one line before you begin — Psalm 119:18 has been used for this for centuries: Open my eyes, that I may behold wondrous things out of your law.

Short is better.
Ten verses, not ten chapters.

Choosing a passage

The single most common mistake is choosing too much. Lectio works on a few verses, not a chapter. You are not trying to cover ground.

Good places to start:

  • The Gospels. Casey's advice is blunt — you can strike gold very quickly in them. A single healing, a single conversation, a single parable.
  • The Psalms. The prayer book of the Church, and the text the monks knew by heart. Psalm 27, 42, 63, 130, 139.
  • The day's Mass readings. If you want the Church to choose for you, this is the simplest option and it keeps you in step with everyone else praying that day.

Use a translation you find beautiful rather than one you find precise. You are going to be repeating these words to yourself.

The first movement.

Read aloud, or half-aloud.
Then read it again.

Lectio — the reading

Read the passage slowly. Slower than feels natural. The monastic practice was to murmur the words — reading with the lips and the ear, not only the eye — which is far harder to hurry through.

Then read it a second time. This is not optional. The first reading is almost always consumed by comprehension: your mind is working out what the passage says. Only on the second or third pass does the text start to become prayer.

The word of God is so precious that we do not want to miss the slightest nuance it might contain. Michael Casey, O.C.S.O.

You are waiting for something to catch. A word, an image, a half-line. It may be the most ordinary phrase in the passage. It is not your job to make this happen, and it will not happen every time.

The second movement.

Do not analyse it.
Repeat it.

Meditatio — the word that found you

When something catches, stop reading. Stay with that word or phrase and repeat it slowly, letting it settle.

Casey compares this to palpation — the way a physician presses along the abdomen, moving on where there is no response, stopping where you flinch. If you yelp, there is something there.

Since the time of Origen the tradition has distinguished three levels at which a word can sound. You do not have to work through all three; usually one of them is where the response is.

The echo in the mind

What does this word open about God, about Christ, about the content of the faith? Not a doctrine you already hold — something the text is showing you.

The echo in the conscience

Where does this word call you to turn, to change, to follow? This is often the uncomfortable one, and often the real one.

The echo in the spirit

What desire toward God does this stir? What praise, what longing, what grief?

These are not outcomes you manufacture. They follow the workings of grace, and some days there is nothing.

The third movement.

Second person, not third.

Oratio — the prayer

Now answer. Whatever the word has opened in you — gratitude, resistance, a question, an ache — say it to God directly.

The rule that makes this movement work is small and absolute: write or speak to God, not about him. "Lord, I do not want to forgive this person" is prayer. "I've been reflecting on the theme of forgiveness" is not. The first is addressed to someone. The second is a diary entry.

Prayer is the devoted turning of the heart to God for the removal of evil and the acquisition of good. Guigo II, The Ladder of Monks

This movement can be a sentence or ten minutes. It ends when you have said what is actually there.

The fourth movement.

Nothing is required here.

Contemplatio — resting

Stop speaking. Remain.

This is the movement people find hardest, because it has no task in it and produces nothing you can point to afterwards. Guigo described it as the mind suspended in God, tasting the joys of eternal sweetness — but that description has sent a lot of people looking for a feeling, and looking for a feeling is the fastest way to lose the movement.

Five minutes is enough. If your mind wanders, return quietly to the word that found you earlier. Wandering is not failure; returning is the practice.

On the days it does not work.

When nothing happens

It will happen often — more often than not, at the beginning. Nothing catches, nothing echoes, and you finish the twenty minutes with the distinct sense of having wasted them.

This is normal, it is documented across the whole tradition, and it is not a sign you are doing it wrong. The Desert Fathers took the view that showing up was the entire obligation; what happened during the sitting was God's business rather than yours.

Two practical notes. First, do not extend the time to force something — end when you planned to end. Second, do not change methods. Almost everyone who abandons Lectio does so during a dry stretch, in search of a technique that works better, and there is no such technique.

Do not be surprised if you fall every day; do not give up, but stand your ground courageously. St. John Climacus

How long, how often.

Building the habit

Twenty minutes, once a day, is the standard shape. If that is not available, ten minutes daily beats forty minutes on Sunday — this is a practice that works by accumulation, and a long gap costs more than a short session gains.

Most people find morning easier than evening, because Lectio asks for a mind that is tired-but-not-exhausted. Some find the opposite. The hour matters less than the sameness of the hour.

Keep a note of the word that found you each day. Over months this record becomes surprisingly legible — you will see the same word returning across seasons, which is information about your interior life that is very difficult to get any other way.

Still guides Lectio Divina through all four movements — settling, the double reading, the three echoes, and a timed rest — and keeps the word that found you in a record you can read back over months.
